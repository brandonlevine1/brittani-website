## Louisiana Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of Louisiana attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up agents who relocated from common-law states or who simply never dug into the Civil Code. Louisiana operates under a legal framework with no parallel elsewhere in the United States, and getting this wrong does not just delay your deal — it can void the transfer entirely. Every agent and broker in the state needs a clear, current understanding of who must physically attend or officiate the closing table.

Louisiana is one of a handful of states where the closing process centers on a notarial act rather than a simple deed execution. The distinction between an attorney, a notary public, and a title company matters more here than in any other jurisdiction. Confusing their roles is one of the fastest ways to land yourself in front of the Louisiana Real Estate Commission.

## The Civil Law Difference: Why Louisiana Closings Are Unique

**Louisiana's legal system derives from French and Spanish civil law, not English common law, and that single fact reshapes every closing in the state.** Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 1839, a transfer of immovable property must be made by authentic act — meaning it must be passed before a notary public and two competent witnesses. No other state imposes this precise requirement.

An "authentic act" is not the same as a notarized document in Texas or Florida. In those states, a notary merely acknowledges a signature. In Louisiana, the notary reads the act of sale aloud, confirms the parties understand the terms, and then the parties sign in the notary's presence alongside two witnesses. The notary is an active participant, not a passive stamp.

This requirement comes from Louisiana Revised Statutes Title 35, Sections 1 through 625, which govern the commissioning, authority, and responsibilities of notaries public. If the act of sale does not comply with these provisions, the parish clerk of court will refuse to record it, and the transfer of ownership fails.

## Attorney, Notary, or Title Company — Defining Each Role

The confusion usually starts because Louisiana notaries are not what agents from other states expect. In Louisiana, a notary public is a quasi-judicial officer with substantially more authority than notaries elsewhere. They can prepare legal instruments, take depositions, and perform closings independently — without being licensed attorneys.

| Role | Authority at Closing | Licensed By | Required at Louisiana Closing? |
|------|---------------------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Louisiana Notary Public | May prepare and pass the act of sale, perform title examination, issue title opinions | Secretary of State (RS 35:1 et seq.) | Yes — must officiate the act of sale |
| Licensed Attorney | May perform all notary functions if also commissioned as notary; may provide legal advice | Louisiana Supreme Court / State Bar | No — not independently required |
| Title Company | May issue title insurance, coordinate document preparation, hold escrow funds | Louisiana Department of Insurance | No — cannot officiate act of sale alone |
| Real Estate Agent | May coordinate parties, present offers, attend closing | LREC (RS 37:1430 et seq.) | No — attendance is customary, not mandatory |

The critical takeaway: a title company cannot close a Louisiana real estate transaction by itself. It can issue a title commitment, it can underwrite title insurance, and it can coordinate the closing — but the act of sale must still be passed before a commissioned Louisiana notary public with two witnesses present.

## What the Louisiana Real Estate Commission Expects from Agents

The LREC regulates agent conduct under Louisiana Revised Statutes Title 37, Chapter 16. While the commission does not dictate who officiates the closing, it does hold agents accountable for facilitating transactions that comply with state law. Rule 2.01 of the LREC Rules and Regulations requires that licensees act in accordance with all applicable statutes.

**If you steer a client toward a closing arrangement that fails to include a properly commissioned notary, you are exposed to LREC disciplinary proceedings under RS 37:1455.** Penalties include license suspension, revocation, and fines up to $5,000 per violation. The commission has specifically cited agents who allowed out-of-state title companies to conduct closings remotely without a Louisiana notary present.

The LREC-approved Louisiana Residential Agreement to Buy or Sell (commonly referenced as the "LREC Form 1" or the "LAR Form") includes a section designating the closing entity. Agents must ensure this section is completed accurately and that the designated entity includes or has arranged for a Louisiana notary to officiate. Leaving it blank or filling it with an entity that cannot legally close the transaction creates liability from the moment the contract is executed.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make at Louisiana Closings

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in LREC complaint files and title insurance claims. Each one is preventable with basic diligence.

**First, agents assume a title company handles everything.** In states like Texas, a title company runs the closing start to finish. Agents transferring that assumption to Louisiana discover too late that the title company coordinated document preparation but nobody arranged for a notary. The closing stalls, the lock expires, and the buyer's lender demands a new rate.

Second, agents confuse a notary acknowledgment with an authentic act. A seller who signs a power of attorney in another state may have it "notarized" under that state's rules, but Louisiana may not recognize it as an authentic act for purposes of transferring immovable property. The distinction under Civil Code Article 1833 is absolute: an authentic act requires a Louisiana notary or equivalent authority.

Third, agents fail to confirm the notary's active commission. Louisiana notary commissions are issued for life in Orleans Parish but for five-year terms in other parishes under RS 35:191. An expired commission renders the entire act of sale void. Agents should independently verify commission status through the [Louisiana Secretary of State's notary database](https://www.sos.la.gov/NotaryAndCertifications/Pages/default.aspx).

Fourth, agents neglect the two-witness requirement. The act of sale requires two competent witnesses who are present at the signing. Using a title company employee who stepped out of the room, or listing a witness who was not physically present, invalidates the act. This mistake is especially common in hybrid or partially remote closings.

Fifth, agents allow clients to rely on "mobile notary" services designed for other states. A traveling notary from a national signing service may not hold a Louisiana commission, and even if they do, they may not understand authentic act requirements. The result is a defective closing that requires a corrective act — at additional cost and delay.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under RS 37:1447 and LREC Rule 4.01. When an agent under your supervision facilitates a closing that violates notary requirements, the broker is jointly liable for the resulting complaint. This is not theoretical — LREC enforcement actions in 2024 and 2025 named supervising brokers alongside agents in multiple cases involving defective closings.

| Broker Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Closing entity designation on Form 1 | Confirm a Louisiana notary is identified or arranged | Every transaction |
| Notary commission verification | Cross-reference with SOS database | Every transaction |
| Witness documentation | Confirm two witnesses listed and present | Post-closing file review |
| Out-of-state POA compliance | Verify authentic act or properly apostilled document | As applicable |
| Title company coordination | Confirm title company is not acting as sole closing entity | Every transaction |

Brokers should implement a closing compliance checklist that flags any transaction where the closing entity is an out-of-state company, where a mobile notary service is used, or where the designated notary's commission cannot be independently verified. These are the transactions most likely to generate complaints, E&O claims, and title defects. Transaction files must include a copy of the notary's commission or a reference to its verification. Brokers who treat this as optional are gambling with their license. The LREC has been increasingly proactive about file audits, and a missing notary verification is low-hanging fruit for investigators.

## How Remote and Hybrid Closings Fit the Louisiana Framework

Remote Online Notarization became available in Louisiana through Act 264 of 2020, codified in RS 35:626 et seq. This statute allows a Louisiana notary to perform certain notarial acts via audio-visual communication technology. However, RON for immovable property transfers requires strict compliance with additional rules issued by the Secretary of State.

The notary must still be commissioned in Louisiana. The audio-visual session must be recorded and stored for a minimum period. Two witnesses must still be present — either physically with the notary or connected via the same technology platform. Agents who tell clients "we can do this over Zoom" without confirming the notary's RON authorization and technical compliance are setting up a voidable transaction.

Not every Louisiana notary is authorized to perform RON. The Secretary of State maintains a separate registry of notaries approved for remote notarization. Agents must verify this authorization independently, especially when working with clients relocating from out of state who expect fully remote closings. ## Liability When Things Go Wrong

A defective act of sale does not just inconvenience the parties — it creates a title defect that can persist for years. The buyer does not have valid ownership. The seller may still be legally responsible for the property. The lender's mortgage may be unenforceable. Everyone involved in the transaction faces potential liability.

**For the agent, LREC sanctions are only the beginning.** Civil liability under Louisiana Code of Civil Procedure Article 1005 allows damaged parties to sue agents and brokers for negligence in facilitating a defective transaction. E&O insurance may cover some exposure, but policy exclusions for "known violations of law" often apply when the agent should have known the closing was non-compliant.

Title insurance companies will deny claims arising from closings they did not underwrite or where the act of sale was fundamentally defective. This leaves the buyer without recourse through the title policy and shifts the blame directly to the professionals who coordinated the closing. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) or skip verification steps are the ones most exposed.

## Practical Steps for Agents in 2026

Every Louisiana transaction should begin with a confirmed closing arrangement that includes a commissioned Louisiana notary. Write the notary's name and commission information into the purchase agreement at the time of execution. Do not leave this to the last week before closing.

Confirm that the title company, if one is involved, understands its role as a coordinator and insurer — not as the officiant. Request written confirmation from the title company that a Louisiana notary has been engaged for the act of sale. Keep this confirmation in your transaction file.
