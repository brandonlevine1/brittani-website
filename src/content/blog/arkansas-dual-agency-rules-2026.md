## Understanding Arkansas Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The rules governing dual representation in Arkansas real estate transactions are more prescriptive than many agents realize. Arkansas dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 carry specific statutory requirements that, when ignored, put licenses at risk and deals in jeopardy. The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) enforces these provisions under Arkansas Code Annotated Title 17, Chapter 42, and the consequences for noncompliance have real teeth.

Arkansas permits both dual agency and designated agency, but the two function under different legal frameworks with distinct obligations. Conflating them is one of the fastest ways to trigger a complaint. This post breaks down exactly what each structure requires, which forms you must use, where agents routinely fail, and what brokers need to audit before problems reach AREC's enforcement desk.

## The Statutory Framework: Ark. Code 17-42-108

Arkansas agency relationships are governed primarily by [Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-108](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/laws-and-rules/), which defines the types of representation a licensee may offer. The statute recognizes seller agency, buyer agency, dual agency, and designated agency as permissible relationships. Each requires specific disclosure and consent procedures before any substantive representation begins.

**Dual agency arises when a single licensee (or a single brokerage without designated agents) represents both the buyer and seller in the same transaction.** Under Ark. Code 17-42-108(c), this arrangement demands written informed consent from both parties. The consent must specify the limitations on the agent's ability to advocate, negotiate, or disclose confidential information on behalf of either party.

Designated agency, by contrast, occurs when the principal broker assigns separate licensees within the same brokerage to represent the buyer and the seller independently. The broker becomes a supervisory neutral rather than a dual agent, and each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties to their assigned client. This distinction matters enormously for liability purposes.

## Required Disclosures and Form Numbers

AREC mandates the use of the Agency Disclosure and Election Form at or before the first substantive contact with a consumer. This form outlines all available agency relationships and requires the consumer to acknowledge which type they have selected. Timing is not optional—presenting it after substantive discussions have occurred violates Commission Rule 10.11.

| Requirement | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Written consent required | Yes, from both parties | Yes, from both parties |
| Fiduciary duties limited | Yes—balanced obligation to both | No—full duties to each client |
| Broker's role | Active dual agent | Supervisory neutral |
| Confidential info sharing | Prohibited without permission | Prohibited between designated agents |
| Form trigger | First substantive contact | First substantive contact |

Beyond the Agency Disclosure and Election Form, agents entering a dual agency relationship must provide additional written explanation of what dual agency means in practice. AREC's prescribed language warns consumers that the agent cannot advocate for one party's interests over the other. Failure to include this language—or substituting brokerage-created language that omits required elements—can result in the consent being deemed invalid.

When designated agency is elected, the brokerage must maintain documentation showing which agents were assigned to which parties. AREC expects this assignment to be formalized before the agents begin substantive work for their respective clients, not retroactively documented after an offer is already on the table.

## Consequences of Noncompliance

The penalties for violating Arkansas agency disclosure rules extend beyond a slap on the wrist. AREC has authority under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311 to impose disciplinary action including formal reprimand, mandatory continuing education, fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension, and license revocation. The severity depends on whether the violation was inadvertent or reflects a pattern.

**Beyond regulatory discipline, a failure to properly disclose and obtain consent for dual agency can expose the agent and brokerage to civil liability.** An aggrieved party may argue that consent was not truly informed, rendering the agency relationship voidable. If a court agrees, the transaction itself may be rescinded, and the brokerage may be ordered to disgorge its commission.

In practice, the most dangerous scenario involves a buyer who later claims they did not understand what dual agency meant and that the agent favored the seller's interests. Without a properly executed, AREC-compliant written consent form bearing the buyer's signature, the agent has virtually no defense. The agent who relies on verbal acknowledgment or a generic brokerage form without the required statutory language is building a case against themselves.

Transaction files missing the disclosure form have also been flagged during routine AREC audits, which can be triggered randomly or by complaint. Agents sometimes assume that because a deal closed without issues, the paperwork gap will never matter. It matters the moment AREC requests the file, and the absence of a required form converts a routine audit into an investigation.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Arkansas

Three to five errors appear repeatedly in AREC disciplinary actions and broker audit failures involving dual and designated agency.

**First, agents present the Agency Disclosure and Election Form too late.** The statute and Commission rules require disclosure at or before first substantive contact. Agents who wait until the listing appointment is underway or until a buyer has already toured properties are technically in violation before the relationship is even formalized. "Substantive contact" includes discussing a client's specific needs, financial qualifications, or property preferences—not just signing a contract.

Second, agents confuse dual agency with designated agency and document the wrong one. A brokerage with two agents working opposite sides of the same deal is operating under designated agency, not dual agency. When the file paperwork reflects dual agency consent but the actual structure is designated, the documentation fails to match reality—creating a compliance gap that confuses both AREC auditors and, potentially, a jury.

Third, agents in dual agency situations continue to advise one party on price strategy or negotiation tactics. The moment you became a dual agent, your obligation to advocate died. Telling your buyer client that the seller will probably accept $10,000 less, or telling your seller that the buyer is desperate, violates the balanced duty you owe under dual agency. This is the mistake that generates the most complaints because the harmed party feels genuinely betrayed.

Fourth, agents operating under designated agency share confidential client information with each other or with the broker. The information walls required by designated agency are not metaphorical. An offhand comment at a Monday meeting about what price a buyer will really pay can unravel the entire designated agency structure and expose the brokerage to claims of constructive dual agency without consent.

Fifth, brokers fail to formally assign designated agents in writing. Without documentation of which licensee represents which party, there is no evidence that designated agency was properly established. AREC can treat the transaction as an undisclosed dual agency, triggering all of the penalties described above.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers bear supervisory liability under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-305 for the agency practices of their affiliated licensees. A broker who fails to implement systems for compliance is not shielded by claiming ignorance of an agent's error. This makes proactive auditing a matter of self-preservation, not just good management.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Agency Disclosure Form present in file | Every transaction | Signed form with date |
| Dual agency consent language matches AREC requirements | Every dual agency file | Signed consent with statutory language |
| Designated agent assignment documented | Every in-house both-sides deal | Written assignment with date and names |
| Information barrier protocols followed | Ongoing | Broker attestation, meeting policies |
| Timing of disclosure delivery | Spot-check quarterly | Date comparison: form vs. first contact |

Brokers should not rely solely on agents self-reporting compliance. A quarterly file audit—pulling 10 to 15 random transaction files and verifying that agency disclosures are present, properly executed, and timely—catches problems before AREC does. The cost of an internal audit pales against the cost of defending a disciplinary action.

**Brokerage policy manuals should include explicit instructions on the difference between dual and designated agency, when each applies, and what documentation is required.** Simply having a policy is not enough. Brokers need a signed acknowledgment from each affiliated licensee confirming they received and understood the policy. This creates a paper trail showing supervisory diligence if an agent later violates the rules.

For brokerages with multiple offices or large teams, the risk of accidental dual representation increases. Implementing a system that flags when two affiliated agents enter the same transaction—before they begin work—prevents the scramble of retroactive documentation. ## Designated Agency: The Preferred Risk-Reduction Tool

Many Arkansas brokerages have moved toward designated agency as the default for in-house transactions specifically because it preserves full fiduciary duties for each client. The buyer's designated agent can still advise on price, negotiate aggressively, and share market intelligence—because they owe no duty to the seller. The seller's designated agent operates identically on the other side.

This structure reduces complaint risk because neither client feels underserved. The catch is that it demands genuine separation between the two agents. Social relationships within the brokerage, shared office space, and team structures can all undermine the information barriers that make designated agency legally distinct from dual agency. Brokers must take these barriers seriously, not treat them as a checkbox exercise.

Agents moving between firms or [managing pipeline across multiple deals](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) should verify their brokerage's specific designated agency protocols during onboarding. Protocols vary from firm to firm even though the underlying statute is the same, and carrying assumptions from a prior brokerage can lead to inadvertent violations.

## How to Protect Yourself Going Forward

Documentation timing is everything. The best practice is to present the Agency Disclosure and Election Form at the very first meeting—whether it is a listing consultation, a buyer intake meeting, or even a phone call that turns substantive. If the conversation shifts from casual to specific, the form must follow. Agents who [understand why missed deadlines kill deals](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) already know that procedural discipline prevents downstream disasters.

Keep copies of every signed form in both paper and digital format. AREC can request files years after closing, and the agent who cannot produce a disclosure form from 2024 in 2026 faces the same exposure as the agent who never obtained one. File retention is not glamorous work, but it is the foundation of defensible practice.
