North Dakota stands out among the 50 states for one reason that surprises many agents who move in from other markets: it does not impose a traditional real estate transfer tax. That single fact shapes everything about how closing costs are calculated, how settlement statements are prepared, and what your clients actually owe the county recorder. Understanding the North Dakota transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 is not optional knowledge — it is the kind of detail that separates agents who close smoothly from those who scramble at the settlement table.

This post breaks down every fee, form, and statutory reference you need, along with the mistakes that trip agents up and the audit points brokers should be watching.

## Why North Dakota Has No Transfer Tax — and What It Has Instead

North Dakota is one of a handful of states that has never enacted a deed transfer tax or real estate excise tax at the state level. There is no equivalent of the transfer stamps you would encounter in states like Wisconsin or West Virginia. The North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) simply does not include a provision taxing the conveyance of real property between parties.

That does not mean recording a transaction is free. County recorders across North Dakota's 53 counties charge per-page recording fees governed by [NDCC Chapter 11-18](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t11c18.pdf). These fees fund the maintenance of public land records and the technology systems county offices depend on.

Agents sometimes confuse the absence of a transfer tax with an absence of closing-related government fees altogether. That misunderstanding leads to inaccurate buyer and seller estimates, which can erode trust fast. Recording fees, while modest compared to transfer taxes in high-cost states, still need to appear on every Closing Disclosure.

## North Dakota Recording Fee Schedules 2026: The Numbers

**The base recording fee structure in North Dakota has remained stable, but agents need to verify county-specific surcharges every year.** Under NDCC 11-18-05, the county recorder charges $10 for the first page of any document submitted for recording. Each additional page costs $3. A standard two-page warranty deed, for example, costs $13 to record.

Several document types carry the same fee structure, but the total varies based on document length and the county involved. Here is a quick comparison of typical recording costs agents encounter:

| Document | Typical Page Count | Base Fee (NDCC 11-18-05) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warranty Deed | 2 pages | $13 | Most common conveyance instrument |
| Quit Claim Deed | 1-2 pages | $10-$13 | Used in divorces, estate transfers |
| Mortgage / Deed of Trust | 10-20 pages | $37-$67 | Lender documents vary in length |
| Satisfaction of Mortgage | 1-2 pages | $10-$13 | Seller's lender files this |
| Affidavit of Heirship | 2-4 pages | $13-$19 | Probate-related recordings |

Some counties also charge a small technology modernization fee. Cass County and Burleigh County, for instance, have historically included nominal surcharges to fund digital records systems. Always confirm the current schedule with the specific county recorder's office handling the transaction.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

North Dakota real estate transactions rely on a handful of standardized forms, many of them published by the North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR). The purchase agreement itself — typically the NDAR Residential Purchase Agreement — is where recording fee allocation between buyer and seller gets negotiated. If the agreement is silent on who pays, local custom generally assigns the deed recording fee to the buyer and the mortgage satisfaction recording to the seller.

**The deed itself must comply with NDCC 47-19-04, which sets out the minimum requirements for a valid conveyance.** The statute requires a description of the property, the grantor's signature, and proper acknowledgment before a notary. Agents do not draft deeds — that is the attorney's or title company's role — but agents need to confirm these elements are in place before the document reaches the recorder.

For transactions involving title insurance, [agents should understand how the closing process works in states without mandatory attorney involvement](/blog/wisconsin-title-and-closing-procedures-2026). North Dakota does not require an attorney at closing, and most transactions are handled through title companies. The title company prepares the settlement statement, records documents, and disburses funds.

One form that catches out-of-state agents off guard is the Statement of Full Consideration, sometimes referenced in county recording requirements. While not universally required across all 53 counties, some recorders request a statement of the sale price to accompany the deed. Since North Dakota has no transfer tax, this statement serves a property tax assessment function rather than a tax collection one.

## Who Pays What: Buyer vs. Seller Fee Allocation

Fee allocation in North Dakota is almost entirely governed by the purchase agreement, not by statute. There is no state law mandating that one party must pay a specific recording fee. That said, long-standing custom in most North Dakota markets follows a predictable pattern.

| Fee Type | Customary Payer | Negotiable? |
|---|---|---|
| Deed recording | Buyer | Yes |
| Mortgage recording | Buyer | Yes |
| Satisfaction of existing mortgage | Seller | Yes |
| Title insurance (owner's policy) | Negotiable | Yes |
| Title insurance (lender's policy) | Buyer | Yes |
| Title search / abstract continuation | Negotiable | Yes |

**Agents who assume custom equals law are making a dangerous mistake.** Every single one of these line items can be shifted between parties through negotiation. In competitive markets, sellers sometimes agree to cover the buyer's recording fees as a concession. In buyer-friendly markets, the opposite can happen.

If you work transactions across multiple states, the [breakdown of who pays closing costs can vary dramatically](/blog/wisconsin-who-pays-closing-costs-2026). North Dakota's flexibility here is actually a strength for skilled negotiators, but it requires clear language in the purchase agreement to avoid disputes at the closing table.

## North Dakota Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Even experienced agents stumble on recording fee details. Here are five specific errors that show up repeatedly in North Dakota transactions.

First, agents fail to account for multi-page mortgage documents when estimating closing costs. A 15-page mortgage costs $52 to record, not $10. Quoting the first-page fee alone leaves the buyer facing an unexpected line item on the Closing Disclosure. Title companies catch this, but by then the agent has already set incorrect expectations.

Second, agents neglect to verify county-specific fees. NDCC 11-18-05 sets the baseline, but individual counties can layer on additional charges. An agent who closes a deal in Walsh County using Cass County's fee schedule may be off by a few dollars — a small amount that signals carelessness to clients and colleagues alike.

Third, some agents forget that the deed must be recorded to protect the buyer's interest. Under NDCC 47-19-41, an unrecorded deed, while valid between the original parties, is not enforceable against a subsequent good-faith purchaser. If the title company does not record the deed promptly — or if a for-sale-by-owner transaction skips recording altogether — the buyer's ownership is legally vulnerable.

Fourth, agents miscommunicate the absence of a transfer tax as "no government fees at closing." This creates confusion when recording fees appear on the settlement statement. Clients feel blindsided, even though the amounts are small.

Fifth, agents sometimes list "transfer tax: $0" on preliminary estimates without explaining why. A single sentence noting that North Dakota does not impose a transfer tax, paired with the actual recording fees, prevents client questions and builds credibility.

## What Happens When Compliance Falls Apart

North Dakota does not impose fines on agents specifically for recording fee errors — those fees are a function of county recorder requirements, not licensing law. But the downstream consequences of sloppy handling are real.

If a deed is not properly recorded, the buyer's title is at risk. Title insurance claims can be denied if the agent or closing party failed to follow standard recording procedures. The North Dakota Real Estate Commission, which governs agent conduct under NDCC Chapter 43-23, can take disciplinary action if an agent's negligence in a transaction rises to the level of incompetence or misrepresentation.

A deal does not typically get cancelled over a recording fee discrepancy alone. However, RESPA and TRID rules enforced by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau require that the Closing Disclosure accurately reflect all recording fees. If the title company or agent provides a Loan Estimate with materially inaccurate recording fees, the lender may need to re-disclose and the closing date could slip. That delay can cascade into rate lock expirations, inspection contingency deadlines, and [the kind of missed deadlines that kill deals](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

> "Every instrument affecting the title to real property... must be recorded in the office of the recorder of the county in which the real property affected thereby is situated." — NDCC 47-19-01

That statute is not a suggestion. It is the backbone of North Dakota's recording system, and agents who treat recording as an afterthought are courting liability.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under NDCC 43-23-11.1 for the actions of their affiliated agents. When it comes to recording fees and transaction compliance, there are specific audit points every managing broker should build into their review process.

Start with the purchase agreement. Confirm that every agreement your agents submit includes clear language about who pays recording fees. Ambiguity here is the top source of closing-day disputes. If your brokerage uses the NDAR standard form, the relevant section is already there — but agents sometimes leave it blank or check conflicting boxes.

Review preliminary net sheets and buyer estimates. Are your agents quoting recording fees using current county schedules? A broker who spots a pattern of inaccurate estimates can address it in a team meeting before it becomes a complaint to the North Dakota Real Estate Commission.

Verify that post-closing files include recorded document copies. The title company handles recording, but your file should contain confirmation that the deed, mortgage, and any other recorded instruments were accepted by the county recorder. Missing confirmation is a red flag in any file audit.

Finally, confirm that agents are not providing legal advice about deed preparation. North Dakota agents may explain the process, but drafting deeds or advising on title-related legal questions crosses into unauthorized practice of law. Brokers who set this boundary clearly protect both the agent and the brokerage.
