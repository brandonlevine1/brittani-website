## The Definitive Answer on Maine Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

Maine stands firmly in the "attorney state" column, and that distinction carries real consequences for every transaction you touch. The question of Maine attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 is not theoretical — it determines who can legally prepare deeds, certify title, and disburse funds at the settlement table. Getting this wrong does not just stall a deal; it exposes you, your brokerage, and your clients to liability that no errors and omissions policy was designed to absorb.

Unlike states where a title company can independently run a closing from commitment to disbursement, Maine requires a licensed attorney to perform functions that would otherwise constitute the unauthorized practice of law. The Maine Board of Overseers of the Bar has repeatedly affirmed this position, and the Maine Real Estate Commission enforces it on the licensee side. This article breaks down exactly what the law requires, where agents stumble, and what brokers must do to keep their offices compliant.

## What Maine Law Actually Says About Attorney Involvement

**Maine's requirement for attorney involvement at closing is rooted in its unauthorized practice of law framework, not in a single tidy statute that says "attorneys must close real estate transactions."** The authority comes from multiple sources working together: Title 4 M.R.S. Section 807 grants the Maine Supreme Judicial Court exclusive authority to define and regulate the practice of law, and the Court's opinions have consistently held that preparing deeds, mortgages, and conducting title examinations for certification purposes constitutes legal practice.

The [Maine Board of Overseers of the Bar](https://www.mebaroverseers.org/) has issued advisory opinions clarifying that non-attorneys — including title company employees and real estate agents — may not prepare legal instruments conveying real property interests. Title insurance agents may issue commitments and policies, but the actual settlement, deed preparation, and title certification must occur under attorney supervision.

This means a title company in Maine typically operates alongside an attorney, not instead of one. The title company may underwrite the policy, but the closing attorney conducts the settlement, prepares the deed, and certifies clear title to the insurer.

## How Maine Differs From Title-Company States

Understanding the structural difference matters when you are explaining the process to clients who relocated from states where they never met an attorney at closing. Here is how the roles break down:

| Function | Maine (Attorney State) | Title-Company States (e.g., Wisconsin) |
|----------|----------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Deed preparation | Licensed attorney required | Title company or escrow officer |
| Title examination/certification | Attorney certifies to insurer | Title company staff examines |
| Closing/settlement conduct | Attorney supervises or conducts | Title agent or escrow officer |
| Title insurance issuance | Title insurer underwrites; attorney may be agent | Title company issues directly |
| Disbursement of funds | Attorney's trust account or title company IOLTA | Title/escrow company account |

The practical effect is that your clients will pay an attorney fee — usually ranging from $500 to $1,500 depending on complexity — that would not exist in a pure title-company state. That fee covers deed drafting, title review, document preparation, and closing supervision. Agents who [understand where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) know that failing to set this expectation early creates friction at the finish line.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Maine real estate transactions rely on forms published by the Maine Association of REALTORS (MAR) and approved by the Maine Real Estate Commission. The key forms intersecting with closing requirements include:

The MAR Purchase and Sale Agreement (Form 102) contains provisions specifying attorney involvement and allocating closing costs. Paragraph 12 of Form 102 addresses title examination requirements and establishes that the seller must deliver a warranty deed prepared by an attorney. Agents must ensure this paragraph is not inadvertently modified to remove attorney involvement.

**Form 706, the Maine Seller's Property Disclosure Statement, must be completed and delivered before closing — but the closing attorney also reviews it for legal sufficiency.** The TRID-compliant Closing Disclosure (federal CFPB form) must be prepared or reviewed by the closing attorney to ensure accuracy of fees attributed to the transaction.

Maine also requires a Real Estate Transfer Tax Declaration (Form REA-1) filed with the county registry of deeds. The closing attorney typically prepares this form, calculating the transfer tax at the current rate of $2.20 per $500 of consideration (split $1.10 buyer / $1.10 seller unless otherwise negotiated). Agents who pre-calculate these figures on net sheets but get the math wrong create last-minute chaos that the attorney must untangle.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Regarding Closing in Maine

The errors below surface repeatedly in Commission complaint files and broker audits. Each one is avoidable, and each one carries genuine consequences.

**First, agents direct clients to "just use the title company" without attorney involvement.** This happens most often with out-of-state agents joining a Maine brokerage or agents whose clients insist the process worked differently in their prior state. If a closing proceeds without proper attorney supervision, the deed may be challenged, title insurance coverage could be voided, and the agent faces a Commission complaint for facilitating unauthorized practice of law.

Second, agents draft or modify deed language themselves. Even inserting a simple survivorship clause or correcting a name spelling on a deed constitutes legal practice in Maine. The Maine Real Estate Commission's Rules Chapter 10, Section 3 explicitly prohibits licensees from drafting legal instruments. Violations can result in license suspension or revocation under 32 M.R.S. Section 13186.

Third, agents fail to confirm the closing attorney's availability before scheduling. Maine has a limited pool of active real estate closing attorneys in rural counties. Scheduling a closing date in the purchase agreement without verifying attorney availability leads to extension requests, missed deadlines, and frustrated clients. Agents who [understand why deadlines get missed](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) build attorney confirmation into their timeline from day one.

Fourth, agents neglect to inform buyers about the separate attorney fee early in the transaction. When this fee appears for the first time on the Closing Disclosure, buyers who budgeted tightly may scramble for funds or blame their agent for lack of transparency. Disclosing this cost during the offer stage — verbally and in writing — eliminates the surprise.

Fifth, agents assume "attorney supervision" means the attorney needs to physically attend closing. Maine law requires supervision, not necessarily physical presence at every moment. Some closings proceed with the attorney's paralegal handling document execution under the attorney's direct supervision, with the attorney available by phone or video. However, the attorney must have reviewed all documents, approved disbursement, and remain responsible for the transaction. Agents who tell clients "the attorney doesn't need to be there" without understanding the supervision standard risk facilitating a non-compliant closing.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory liability under 32 M.R.S. Section 13174 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. When it comes to closing procedures, the audit checklist is specific and non-negotiable.

**Brokers must verify that every closed transaction file contains documentation of attorney involvement.** This means a copy of the attorney's engagement letter or a settlement statement bearing the attorney's name and bar number. If a file shows a closing conducted solely by a title company with no attorney of record, that file represents a compliance failure the broker must address immediately.

Brokers should maintain an approved list of closing attorneys and require agents to confirm attorney engagement within a defined timeframe after accepted offer — typically within five business days. This prevents the scramble described above and creates an auditable checkpoint. The broker's policies and procedures manual, required under Maine Real Estate Commission Rules Chapter 10, should explicitly address the attorney requirement and prohibit agents from directing clients away from attorney involvement.

Brokers operating multiple offices across Maine's counties should also recognize that attorney custom varies by region. In Cumberland County, large firms handle high volume; in Aroostook County, a single practitioner may manage closings across several towns. ## Liability Exposure When Compliance Fails

The consequences of conducting or facilitating a closing without proper attorney involvement in Maine cascade across multiple domains. The Maine Board of Overseers of the Bar may bring unauthorized practice of law proceedings against any non-attorney who performed legal functions. This carries potential injunctive relief and contempt sanctions.

On the licensee side, the Maine Real Estate Commission can impose sanctions under 32 M.R.S. Section 13186 ranging from a formal warning to license revocation. Fines of up to $1,500 per violation are authorized. Beyond regulatory action, a title defect resulting from improper closing creates civil liability — the buyer may sue the agent, the brokerage, and the title insurer for damages required to cure the defect or for diminished property value.

Title insurance underwriters in Maine routinely audit their agent-attorneys for compliance. If an underwriter discovers that closings were conducted without proper attorney supervision, it may rescind the agent's authority to issue policies, void coverage on affected transactions, and report the matter to the Board of Overseers. The downstream effect on the buyer who loses title insurance coverage is catastrophic — and the originating agent's involvement in the transaction makes them a named defendant.

## How 2026 Changes Affect the Attorney Requirement

No legislation pending or enacted for 2026 eliminates Maine's attorney-closing requirement. The Maine Legislature has periodically considered bills to allow limited settlement functions by licensed title agents, but none have advanced past committee in recent sessions. The attorney requirement remains firmly in place, and agents should not rely on rumors of deregulation to justify shortcuts.

What has changed is the increasing use of remote online notarization (RON) under Maine's RON statute (33 M.R.S. Section 203-A), which allows certain closing documents to be notarized remotely. This does not remove the attorney requirement — the attorney still supervises the closing — but it does allow the physical logistics to shift. Agents facilitating RON closings must confirm that the closing attorney has approved the remote format and that all parties have adequate technology access.

The Maine Real Estate Commission also updated its continuing education requirements effective 2025-2026, adding mandatory ethics hours that explicitly cover unauthorized practice of law boundaries. Agents renewing in 2026 will encounter this material in their required coursework, making ignorance an increasingly indefensible position.
