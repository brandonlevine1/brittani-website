Colorado's HOA and condominium disclosure framework is one of the more prescriptive in the Mountain West, and the **Colorado hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** carry real teeth for agents who get sloppy. Between the Colorado Common Interest Ownership Act (CCIOA), the Real Estate Commission's approved forms, and the statutory timelines that govern buyer cancellation rights, there is very little room for improvisation. This post breaks down the statutes, forms, deadlines, and liability exposure that every licensed agent and employing broker in the state should have dialed in before the next closing.

## The Statutory Framework: CCIOA and C.R.S. 38-33.3

The Colorado Common Interest Ownership Act, codified at C.R.S. 38-33.3-101 through 38-33.3-405, is the backbone of every HOA and condominium disclosure obligation in the state. Section 222 specifically governs resale disclosures for units in common interest communities, mandating that sellers provide buyers with a defined set of documents before or during the inspection period. The [Colorado Division of Real Estate](https://dre.colorado.gov/) enforces compliance on the licensee side, while the HOA itself is bound by the statute's document production requirements.

**C.R.S. 38-33.3-222 requires disclosure of the declaration, bylaws, rules, current budget, reserve study (if one exists), and any pending assessments or litigation.** The statute also allows the HOA to charge a fee for preparing this packet, and recent amendments have placed caps on expedited delivery charges. Agents need to know that these documents are not optional add-ons; they are contractual deliverables that trigger buyer review rights.

The distinction between HOA communities governed by CCIOA and older communities formed before July 1, 1992 matters. Pre-CCIOA associations may not be subject to every provision of Section 222, but the Colorado Real Estate Commission forms still impose disclosure obligations on the parties through the contract itself. If you are listing in a pre-1992 community, confirm whether CCIOA applies or whether the contract language alone controls.

## Colorado Real Estate Commission Forms You Must Know

The Colorado Real Estate Commission publishes approved forms that implement CCIOA's disclosure requirements at the transaction level. The two most relevant for HOA transactions are the **Contract to Buy and Sell Real Estate (Residential)** — form CBS1-7-24 as currently approved — and the **Seller's Property Disclosure** form (SPD19). Within the CBS, Section 7 addresses title and association documents, including the specific items the seller must deliver and the buyer's right to review them.

Form CBS1 includes fields for specifying how many days the buyer has to review HOA documents after receipt and whether the seller or buyer will pay for production costs. Section 7.3 specifically addresses the association documents deadline, the right to object, and the buyer's termination right if objections are not resolved. If you leave this section blank or miscalculate the timeline, you have created an ambiguity that could unravel the deal or expose your client to liability.

The **HOA Information & Documents (HRIS)** addendum, sometimes referenced as the Association Information Sheet, is used to communicate specific association details such as monthly assessments, transfer fees, and contact information for the management company. This is distinct from the statutory resale packet itself and should not be treated as a substitute for full CCIOA compliance.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Who Provides It | Statutory Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBS1-7-24 (Section 7) | Sets buyer review deadlines and HOA document delivery terms | Agents draft; parties sign | Contract-based (incorporates CCIOA) |
| SPD19 (Seller's Property Disclosure) | Discloses known material facts including HOA issues | Seller | C.R.S. 38-35.7-102 |
| Resale Disclosure Certificate | Full HOA document packet (budget, reserves, litigation, rules) | HOA / Management Company | C.R.S. 38-33.3-222 |
| HRIS / Association Addendum | Summary of HOA fees, contacts, and basic terms | Listing agent / Seller | Commission-approved form |

## Colorado HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Timelines and Deadlines

Timing is where most deals go sideways. The buyer's right to review HOA documents is contractual — meaning the number of days specified in Section 7 of the CBS governs when the buyer must object or accept. Unlike inspection periods, this clock does not start until the buyer actually receives the documents. If the HOA takes two weeks to produce the resale certificate, the review period has not yet begun.

**Agents representing buyers must track two clocks independently: the inspection objection deadline and the HOA document review deadline.** These can overlap or run sequentially depending on how the contract is drafted. A common practice is to make the HOA review period coterminous with the inspection period, but if the documents arrive late, that alignment breaks down.

Under C.R.S. 38-33.3-222(2), the HOA is required to produce the resale disclosure certificate within a "reasonable time" — but the statute does not define a hard day count for standard delivery. Expedited delivery can be requested for an additional fee (capped under recent amendments), but even that does not guarantee a specific number of calendar days. Smart listing agents order the resale certificate the day the property goes under contract — not after inspection.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The liability exposure for disclosure failures in Colorado runs in three directions: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and contract termination. The Colorado Real Estate Commission can issue fines, require additional education, or suspend a license for failure to follow commission-approved procedures related to HOA disclosures. Commission Rule 6.16 requires brokers to exercise reasonable skill and care, which includes ensuring that contractual deadlines are met and that clients receive the documents they are entitled to.

On the civil side, buyers who do not receive required HOA documents retain their termination rights indefinitely — the review clock never starts. This means a buyer can walk away weeks or even months into a transaction if the seller never properly delivered the CCIOA packet. The seller's agent who failed to coordinate delivery may face a breach-of-duty claim from the seller, and the buyer's agent who failed to demand the documents may face a negligence claim from their own client.

Contract cancellation is the most immediate consequence. If a buyer terminates under the HOA document review provision, they receive a full refund of earnest money. Sellers who were counting on that money to fund their own purchase are suddenly scrambling. The deal collapses not because of a material defect but because of a paperwork failure that could have been prevented with a basic [checklist-driven approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Colorado HOA Disclosures

**First, agents frequently confuse the HRIS summary sheet with the full statutory resale disclosure certificate.** The summary is helpful context, but it does not satisfy CCIOA requirements. Delivering the HRIS alone and checking the box does not start the buyer's review period. The full packet — declaration, bylaws, rules, budget, reserves, assessments, litigation — must be delivered.

Second, listing agents wait too long to order the resale certificate from the HOA or management company. If the property is in a large community managed by a third-party company, production can take 7 to 14 business days. Ordering on day one of the contract rather than day five can be the difference between meeting the deadline and blowing it. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on HOA documents often do so because they treated it as a low-priority task.

Third, buyer's agents fail to calendar the HOA review deadline as a distinct item from the inspection deadline. When these two periods are written as separate deadlines in the CBS (which they should be), missing the HOA objection window means the buyer has accepted whatever is in those documents — even if they never actually read them.

Fourth, agents neglect to confirm delivery. Sending documents via email without read receipts or handing a stack of papers to a buyer without a signed acknowledgment creates a dispute about whether the review clock ever started. Document delivery confirmation should be in writing, timestamped, and filed.

Fifth, agents operating in communities that predate CCIOA assume that no disclosure obligations apply. Even if the statute does not technically apply, the Contract to Buy and Sell still contains HOA document provisions. If those provisions are in the contract and unfulfilled, the legal consequences are contractual, not statutory — but they are still real.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers in Colorado bear supervisory responsibility under Commission Rule 6.1 for every transaction their licensees handle. When it comes to HOA disclosures, that means brokers should be auditing three things in every file involving a common interest community.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Resale Certificate Delivery | Timestamped delivery confirmation in file | No confirmation or delivery date after review deadline |
| CBS Section 7 Completion | All blanks filled, days specified, payment responsibility assigned | Blank fields or "TBD" entries |
| Deadline Tracking | HOA review deadline calendared separately from inspection | Single combined deadline with no tracking of document receipt date |
| Pre-CCIOA Communities | Notation in file regarding applicability of C.R.S. 38-33.3-222 | No documentation of analysis |

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-manage HOA compliance without any oversight system are accepting risk that flows uphill.** A single missed disclosure can trigger a Commission complaint, an E&O claim, or both. The supervisory duty is not theoretical — it is the basis for the majority of broker-level discipline actions in Colorado's annual enforcement reports.

Transaction files should include a copy of the full resale certificate, a dated delivery confirmation, and a record of the buyer's response (acceptance, objection, or termination) within the review period. If any of these three items are missing, the file is incomplete. Brokers running [a tight pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) will catch these gaps before they become problems at closing.
