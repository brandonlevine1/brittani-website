## Who Actually Needs to Be at the Alaska Closing Table?

The question of **Alaska attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026** generates more confusion than it should. Alaska is not an attorney-closing state, which means a licensed title or escrow company can handle settlement without a lawyer present. Yet agents across the state still stumble over the boundaries between legal advice, title services, and their own licensing authority.

Alaska operates under a "title state" framework where the vast majority of residential transactions close through title companies or escrow agents licensed by the Alaska Division of Banking and Securities under AS 06.60. Attorneys may attend closing and often do when deals involve complex structures, but no state statute compels their presence. Understanding this distinction is not just academic — it determines who is liable for what when something breaks down.

This post breaks apart the specific statutes, forms, and compliance requirements that affect how Alaska agents should approach every closing in 2026. If you have been treating the closing table as someone else's problem, the risks outlined below should change your mind.

## Alaska's Legal Framework for Closings

**Alaska Statute 34.20 governs the recording and conveyance of real property**, including the deed and trust deed provisions that frame every closing. The state does not include a mandatory attorney-closing requirement anywhere in Title 34 or in the licensing statutes under AS 08.88, which regulate real estate brokers and salespersons. This puts Alaska squarely in the category of states where title companies run the show.

Escrow and title agents operating in the state must hold licensing under AS 06.60, administered by the Division of Banking and Securities within the Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development. Title insurers are separately regulated under AS 21.66, Alaska's title insurance statutes, which set requirements for title commitments, policy issuance, and claims handling.

The [Alaska Real Estate Commission](https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/cbpl/ProfessionalLicensing/RealEstateCommission.aspx) oversees agent conduct but does not prescribe closing procedures. Its authority stops at licensing, continuing education, and disciplinary enforcement for agents and brokers under AS 08.88. The gap between what the commission regulates and what happens at the closing table is precisely where agents get into trouble.

The practical differences between using an attorney and a title company in Alaska come down to scope of service, cost, and the type of transaction involved.

Most agents will work with a title company on the overwhelming majority of their deals. An attorney becomes relevant when there is a boundary dispute, a probate sale, an unresolved lien, or a transaction involving Native allotment land under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA). Those situations carry legal complexity that a title company simply cannot address.

Agents should know which entity is handling settlement on every deal and confirm licensing status before referring clients. A [failure to track these details is one reason deals fall apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at the last minute.

## What Alaska Agents Cannot Do at Closing

**Alaska Statute 08.88.401 defines the activities that require a real estate license — and just as importantly, the activities that exceed it.** Agents are authorized to facilitate the sale but are not authorized to prepare legal documents, interpret contract clauses with legal opinions, or act as settlement agents unless separately licensed.

Alaska Bar Rule 11 addresses the unauthorized practice of law. An agent who drafts a deed, advises a client on the legal effect of an easement, or interprets title exceptions is crossing into territory reserved for attorneys. The Alaska Bar Association has historically pursued unauthorized practice complaints, and real estate agents are a recurring target.

The Alaska Real Estate Commission's standardized purchase agreement forms — including the Alaska Association of REALTORS (AAR) Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement — were specifically developed so agents can fill in blanks without practicing law. The moment an agent modifies boilerplate language or adds legal conditions outside the form's preprinted fields, they are exposed.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Around Closing

Three to five errors recur with enough frequency that every broker in the state should be watching for them.

**First, agents routinely advise clients on title exceptions without directing them to an attorney or the title officer.** A Schedule B exception on a title commitment is not the agent's document to interpret. When an agent tells a buyer, "That utility easement won't affect you," they have just delivered a legal opinion they are not licensed to give. If the easement later restricts the buyer's plans, the agent faces liability.

Second, agents fail to confirm escrow licensing. Not every entity advertising escrow services in Alaska holds a valid license under AS 06.60. The Division of Banking and Securities maintains a public licensee database, and checking it takes two minutes. Agents who direct clients to unlicensed escrow operations share in the resulting exposure.

Third, agents skip the closing disclosure review window. The TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure (TRID) rules require a three-business-day waiting period after the buyer receives the Closing Disclosure. Agents who pressure title companies to accelerate this timeline — or who schedule closings without confirming delivery — risk triggering a federal compliance violation that can unwind an entire transaction.

Fourth, agents in remote areas of Alaska sometimes participate in "kitchen table closings" where no title company or attorney is present, documents are signed informally, and recording happens after the fact. This practice creates massive title defects and insurance gaps. If something goes wrong, the agent's errors-and-omissions insurance may not cover an informal closing they helped facilitate.

Fifth, agents occasionally accept [wire instructions without independent verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process), particularly when closing with out-of-state title companies that service Alaska transactions remotely. Wire fraud losses in real estate topped $446 million nationally in 2023 according to the FBI's IC3 report. Alaska's geography makes remote closings more common, and that makes wire fraud verification even more critical.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Alaska carry direct responsibility under AS 08.88.091 for the conduct of their licensees. The Alaska Real Estate Commission can sanction a broker for the closing-related errors of an agent operating under their license, even if the broker had no direct involvement.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Escrow agent licensing | Verify AS 06.60 license status for every settlement agent used | Per transaction |
| Form compliance | Confirm only AAR-approved forms or attorney-drafted documents are used | Per transaction |
| TRID timing | Verify Closing Disclosure delivery dates in file | Per transaction |
| Wire fraud protocols | Confirm callback verification of wiring instructions | Per transaction |
| Agent communications | Spot-check emails and texts for unauthorized legal advice | Quarterly |

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-audit are accepting risk they cannot quantify.** A single unauthorized-practice complaint can trigger a Commission investigation under 12 AAC 64.110, which governs disciplinary proceedings. Penalties range from a letter of reprimand to license revocation, and the Commission publishes enforcement actions publicly.

The practical solution is a standardized closing checklist that every agent completes before a transaction reaches the title company. This is not about micromanagement — it is about documentation that protects the brokerage. Many brokerages are now building these [checklists into their transaction workflows](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to reduce reliance on memory alone.

## When an Attorney Actually Makes Sense in Alaska

There are situations where recommending an attorney is not just smart — it borders on mandatory as a practical matter. Any transaction involving ANCSA land, restricted Native allotments, or property held by a Native corporation requires legal review. The federal and state regulatory layers on these properties are dense enough that a title company alone cannot resolve all the issues.

Probate sales, transactions with IRS or state tax liens, and deals involving boundary disputes or adverse possession claims also warrant attorney involvement. Agents who try to push these transactions through a standard title-company closing often find the deal stalled or collapsed when unresolved legal issues surface at the last moment.

**The agent's job is to recognize when the complexity exceeds the title company's scope and make the referral.** This is not the same as giving legal advice. Saying "You should talk to a real estate attorney about this lien" is a referral. Saying "This lien won't be a problem because it expires next year" is a legal opinion. The distinction matters for your license.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of closing-related errors in Alaska fall into three buckets: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

On the regulatory side, the Alaska Real Estate Commission can impose fines, mandate additional continuing education, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely under AS 08.88.071. Violations of the unauthorized practice of law can result in separate proceedings through the Alaska Bar Association and, in extreme cases, criminal misdemeanor charges under AS 08.88.900.

Civil liability is the bigger financial threat. An agent who provides incorrect guidance about a title exception, fails to disclose a known defect, or facilitates an improperly structured closing may face breach-of-duty claims from either party. Alaska follows a comparative fault system under AS 09.17.080, which means the agent's share of liability is proportional to their degree of fault — but even a 20 percent allocation on a $400,000 transaction produces an $80,000 exposure before legal fees.

Transactionally, errors at closing can cause deals to cancel outright. A title commitment with unresolved exceptions, a missed TRID deadline, or an improperly executed deed can delay or kill a closing. Those delays cost everyone money and erode the agent's reputation in a state where the real estate community is small enough that word travels fast.
