Wire fraud cost real estate consumers more than $446 million in 2023 according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and Iowa is not immune. The Iowa wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 standards reflect an aggressive push by both the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) and the Iowa Association of Realtors (IAR) to place affirmative duties on agents before any funds move electronically. If you hold an active Iowa license, the compliance obligations that took shape in 2025 are now fully enforceable—and ignorance is not a defense.

This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, liability exposure, and broker audit responsibilities that govern wire fraud prevention in Iowa residential transactions right now.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind Iowa's Wire Fraud Rules

Iowa does not have a single standalone wire fraud prevention statute the way some states do. Instead, the obligation arises from a combination of Iowa Code Chapter 543B (the Iowa Real Estate License Law), administrative rules in 193E Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 14, and IREC guidance bulletins issued in late 2024 and early 2025. Chapter 543B, Section 543B.34 gives the Commission authority to discipline licensees who fail to disclose material information or who act in a manner harmful to the public interest.

The IREC treats wire fraud risk as a material fact that agents must affirmatively disclose. This interpretation was reinforced in the Commission's 2025 guidance letter to all managing brokers, which stated that failure to provide written wire fraud warnings may constitute a violation of 193E IAC 14.1(1) governing licensee duties.

The Iowa Association of Realtors responded by updating its standard forms package. The key document is IAR Form 185, the Wire Fraud Advisory, which became part of the recommended transaction packet beginning January 2026.

## Iowa Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Specific Forms and Procedures

**IAR Form 185 (Wire Fraud Advisory) is now the standard disclosure document agents must present to buyers, sellers, and any party wiring funds in a transaction.** The form outlines the risks of business email compromise, instructs parties never to rely solely on emailed wiring instructions, and provides a callback verification protocol. Both the client and the licensee sign it.

In addition to Form 185, agents should be aware of IAR Form 107 (Buyer Representation Agreement addendum language) and IAR Form 125 (Purchase Agreement), both of which contain updated wire fraud notification paragraphs as of the 2026 revision cycle. These paragraphs direct parties to independently verify all wiring instructions by calling a known phone number—not a number provided in the email containing the instructions.

| Form | Purpose | When Presented | Signature Required |
|------|---------|----------------|--------------------|
| IAR Form 185 | Wire Fraud Advisory | At first meeting or prior to offer | Both client and agent |
| IAR Form 107 (2026 rev.) | Buyer Rep Agreement wire language | At buyer engagement | Buyer and agent |
| IAR Form 125 (2026 rev.) | Purchase Agreement wire clause | At offer submission | All parties |
| Closing agent verification | Callback confirmation of wire details | 24-48 hours before closing | Title/escrow company |

The procedural expectation is clear: agents must deliver Form 185 early in the relationship, not at closing. Waiting until the closing table defeats the entire purpose of the warning, because by that point, funds may already be in transit.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

**Agents who skip the wire fraud advisory face a three-pronged liability threat: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and E&O coverage gaps.** The IREC can impose sanctions ranging from a formal citation to license suspension under Iowa Code 543B.29. Fines for administrative violations can reach $10,000 per occurrence under 543B.35.

Civil liability is where the real financial pain hits. If a buyer wires $200,000 to a fraudulent account and the agent never warned them about wire fraud schemes or provided a verification protocol, the buyer's attorney will name the agent and brokerage in the lawsuit. Iowa courts apply a standard negligence analysis—duty, breach, causation, damages—and the duty is increasingly easy to establish given the IREC's published expectations.

Then there is the E&O problem. Multiple errors and omissions carriers now include policy language requiring insureds to follow "industry standard" wire fraud prevention protocols. If you cannot produce a signed Form 185 from the transaction file, your carrier may deny coverage on the grounds that you failed to mitigate a known risk. That leaves you personally exposed for the full loss amount.

The transaction itself can also unravel. A buyer who loses earnest money or closing funds to fraud may be unable to perform under the purchase agreement, triggering potential default provisions. The deal dies, commissions evaporate, and the finger-pointing begins.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make on Wire Fraud Compliance

Even agents who know about the requirement make preventable errors. Here are the five most frequent failures showing up in IREC complaint files and brokerage audits.

The first mistake is timing. Agents present Form 185 at closing instead of at the initial client meeting or before the offer is written. The advisory has no protective value if the client has already received and acted on fraudulent wiring instructions days earlier. The form must be delivered early enough that the client internalizes the callback verification habit before any money moves.

The second mistake is relying on verbal warnings without documentation. An agent might tell a buyer to "be careful with wire instructions" during a showing, but without a signed written disclosure, there is no evidence the warning was given. The IREC requires written documentation under 193E IAC 14.1(5), which mandates that licensees maintain records of all material disclosures.

**The third mistake is forwarding wire instructions via email without adding any verification language or disclaimer.** Some agents receive closing instructions from the title company and simply forward them to the buyer. This is extraordinarily dangerous because if the agent's email has been compromised, the forwarded instructions could be altered. Best practice—and increasingly the standard of care—is to never forward wire instructions at all, and instead direct clients to contact the title company directly using a verified phone number.

The fourth mistake involves team structures where the lead agent assumes a transaction coordinator or showing assistant handled the advisory. Delegation does not eliminate liability. The supervising broker and the agent of record remain responsible regardless of who physically handed over the form. For agents working in teams, building [verified wire instruction processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) into your workflow prevents this gap.

The fifth mistake is ignoring post-signing follow-up. Delivering Form 185 once is necessary but not sufficient. Agents should send a reminder communication 48-72 hours before closing reiterating the callback verification protocol. This follow-up is not required by rule, but it dramatically reduces risk and demonstrates a high standard of care if litigation arises.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry supervisory liability under Iowa Code 543B.31, which makes the broker responsible for ensuring all licensees under their supervision comply with Commission rules. A broker who never checks whether agents are using Form 185 is exposed to the same disciplinary and civil consequences as the offending agent.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Evidence Required |
|-------------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Signed Form 185 in every closed file | Per-transaction | Executed copy with date |
| Form 185 presented before offer | Spot-check monthly | Date on form precedes offer date |
| No wire instructions forwarded via agent email | Quarterly email audit | Email server logs or policy acknowledgment |
| Team delegation protocol documented | Annual | Written team procedure manual |
| CE completion on wire fraud topic | Annual | Transcript from approved provider |

Brokers should incorporate wire fraud compliance into their existing [transaction file review systems](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system). A simple date-match audit—comparing the Form 185 execution date against the purchase agreement date—will catch the most common timing violation instantly.

The IREC has signaled through its 2025 enforcement priorities memo that it intends to conduct random brokerage audits focused specifically on consumer protection disclosures, including wire fraud advisories. Brokers who cannot produce signed forms for every closed transaction in the audit period will receive a notice of deficiency at minimum, and repeated failures trigger formal disciplinary proceedings.

Iowa's [continuing education requirements](https://plb.iowa.gov/real-estate) now include approved wire fraud prevention courses that satisfy the mandatory elective hours. Brokers should require all affiliated licensees to complete at least one such course per renewal cycle, even if the specific topic is not technically mandated by rule.

## How Wire Fraud Prevention Intersects With Deal Timelines

Wire fraud attempts spike in the 48 hours before closing because that is when buyers are most likely to receive wiring instructions and act on them quickly under time pressure. Agents who understand [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at the finish line know that a compromised wire can be the ultimate deal-killer—one with no recovery path.

The callback verification protocol in Form 185 requires the buyer to call the title company or closing attorney at a phone number obtained independently—typically from the company's official website or from a business card received in person. The buyer must verbally confirm the routing number, account number, and beneficiary name before initiating the transfer. This takes five minutes and prevents the vast majority of business email compromise attacks.

Agents should build this verification step into their pre-closing communication sequence. A text or phone call to the buyer saying "Before you wire anything, remember to call the title company at the number on their website to confirm" is both good client service and evidence of due diligence.
