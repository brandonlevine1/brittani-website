## North Dakota Building Code Updates 2026: Why Agents Cannot Afford to Ignore Them

The North Dakota building code updates 2026 cycle is forcing real estate agents and brokers to rethink how they evaluate properties, advise clients, and fill out disclosure paperwork. North Dakota's approach to building codes has always been decentralized compared to states with uniform statewide enforcement, and that fragmentation is exactly what makes this round of updates tricky. If you hold an active license through the North Dakota Real Estate Commission, the changes rippling through local jurisdictions right now directly affect your transaction workflow.

North Dakota adopted the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) and the 2021 International Building Code (IBC) as its baseline references, with amendments managed at both the state and municipal level. The state does not mandate a single statewide building code for all jurisdictions — instead, NDCC Chapter 54-21.3 authorizes the Division of Community Services to adopt standards that apply primarily to state-funded construction, while cities and counties retain authority to adopt, amend, or exceed those standards. This patchwork means that a property in Bismarck may be held to different requirements than one ten miles outside city limits.

## How North Dakota Adopts and Amends Building Codes

**The state's code adoption process flows through the North Dakota Division of Community Services, which sits under the Department of Commerce.** Under NDCC 54-21.3-03, this division publishes the state building code that applies to public buildings and state-assisted construction projects. Residential and commercial codes for private construction are largely governed by local ordinances.

Fargo, for instance, enforces a locally amended version of the 2021 IRC with additional energy efficiency requirements that exceed the state baseline. Grand Forks adopted its own set of amendments related to frost depth, insulation, and foundation requirements given the Red River Valley's soil conditions. Agents operating across multiple jurisdictions must verify which code version applies at the property's location — not just the state default.

The 2026 update cycle is notable because several North Dakota cities are now reviewing whether to leapfrog to the [2024 IRC and IBC editions](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/), published by the International Code Council. Minot and West Fargo have both opened public comment periods on potential adoption. If your listing is in one of these municipalities, the code that applied when the home was permitted may differ from the code that governs any current renovation or addition.

## What the Updates Mean for Disclosure Forms and Transaction Paperwork

Every North Dakota residential transaction involves the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement, which is required under NDCC 47-10-02.1. This form asks sellers to identify known material defects, and building code violations fall squarely within that scope. Agents who represent sellers must ensure the disclosure form accurately reflects any known non-compliance with the adopted building code in that jurisdiction.

The North Dakota Association of Realtors publishes standardized forms that most brokerages use, including the Residential Purchase Agreement and the Property Condition Disclosure. While the state does not assign universal "form numbers" the way some states do, the NDAR forms are versioned and dated — agents should confirm they are using the most current edition, which as of mid-2026 reflects updated language around energy code compliance and radon mitigation system requirements.

**Radon disclosure deserves special attention this cycle.** North Dakota ranks among the highest states for radon concentration, and several municipalities have begun requiring radon-resistant new construction (RRNC) features aligned with Appendix F of the IRC. If a property was built or renovated without these features in a jurisdiction that now requires them, the seller needs to disclose that gap. The Property Condition Disclosure Statement includes a section on environmental hazards where radon testing results and mitigation systems should be documented.

| Jurisdiction | Adopted Code Edition | Radon-Resistant Construction Required? | Local Amendments? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fargo | 2021 IRC (amended) | Yes, for new construction | Energy efficiency additions |
| Bismarck | 2021 IRC | Yes, for new construction | Foundation and frost depth |
| Grand Forks | 2021 IRC (amended) | Yes, for new construction | Soil and insulation specs |
| Minot | 2021 IRC (under review for 2024) | Under review | Pending public comment |
| Rural / Unincorporated | Varies (often no local code) | No mandatory requirement | None in many areas |

That table matters because an agent listing a property in an unincorporated area may face no local code enforcement at all, while the same floor plan built inside Fargo city limits is subject to robust inspection requirements. This discrepancy is a liability trap if you don't flag it for buyers.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Building Code Compliance

Agents get into trouble with building codes not because they are expected to be inspectors, but because they overlook obligations that already exist within their licensing framework. Here are the errors that surface most often in disciplinary actions and E&O claims.

**First, agents assume that "grandfathered" means "no problem."** A home built in 1985 does not need to meet 2021 IRC standards in its original construction — but the moment a seller finishes a basement, adds a deck, or replaces the electrical panel, the new work must meet the code in effect at the time of the permit. Agents who list a property with an unpermitted addition and fail to disclose the lack of permits are exposed under both NDCC 47-10-02.1 and the North Dakota Real Estate Commission's licensing rules under NDCC Chapter 43-23.

Second, agents working in rural areas treat the absence of local enforcement as the absence of risk. A buyer who purchases a rural property and later discovers the well, septic, or structural framing does not meet state health department standards or the IRC baseline has grounds to pursue the seller — and potentially the agent — for failure to disclose known conditions. The North Dakota Department of Environmental Quality governs well and septic standards separately from building codes, adding another layer of compliance that agents routinely miss.

Third, agents confuse a home inspection with a code compliance review. A standard home inspection performed by a licensed inspector under NDCC 43-44 evaluates the condition of the property but does not certify code compliance. Agents who tell buyers, "the inspection came back clean, so there are no code issues," are making a representation that exceeds what the inspection report actually covers. The distinction matters when disputes arise post-closing.

Fourth, agents fail to check permit histories before listing. Most North Dakota cities maintain permit records through their planning or building departments. A quick records request can reveal unpermitted work that the seller may not even realize is an issue. Skipping this step is a missed opportunity to protect both the client and yourself. Agents who handle [deadline-heavy transactions without a system](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) to track these verification steps are particularly at risk.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under NDCC 43-23-11.1, which requires them to exercise reasonable supervision over the activities of their licensees. When it comes to building code updates, that obligation translates into concrete audit steps.

Brokers should verify that every listing file includes the current version of the Property Condition Disclosure Statement — not a form from two years ago that omits updated environmental hazard language. Form version control is a low-effort, high-impact audit item. Brokerages that rely on [checklists rather than memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) catch these gaps before they become regulatory problems.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form version | Current NDAR edition with updated hazard sections | Outdated form may omit required disclosures |
| Permit verification on file | Agent confirmed permit history for any additions/renovations | Unpermitted work discovered post-closing triggers liability |
| Jurisdictional code identification | Correct local code edition noted in agent notes | Wrong code reference misleads buyers |
| Radon disclosure completeness | Test results and mitigation system status documented | High-radon state increases E&O exposure |
| Rural property due diligence | Well, septic, and structural standards cross-checked | State health and environmental rules still apply |

**Brokers should also conduct periodic training on the difference between home inspections and code compliance reviews.** If agents in the brokerage are conflating the two in buyer consultations or in writing, the brokerage inherits that risk. A single documented training session per year — with sign-off sheets — goes a long way in defending against claims of inadequate supervision.

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission can take action against a broker's license if a pattern of agent non-compliance suggests a failure of supervision. Penalties range from formal reprimands to license suspension, and the commission publishes disciplinary actions publicly. Beyond regulatory penalties, E&O insurers increasingly scrutinize whether brokerages had written policies addressing code-related disclosures when evaluating claims.

## Liability Consequences When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of ignoring building code obligations are not hypothetical. Under NDCC 43-23-11.1 and the administrative rules enforced by the [North Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://www.realestatend.org/), agents face license discipline for misrepresentation or omission of material facts. Building code violations that a licensee knew about — or should have known about with reasonable diligence — meet that threshold.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. Buyers who discover undisclosed code violations during the inspection period will either renegotiate aggressively or walk away entirely. If the violation surfaces after closing, the buyer's recourse may include a claim against the seller and the listing agent for damages related to the cost of bringing the property into compliance. In North Dakota, where bringing an unpermitted basement finish up to current IRC standards can cost $15,000 to $40,000 depending on scope, those damages are not trivial.

Agents also face fines imposed by the Real Estate Commission, which has authority to levy financial penalties alongside license actions. Repeat offenders or agents whose omissions appear willful rather than negligent face the harshest outcomes. The commission's complaint process is straightforward enough that a frustrated buyer does not need an attorney to initiate it — they just need a form and a documented grievance.
