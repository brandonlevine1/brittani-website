## Understanding North Dakota Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Handling earnest money correctly is one of those tasks that feels routine until it goes wrong. North Dakota earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real consequences for agents and brokers who treat trust fund management as an afterthought. A mishandled deposit can unravel a deal, trigger a complaint to the North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC), and put your license on the line.

North Dakota's statutory framework for earnest money sits primarily within North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 43-23, which governs real estate licensing, brokerage duties, and trust account requirements. The corresponding administrative rules in North Dakota Administrative Code (NDAC) Title 70, Article 02 add specificity to the obligations codified in statute. Agents who operate without a clear understanding of both layers are flying blind.

This post breaks down the deposit timelines, trust account mandates, dispute protocols, and liability traps that apply to every residential transaction in the state. If you hold a North Dakota license, this is the rulebook you are measured against.

## Statutory Foundation: NDCC 43-23-11.1 and NDAC 70-02-03

**The core statute governing trust funds is NDCC 43-23-11.1, which requires every broker to maintain a separate trust or escrow account at a federally insured financial institution within North Dakota.** Earnest money, security deposits, and any other funds received on behalf of a client must go into that account. The statute is explicit: a broker may not commingle client funds with personal or operating funds.

NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 further clarifies the operational rules. A broker's trust account must be designated as such on the bank records, and the broker must keep detailed records that reconcile to the penny. The [NDREC](https://www.realestatend.org/) audits trust accounts both on schedule and in response to complaints, and discrepancies are treated seriously.

There is a narrow exception: a broker may maintain a small amount of personal funds in the trust account—generally up to $500—solely to cover bank service fees and prevent the account from being closed. Anything beyond that threshold starts looking like commingling.

## Deposit Deadlines and the Three-Business-Day Rule

One of the most frequent compliance questions involves timing. North Dakota requires earnest money to be deposited into the broker's trust account within three business days of receipt, unless the purchase agreement specifies otherwise. This three-business-day window is not a suggestion; it is an enforceable rule under NDAC 70-02-03-01.1.

**Agents who hold a check in their desk drawer over a long weekend or wait until "after the inspection" are violating the rule.** The clock starts when the licensee physically receives the funds, not when the offer is accepted. If a buyer hands an agent a check on Friday afternoon, that check must hit the trust account by the close of business the following Wednesday at the latest.

Checks that are postdated or accompanied by written instructions from the parties to delay deposit are treated differently. In those cases, the agent should document the instruction in writing and keep the check in a secure location until the agreed-upon deposit date. The key is written authorization—verbal agreements offer no protection during an NDREC audit.

| Scenario | Deposit Deadline | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Standard earnest money check | 3 business days from receipt | Purchase agreement, trust ledger entry |
| Postdated check with written instruction | Date specified in writing | Written delay authorization from both parties |
| Wire transfer from buyer | 3 business days from receipt into brokerage account | Wire confirmation, trust ledger entry |
| Cash earnest money | 3 business days from receipt | Signed receipt to buyer, trust ledger entry |

Cash deposits deserve special attention. While not common in North Dakota residential transactions, they happen. Always issue a written receipt immediately. A missing receipt for a cash deposit is one of the fastest ways to trigger a complaint.

## Forms Every North Dakota Agent Must Know

North Dakota does not mandate a single statewide purchase agreement form in the way some other states do, but the North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR) publishes standard forms that most agents rely on. The most relevant forms for earnest money and escrow include the following:

The NDAR Purchase Agreement (Form 1) contains the standard earnest money provisions, including the deposit amount, who holds the funds, and the conditions under which the deposit is refundable. Agents must fill in every blank. Leaving the escrow holder line empty creates ambiguity that invites disputes later.

The NDAR Addendum forms—including the Financing Addendum (Form 3) and the Inspection Addendum (Form 5)—contain contingency deadlines that directly affect whether earnest money becomes refundable or nonrefundable. Missing a contingency deadline on one of these forms can mean the difference between a buyer getting their deposit back and forfeiting it entirely. This is [where many deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down), and it often traces back to a missed calendar date.

The Earnest Money Disbursement Agreement is the form used when a transaction fails and both parties need to agree on who receives the deposit. Without signed authorization from both buyer and seller, the broker cannot release the funds. Period.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The NDREC has broad enforcement authority under NDCC 43-23-08 and can impose penalties ranging from a formal reprimand to license revocation. Trust account violations are among the most common grounds for disciplinary action in North Dakota.

**A first offense for a deposit-timing violation will typically result in a formal warning or a fine, but repeat offenses escalate quickly.** The commission has suspended licenses for chronic commingling, failure to maintain trust account records, and unauthorized disbursement of earnest money. These enforcement actions are public and appear on the licensee's record in the NDREC's online database.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money was mishandled can sue the agent, the broker, and the brokerage for breach of fiduciary duty. Brokers can be held vicariously liable for their agents' trust account errors even if the broker had no direct knowledge of the violation—a point many managing brokers underestimate. If you are [missing deadlines on trust fund deposits](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the exposure is not theoretical.

There is also the deal itself. A seller who learns that earnest money was never deposited—or was deposited late—may have grounds to cancel the purchase agreement. Courts in North Dakota have held that timely deposit of earnest money is a material term of the contract, and failure to perform that term can constitute a breach.

## Five Common Mistakes North Dakota Agents Make

**First, agents routinely deposit earnest money into the wrong account.** New agents sometimes deposit a buyer's check into the brokerage's operating account rather than the designated trust account. This is commingling, even if accidental, and it triggers the same NDREC scrutiny as intentional misuse.

Second, agents fail to reconcile trust account records monthly. NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 requires brokers to reconcile their trust account ledger against bank statements at least monthly. Agents who assist with bookkeeping but lack training in trust accounting create errors that compound over time, leaving the broker exposed during audits.

Third, agents release earnest money based on a phone call rather than a signed disbursement form. A buyer's agent who calls and says "go ahead and release the deposit" is not giving valid authorization. The broker needs written, signed instructions from all parties to the transaction before moving a dollar.

Fourth, agents do not keep copies of earnest money checks before depositing them. The NDREC expects a photocopy or digital image of every check deposited into the trust account. If the check clears and the bank image is later unavailable, the brokerage has a documentation gap.

Fifth, agents forget to account for earnest money held by third parties. When a title company or attorney holds the deposit instead of the listing broker, the agent still needs to confirm that the deposit was actually received and document that confirmation in the transaction file. The responsibility to verify does not vanish just because someone else is holding the money. This is one of many areas [where checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) every time.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in North Dakota carry primary responsibility for trust account compliance under NDCC 43-23-11.1. The commission does not accept "I didn't know my agent did that" as a defense. Brokers must build systems that prevent violations before they happen.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | Ledger balance matches bank statement to the penny |
| Deposit timing verification | Every transaction | Earnest money deposited within 3 business days |
| Disbursement authorization | Every transaction | Signed forms from all parties before any release |
| Agent-held funds | Weekly spot checks | No agent is holding checks, cash, or wire confirmations past the deposit window |
| Record retention | Annually | All trust account records retained for a minimum of 3 years per NDAC requirements |

Brokers should conduct random audits of transaction files at least quarterly. Pull five to ten closed files, verify the deposit date against the contract execution date, and confirm that a copy of the earnest money instrument is in the file. A fifteen-minute spot check per file can prevent a six-month NDREC investigation.

Brokers also need a written trust account policy that every agent in the office signs. This policy should specify who is authorized to deposit funds, who is authorized to disburse, and the exact procedure for handling disputed earnest money. A signed acknowledgment from each agent creates a paper trail that protects the broker if an individual licensee goes off-script.

Training matters, too. Hosting a quarterly refresher on trust fund handling—especially for agents who joined the brokerage within the past twelve months—is one of the lowest-cost, highest-return compliance investments a broker can make.

## Disputed Earnest Money: The Interpleader Option

When a transaction falls apart and both buyer and seller claim the earnest money, the broker is caught in the middle. North Dakota law does not allow the broker to pick a side. The funds stay in the trust account until the parties reach a written agreement or a court orders disbursement.

If the dispute drags on, the broker's best option is to file an interpleader action in North Dakota district court. An interpleader deposits the disputed funds with the court and asks the judge to determine who gets the money. Once the funds are interpleaded, the broker is released from liability for the disbursement decision. Filing fees and attorney costs for interpleader actions in North Dakota are modest relative to the risk of making the wrong call.

Brokers should not wait indefinitely before interpleading. Holding disputed funds for months without action can draw NDREC scrutiny, and it ties up trust account records. A reasonable timeline is 30 to 60 days of good-faith attempts to get a mutual release signed before moving to interpleader.
