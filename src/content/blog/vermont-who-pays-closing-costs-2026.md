The question of Vermont who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 sounds simple until you are sitting across from clients who assume the other party covers everything. Vermont's attorney-state closing model, its property transfer tax structure, and the specific forms agents must use all create a cost-allocation landscape that differs meaningfully from neighboring New Hampshire and New York. Agents who misstate these responsibilities risk more than a confused client — they risk disciplinary action, deal collapse, and personal liability.

This post breaks down the statutory framework, the exact dollar responsibilities that fall on each side of the transaction, and the errors that keep showing up in Vermont Office of Professional Regulation complaint files.

## How Vermont Law Assigns Closing Costs

Vermont is one of a handful of states where attorneys conduct closings rather than title companies alone. The Vermont Supreme Court's longstanding interpretation of the unauthorized practice of law means that a licensed attorney must oversee deed preparation, title examination, and closing document execution. That attorney's fee is a closing cost someone has to pay.

The two primary statutes governing cost allocation in Vermont real estate transactions are 32 V.S.A. § 9602 (property transfer tax) and 27 V.S.A. § 341 (recording requirements). The transfer tax statute assigns the obligation to the purchaser by default, while recording fees follow the party whose documents are being recorded. Neither statute prevents contractual reallocation — meaning agents can negotiate who pays what, as long as the purchase and sale agreement spells it out clearly.

**Vermont does not have a single statutory provision that locks every closing cost to one party.** The allocation is primarily governed by contract, with a few statutory defaults that kick in when the contract is silent. This is precisely why the purchase and sale agreement language matters so much.

## The Standard Cost Split: Buyer vs. Seller Breakdown

While every deal can be customized, Vermont market practice has created a fairly predictable baseline. The table below reflects the customary allocation agents encounter in the majority of 2026 residential transactions.

| Closing Cost Item | Customarily Paid By | Statutory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Property Transfer Tax (1.45% / 0.50% on first $100K for primary residence) | Buyer | 32 V.S.A. § 9602 |
| Deed Preparation | Seller | Custom (attorney fee) |
| Title Search and Title Insurance (owner's policy) | Buyer | Custom |
| Lender's Title Insurance Policy | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Recording Fees — Deed | Buyer | 27 V.S.A. § 341 |
| Recording Fees — Mortgage | Buyer | 27 V.S.A. § 341 |
| Seller's Attorney Fee | Seller | Custom |
| Buyer's Attorney Fee | Buyer | Custom |
| Real Estate Commission | Seller (per listing agreement) | Contract |
| Property Tax Proration | Both (adjusted at closing) | Contract |

Agents should note that Vermont's property transfer tax has a split-rate structure. The standard rate is 1.45% of the purchase price, but if the buyer will occupy the property as a principal residence, the first $100,000 of value is taxed at just 0.50%. The [Vermont Department of Taxes](https://tax.vermont.gov/property/transfer-tax) administers the Property Transfer Tax Return (Form PT-172), which must be filed within 30 days of the transfer.

## Forms and Documents Vermont Agents Must Know

The Vermont Real Estate Commission, housed within the Office of Professional Regulation (OPR), does not mandate a single statewide purchase and sale agreement form the way some states do. Most agents in Vermont use forms published by the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR), including the VAR Purchase and Sale Contract, which contains specific provisions for closing cost allocation in its financing and settlement sections.

**Agents must also ensure the Property Transfer Tax Return (Form PT-172) is completed accurately, because errors on this form can trigger tax penalties that land on the buyer — and blowback on the agent who provided incorrect figures.** The PT-172 requires disclosure of the sale price, the allocation of the transfer tax, and whether the property qualifies for the reduced primary residence rate.

Beyond the PT-172, agents should be familiar with the Vermont Lead Paint Disclosure form (required for pre-1978 properties under both federal law and Vermont's own 18 V.S.A. § 1759), the Agency Disclosure form required by the Vermont Real Estate Commission under 26 V.S.A. § 2297, and the Seller's Property Disclosure form. Each of these forms can surface issues that affect closing costs — a lead paint issue can trigger remediation credits, and an agency disclosure failure can unwind a deal entirely.

The way [deals tend to break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often involves form errors or missing disclosures rather than dramatic negotiation failures. Vermont's attorney-driven closing process catches many of these problems, but by the time an attorney flags a form deficiency at the closing table, the damage to timelines and client relationships is already done.

## Vermont's Property Transfer Tax: The Cost That Catches Buyers Off Guard

No single closing cost generates more confusion in Vermont transactions than the property transfer tax. At 1.45% of the purchase price — or a blended rate for qualifying primary residences — this tax represents a material expense that buyers sometimes fail to budget for.

Here is how the math works on a $400,000 purchase with a primary residence qualifying buyer. The first $100,000 is taxed at 0.50%, producing $500. The remaining $300,000 is taxed at 1.45%, producing $4,350. The total transfer tax is $4,850. On that same $400,000 sale, a non-primary-residence buyer (an investor or second-home purchaser) would owe $5,800 — the full 1.45% on the entire price.

| Scenario | Sale Price | Rate on First $100K | Rate on Balance | Total Transfer Tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary Residence Buyer | $400,000 | 0.50% ($500) | 1.45% ($4,350) | $4,850 |
| Non-Primary Residence Buyer | $400,000 | 1.45% ($1,450) | 1.45% ($4,350) | $5,800 |
| Primary Residence Buyer | $275,000 | 0.50% ($500) | 1.45% ($2,537.50) | $3,037.50 |
| Non-Primary Residence Buyer | $275,000 | 1.45% ($1,450) | 1.45% ($2,537.50) | $3,987.50 |

Agents representing buyers need to surface this number early — ideally during the first consultation and certainly before an offer is submitted. Vermont buyer agents who wait until the Closing Disclosure to mention a $5,000+ tax bill are setting up a trust-destroying moment that can kill referrals.

## Seller Concessions and Lender Limits

Vermont law does not cap the amount a seller can contribute toward a buyer's closing costs. The ceiling comes from the buyer's lender. FHA loans cap seller concessions at 6% of the sale price. Conventional loans set the limit between 3% and 9% depending on the buyer's down payment amount. VA loans permit up to 4% in seller concessions.

**Agents listing properties should understand that advertising "seller will pay all closing costs" without specifying a dollar cap or percentage limit creates exposure.** If the buyer's lender restricts the concession, the seller may be contractually obligated to contribute more than the lender allows, which can force a last-minute contract amendment or kill the deal.

The smarter approach is to write seller concessions as a specific dollar amount or a capped percentage in the VAR Purchase and Sale Contract. This keeps the language clean for underwriting and avoids surprises. Agents on [both sides of the transaction](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) need to coordinate with the buyer's loan officer before finalizing concession language.

## Five Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make on Closing Costs

The Vermont Office of Professional Regulation's disciplinary records and common attorney complaints reveal a pattern of repeated errors by agents handling closing cost discussions.

**First, agents misrepresent the property transfer tax as a seller obligation.** This happens frequently with agents who work across state lines, particularly those also licensed in New Hampshire (which has no broad-based transfer tax at the state level but imposes a real estate transfer tax split equally between buyer and seller). In Vermont, the statutory default places the tax on the buyer. Telling a buyer otherwise is a material misrepresentation under 26 V.S.A. § 2296(a)(1).

Second, agents fail to account for the primary residence rate differential when estimating buyer costs. Quoting the full 1.45% rate to a first-time homebuyer who will occupy the property inflates the estimate by hundreds or thousands of dollars. This is not just a math error — it can cause a buyer to walk away from a home they could actually afford.

Third, listing agents neglect to disclose potential closing cost obligations in the listing agreement itself. Vermont listing agreements should specify whether the seller will entertain concession requests and set a cap. Agents who leave this ambiguous create negotiation chaos once offers arrive.

Fourth, agents on both sides ignore the recording fee structure. Vermont recording fees are set by the individual town clerk's office, and they vary. A deed recording in Burlington may cost a different amount than in Stowe. Agents who use a generic estimate without checking the specific town's fee schedule can be off by enough to matter at the closing table.

Fifth, agents submit incomplete or inaccurate PT-172 forms, triggering processing delays at the town clerk's office. The Vermont Department of Taxes can assess penalties for late or incorrect filings, and those penalties fall on the buyer — who then blames their agent.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Vermont carry direct responsibility under 26 V.S.A. § 2299 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. The OPR has made clear in past enforcement actions that a broker who fails to implement reasonable supervisory procedures can be held liable for an agent's closing cost misrepresentations.

Brokers should audit three things on every transaction file. First, confirm that the purchase and sale agreement contains explicit closing cost allocation language — not boilerplate, but deal-specific terms that match what was negotiated. Second, verify that any seller concession is expressed as a specific dollar amount or capped percentage and that it falls within the buyer's lender limits. Third, check that the PT-172 matches the contract terms and that the correct transfer tax rate (primary residence vs. non-primary residence) has been applied.

Beyond file audits, brokers should require agents to provide buyers with a written closing cost estimate — separate from the lender's Loan Estimate — within 48 hours of an accepted offer. This is not a statutory requirement, but it is a best practice that dramatically reduces complaints. The estimate should itemize buyer-side costs including the transfer tax, recording fees, attorney fees, title insurance, and any lender-required items.
