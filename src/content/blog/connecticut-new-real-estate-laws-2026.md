## Connecticut New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutes That Changed Everything This Year

The phrase Connecticut new real estate laws 2026 is not just a search term agents are typing out of curiosity—it reflects a real shift in how transactions must be documented, disclosed, and supervised statewide. Between the legislative session that wrapped in June 2025 and regulatory guidance issued by the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection (DCP) in early 2026, agents are operating under a materially different compliance framework than they were twelve months ago. Missing even one of these changes can kill a deal or trigger a license investigation.

This article breaks down the specific statutes, form numbers, penalties, and operational adjustments that matter to working agents and their brokers. If you closed transactions in 2025 without incident, that muscle memory may now be a liability.

## Mandatory Buyer Agency Agreements Under Public Act 24-104

Connecticut formally codified the buyer representation agreement requirement through Public Act 24-104, effective January 1, 2026. Section 3 of the act requires that a written buyer agency agreement be executed before an agent provides "substantive real estate services," which the statute defines to include property showings, market analyses prepared for a specific buyer, and negotiation discussions.

The approved form is the CT-BAA (Connecticut Buyer Agency Agreement), published by the Connecticut Association of Realtors in coordination with DCP. Agents must use this form or a brokerage-specific equivalent that contains all mandatory disclosures outlined in Section 3(b) of the act, including the compensation structure, the duration of the agreement, and the buyer's right to terminate.

**Failure to secure a signed CT-BAA before showing property creates immediate regulatory exposure.** DCP has stated in its March 2026 guidance bulletin that agents operating without a signed agreement may face fines of up to $25,000 per occurrence under CGS Section 20-312, plus potential license suspension. The buyer also gains a right to void any subsequent purchase agreement if the CT-BAA was not in place before services began.

## Updated Residential Disclosure Requirements and Form RCRD

Connecticut General Statutes Section 20-327b has governed seller disclosures for decades, but the 2026 amendments under Public Act 25-38 expanded the scope of what must be disclosed. Sellers must now affirmatively report known deficiencies in electrical panels manufactured before 1990, the presence of private well water testing results from the prior five years, and any insurance claims related to water intrusion filed since 2020.

The updated Residential Condition Report Disclosure (Form RCRD, revised January 2026) includes three new line items addressing these categories. Agents representing sellers must ensure the revised form—not the legacy 2023 version—is used for all listings entered into the MLS after February 1, 2026.

Buyers who do not receive Form RCRD prior to signing the purchase contract retain a statutory three-business-day rescission right under CGS Section 20-327b(c). The rescission right is absolute and does not require the buyer to demonstrate harm. For agents, this means a deal you thought was locked can evaporate on day two if your seller's disclosure was late or missing.

## Wire Fraud Advisory: The CT-WFA Form Requirement

Public Act 25-61 created a new mandatory disclosure specific to wire fraud. Connecticut is now among approximately a dozen states that require agents to provide a standalone written advisory about wire fraud risks at or before contract execution. The form is designated CT-WFA, and it must bear the signatures of all parties to the transaction.

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Form designation | CT-WFA (Wire Fraud Advisory) |
| Effective date | January 1, 2026 |
| Timing | At or before contract signing |
| Signatures required | All buyers, all sellers, all agents |
| Statute | Public Act 25-61, Section 2 |
| Penalty for noncompliance | $5,000 fine per transaction; potential DCP complaint |

The CT-WFA is not optional, even if your brokerage already includes wire fraud language in its contract addenda. DCP's position, stated in its [FAQ on new statutory disclosures](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/Real-Estate/Real-Estate-Commission), is that embedded contract language does not satisfy the standalone disclosure requirement. Agents familiar with [wire fraud verification processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) will recognize this as a formalization of what was previously a best practice.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

Five months into the new regulatory year, DCP complaint data and brokerage audit reports are revealing patterns of noncompliance. Here are the errors showing up most frequently.

**Using the wrong version of Form RCRD is the single most common mistake in Connecticut transactions right now.** Many agents are pulling saved PDFs from their document libraries without verifying the revision date. The 2023 RCRD is missing the three new disclosure categories, and using it means the seller has not made a legally sufficient disclosure. This triggers the buyer's rescission right and can expose the listing agent to a negligence claim.

Second, agents are presenting the CT-BAA at the wrong time. The statute requires execution before substantive services begin. Agents who show three properties and then ask for a signature at the fourth showing are already in violation. DCP considers a single showing to constitute substantive services.

Third, some agents are having buyers sign the CT-WFA electronically after contract execution, sometimes days later, as a "cleanup" task. The statute says at or before contract signing. A post-execution signature does not satisfy the timing requirement, and DCP has indicated it will not accept backdated forms as evidence of compliance.

Fourth, dual agency disclosures under CGS Section 20-325a are being conflated with the new buyer agency agreement. They are separate obligations. An agent who discloses dual agency but fails to have a pre-existing CT-BAA with the buyer has satisfied one requirement while violating another.

Fifth, agents are omitting the agreement duration from the CT-BAA or inserting open-ended terms. Public Act 24-104 Section 3(b)(4) requires a specific expiration date. "Until buyer purchases a property" is not a compliant duration.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

The DCP holds designated brokers directly responsible for supervisory compliance under CGS Section 20-312. A broker who fails to audit agent files for the new 2026 requirements is personally exposed to administrative penalties, regardless of whether the broker was involved in the transaction.

| Audit Item | Form/Requirement | Audit Frequency | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buyer agency agreement | CT-BAA (signed, dated, with expiration) | Every buyer-side file | $25,000 fine; deal rescission |
| Seller disclosure | Form RCRD (2026 revision) | Every listing file | Buyer rescission; E&O claim |
| Wire fraud advisory | CT-WFA (signed by all parties) | Every transaction file | $5,000 fine per transaction |
| Dual agency disclosure | CGS 20-325a form | Every dual agency file | License suspension |
| Agreement duration | Specific date in CT-BAA | Every buyer-side file | DCP complaint; noncompliant form |

Brokers should build a quarterly file review schedule that checks for these five items. Monthly is better during 2026 as agents adjust to new habits. Any file missing the CT-WFA or containing an outdated RCRD should trigger immediate corrective action, including contacting the other party's agent to remedy the gap before closing.

**Brokerage managers who rely solely on agents to self-report compliance are already behind.** The DCP's 2026 enforcement posture has shifted from complaint-driven to audit-driven, meaning routine office inspections may surface violations before any consumer files a complaint. Brokers operating [teams with multiple agents](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) face compounded risk because a single supervisory failure multiplies across dozens of files.

## Compensation Transparency Rules Under Section 5 of PA 24-104

Section 5 of Public Act 24-104 introduced new requirements around how compensation is disclosed and documented. Agents may no longer reference MLS-published co-op compensation in buyer-facing documents. Instead, all buyer-agent compensation must be documented exclusively within the CT-BAA or a separate compensation agreement signed by the buyer.

This means the purchase contract cannot contain a blanket reference to "compensation as published in the MLS." If the seller is offering to contribute to buyer-agent compensation, that contribution must flow through a separate written agreement between the listing broker and the buyer's broker, with the buyer's acknowledgment documented on the CT-BAA addendum (Form CT-BAA-A).

Agents who attempt to include compensation terms in the offer itself risk having the entire offer deemed noncompliant. Several Connecticut attorneys have already rejected purchase contracts at review because compensation language violated the new segregation requirement.

## Timelines and Deadlines That Changed

The legislative changes did not only add new forms—they also tightened existing timelines. Under the amended CGS Section 20-327b, the seller must provide the updated RCRD to the buyer no later than three calendar days before the contract signing date. Previously, the statute allowed delivery "before or at" contract execution. This three-day advance requirement gives buyers time to review disclosures without pressure.

The inspection contingency default period under the standard Connecticut purchase contract (Form CT-RPC, revised March 2026) also changed. The default is now 12 calendar days, reduced from the prior 14-day standard. Agents who use older contract forms with a 14-day default are not technically in violation, but they are creating confusion when paired with the new RCRD timing rules.
