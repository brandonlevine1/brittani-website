# Nebraska HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Deliver Before Closing

Understanding **Nebraska HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026** is not optional if you hold an active license in this state. The Nebraska Real Estate Commission expects every licensee—and their supervising broker—to ensure HOA and condominium resale documents reach buyers on time, in full, and with the correct statutory content. Fall short, and you open the door to rescission rights, commission clawbacks, and potential disciplinary action.

Nebraska's disclosure framework for condominiums sits primarily in the Nebraska Condominium Act (Neb. Rev. Stat. sections 76-825 through 76-894). Planned communities and standard homeowners associations that do not qualify as condominiums are governed by association-specific covenants recorded against the property, plus general contract law principles that still impose disclosure duties on agents. The distinction matters more than most agents realize.

## The Statutes That Control Condo Resale Disclosures

**Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-872 is the single most important section for agents listing or selling a condominium unit.** It requires the seller (referred to in the statute as the "unit owner") to furnish a resale certificate to the purchaser before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed. The certificate must be prepared by the association's management or board.

The resale certificate must contain specific data points defined by statute: monthly common expense assessments, any unpaid assessments or fees currently due from the unit, the association's current operating budget, the amount in reserve funds, any pending capital expenditures exceeding a threshold set by the declaration, any pending lawsuits involving the association, and a statement of insurance coverage. Missing even one of these elements can render the certificate legally incomplete.

Nebraska does not prescribe a single state-issued form for the resale certificate. Instead, the content requirements are codified, and most management companies generate their own template. Agents should verify completeness against the 76-872 checklist rather than assuming the management company's output is sufficient.

## Non-Condo HOAs: Where Nebraska's Rules Get Thinner

For properties in planned communities that are not condominiums—think single-family subdivisions governed by a homeowners association—Nebraska does not impose the same statutory resale certificate requirement. There is no mirror statute to 76-872 for these communities. That gap catches agents off guard.

**The absence of a statutory mandate does not eliminate your disclosure duty.** Nebraska License Act provisions (Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2417 and 76-2421) require licensees to disclose material facts that affect the value or desirability of a property. Restrictive covenants, assessment obligations, transfer fees, and pending special assessments all qualify as material facts.

The Nebraska REALTORS Association offers a Seller Property Condition Disclosure Statement (Form 56) that includes a section on HOA membership, monthly dues, special assessments, and pending litigation. If your seller checks "yes" to HOA membership but leaves the financial fields blank, you have an affirmative obligation to follow up. Leaving blanks on Form 56 is not a defense—it is evidence of negligence.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

The table below compares the documentation requirements for condominiums versus non-condo HOA properties in Nebraska.

| Requirement | Condominium (76-872) | Non-Condo HOA |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate | Statutorily required | Not required by state law, but recommended |
| Governing documents delivery | Required by contract (standard practice) | Required by contract (standard practice) |
| Current budget and reserves | Must appear in resale certificate | Agent should request from HOA |
| Pending litigation disclosure | Must appear in resale certificate | Material fact—agent must disclose if known |
| Cost responsibility | Seller unless contract states otherwise | Negotiable per purchase agreement |
| Buyer cancellation right | Yes (statutory) | Only if contract provides one |

The Nebraska REALTORS Association Purchase Agreement (Form 45) includes an HOA addendum provision where parties can specify the timeline for document delivery and the buyer's review period. Agents using Form 45 should complete this section even if the property is in a non-condo HOA. Leaving the addendum blank signals to the Commission that you did not address a known material condition.

## Nebraska HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Liability When You Miss the Mark

Buyers who never receive a resale certificate for a condominium unit can void the purchase agreement under 76-872. The statute is clear: the contract is voidable at the buyer's discretion until the certificate is delivered and the review period expires. That means a buyer who discovers a massive pending special assessment at the closing table can walk away—and the listing agent's failure to procure the certificate on time may expose them to a claim from their own client.

Beyond rescission, the Nebraska Real Estate Commission has authority under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2435 to impose discipline for failure to disclose material facts or for negligent conduct. Penalties range from a formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation. Fines can reach $10,000 per violation in egregious cases.

Civil liability adds another layer. A buyer who closes without proper disclosures and later discovers undisclosed assessments or litigation can bring a claim for damages against the seller and potentially the listing agent. Courts in Nebraska have recognized that agents who knew or should have known about HOA financial problems owe a duty to disclose them, even when acting solely for the seller.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA Disclosures

**Mistake one: assuming the title company will handle everything.** Title companies in Nebraska will pull lien letters and payoff statements, but they do not typically verify that a resale certificate meets all 76-872 content requirements. That responsibility sits squarely with the listing agent and seller.

Mistake two: ordering the resale certificate too late. Many Nebraska HOA management companies require 10 to 15 business days to prepare a resale certificate. If you wait until the buyer is under contract with a 30-day close, you have already consumed half your timeline before the certificate arrives. Order it the day the listing goes active or, at minimum, the day you receive an offer.

Mistake three: failing to distinguish between condominiums and planned communities. An agent who delivers a resale certificate for a property that is technically a planned community—not a condominium—may give the buyer a false sense of statutory cancellation rights that do not exist. Conversely, an agent who skips the resale certificate for what is actually a condominium (some townhome communities are declared as condominiums even though they look like detached homes) violates 76-872. Pull the declaration from the county recorder's office to confirm the legal structure.

Mistake four: not reviewing the resale certificate for red flags before delivery. If the certificate reveals that reserves are funded at 12 percent, that a roof replacement assessment is pending, or that the association is party to litigation, you need to discuss these facts with your client before the buyer reads them. Being caught off guard by your own disclosure document destroys client confidence.

Mistake five: ignoring transfer fees and working capital contributions. Many Nebraska associations charge a transfer fee or require a working capital contribution from incoming buyers. These fees appear on the closing disclosure and can range from $200 to several thousand dollars. If the purchase agreement does not specify who pays, you will have a dispute at the closing table. Address it in the offer.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers carry personal liability under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. A broker who allows agents to close transactions without proper HOA documentation risks their own license.

| Audit Point | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate on file | Every condo transaction | Certificate present, dated, complete per 76-872 |
| Form 56 HOA section | Every HOA transaction | No blank fields; follow-up documented |
| Purchase agreement addendum | Every HOA/condo transaction | Review period and cost allocation specified |
| Timeline compliance | Weekly pipeline review | Certificate ordered within 3 days of contract |
| Red flag escalation | As needed | Agent flagged material issues to client in writing |

Brokers should build a [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that includes a checkbox for HOA document status. A transaction file missing the resale certificate at the 10-day mark should trigger an automatic escalation. Waiting until the closing coordinator asks for it is too late.

The Nebraska Real Estate Commission conducts audits and reviews complaint files regularly. A pattern of missing HOA disclosures across multiple transactions will draw scrutiny not just to the individual agent but to the brokerage's supervisory systems. Document your processes, train your agents annually, and spot-check files quarterly.

## Practical Workflow for Listing Agents

Start by confirming whether the property is a condominium or a planned community. Pull the recorded declaration from the Register of Deeds. If the word "condominium" appears in the declaration and the property was created under the Nebraska Condominium Act, the 76-872 resale certificate requirement applies.

Next, identify the management company or HOA board contact. Request the resale certificate at listing rather than waiting for a contract. Some management companies maintain standing certificates that are valid for 30 or 60 days. Even if the information needs refreshing at contract execution, having a preliminary version lets you identify and address red flags before marketing the property.

When the buyer's agent presents an offer, confirm that the purchase agreement includes HOA addendum language specifying the delivery deadline, the review period, and cost allocation. If your MLS forms are missing this language, draft an addendum. The NRA standard forms provide this structure, but not every brokerage uses NRA forms exclusively.

Agents [who struggle with deadline tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) across multiple transactions risk letting the resale certificate delivery window lapse silently. Automated compliance tracking—where a system flags approaching deadlines and missing documents—eliminates that risk without adding manual work to your week.

## How Buyer's Agents Should Protect Their Clients

Buyer's agents should never rely solely on the seller's disclosure. Request the full governing documents package: declaration, bylaws, articles of incorporation, current rules and regulations, most recent financial statement, reserve study (if one exists), and board meeting minutes from the past 12 months. The resale certificate alone does not tell the full story.

Review the reserve study critically. Nebraska does not mandate a minimum reserve funding level, so associations with dangerously low reserves face no state penalty—but your buyer faces a future special assessment. Flag any reserve funding below 50 percent in writing to your client.

Confirm whether the association has any pending or threatened litigation. The resale certificate should disclose this, but boards sometimes minimize or omit threats that have not yet been formally filed. A quick call to the management company with a direct question—"Are there any disputes, demand letters, or threatened actions?"—can surface issues the certificate does not.
