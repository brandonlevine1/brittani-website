## Understanding Idaho Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

Wire fraud targeting real estate closings has exploded across Idaho over the past three years, with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reporting over $145 million in real estate wire fraud losses nationally in 2025 alone. The Idaho wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 affect every licensed agent and broker handling transactions where funds move electronically. Ignoring these requirements does not just expose clients to catastrophic financial loss—it puts your license and your brokerage on the line.

The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) has steadily tightened expectations around wire fraud disclosures since 2022, building on Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20, which governs licensee conduct and fiduciary duties. The standard forms published by the Idaho Association of REALTORS now include specific wire fraud advisories that must be executed alongside purchase agreements. If you have not updated your transaction workflow to reflect these changes, you are already behind.

## The Forms You Must Know: RE-26 and Related Disclosures

**The RE-26 Wire Fraud Advisory is the primary form agents must deliver to buyers and sellers in every Idaho transaction involving electronic fund transfers.** This form, published by the Idaho Association of REALTORS, warns parties that criminals routinely intercept emails to redirect wire transfers to fraudulent accounts. It also instructs clients to independently verify all wiring instructions by calling a known, verified phone number before sending any funds.

Beyond the RE-26, agents should be familiar with the RE-25 Disclosure of Brokerage Roles, which establishes the scope of an agent's duties—including the duty to inform clients of known risks. When paired together, these forms create a documented trail showing the agent met their advisory obligations. The RE-26 should be signed at or before the time the purchase agreement is executed, not days before closing when it is often too late.

Idaho's standard Purchase and Sale Agreement (RE-21) also contains language referencing the wire fraud advisory. Agents who skip the RE-26 but rely solely on the boilerplate language in the RE-21 are taking a risk. IREC has signaled through enforcement guidance that standalone wire fraud advisories are the expected standard of practice.

## What Idaho Code Actually Requires of Licensees

Idaho Code [54-2060](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title54/T54CH20/) outlines the grounds for disciplinary action against licensees, including "failing to disclose to all parties a material fact" and "failing to account for or remit funds." While the statute does not contain a standalone wire fraud section, IREC interprets these existing provisions to encompass wire fraud advisory obligations. An agent who knows wire fraud is a pervasive risk and fails to warn clients is arguably withholding a material fact about the transaction process.

IREC's administrative rules under IDAPA 33.01.01 further define the standard of competence expected of licensees. Rule 100 requires agents to exercise "reasonable skill and care," which in 2026 unambiguously includes warning clients about wire fraud schemes. The Commission has not needed new legislation to enforce this standard—it applies existing authority to modern threats.

| Requirement | Statute/Rule | Form | When to Deliver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wire Fraud Advisory | Idaho Code 54-2060, IDAPA 33.01.01.100 | RE-26 | At or before purchase agreement execution |
| Brokerage Role Disclosure | Idaho Code 54-2085 | RE-25 | First substantive contact |
| Purchase Agreement Wire Language | Idaho Code 54-2060 | RE-21 (embedded) | With purchase agreement |
| Escrow Instructions Verification | Title company policy + IREC guidance | Varies by title company | Before wiring funds |

## Liability When Things Go Wrong

**If a buyer wires $350,000 to a fraudulent account and you never delivered the RE-26, your errors-and-omissions carrier will scrutinize the claim before paying—if they pay at all.** Many E&O policies contain exclusions for failure to follow standard industry practices. A missing wire fraud advisory is exactly the kind of procedural lapse that triggers coverage disputes.

IREC can impose disciplinary sanctions ranging from formal reprimands to license suspension under Idaho Code 54-2064. Fines are not the primary enforcement mechanism in Idaho; instead, the Commission typically conditions license renewal on completing additional education or places agents on probation. However, civil lawsuits from defrauded clients often result in six-figure judgments against agents and brokerages—far exceeding any administrative penalty.

Deal cancellation is another real consequence. Title companies in Idaho increasingly refuse to close transactions where proper wire fraud documentation is missing from the file. If a title officer discovers the RE-26 was never signed and the buyer is about to wire funds, expect a delay at minimum and a refusal to close at worst. This puts your commission and your client relationship at immediate risk.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make

The first and most frequent error is timing. Agents deliver the RE-26 at closing instead of at contract execution. By the time a buyer sits at the closing table, they may have already wired funds days earlier. The advisory must arrive early enough to change behavior, not serve as a checkbox exercise after the fact.

The second mistake is verbal-only warnings. Some agents tell clients "watch out for wire fraud emails" but never deliver the written form. Verbal warnings are essentially worthless from a liability standpoint because they cannot be proven in a dispute. IREC and the courts look for signed documentation.

**Third, agents frequently fail to reinforce the warning when closing instructions arrive.** The RE-26 creates awareness at contract execution, but the actual danger peaks when the title company sends wiring details—often weeks later. Agents who do not follow up with a reminder at that critical moment leave a gap in their duty of care. A simple email or text reminding the client to call the title company and verify instructions directly can prevent disaster.

Fourth, some agents assume the title company handles everything and that they have no personal obligation. This is flatly wrong under IREC's interpretation of licensee duties. The title company verifies the mechanics of the wire; the agent must ensure the client knows to verify instructions independently before acting.

Fifth, agents at high-volume brokerages sometimes use outdated form versions. The Idaho Association of REALTORS updates forms periodically, and using a superseded RE-26 version can create ambiguity about whether the disclosure met current standards. Always pull the current year's form from your association's forms library.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Idaho Code 54-2038, which makes the designated broker responsible for the conduct of all licensees operating under their license. This is not a vague suggestion—it is a statutory mandate. If an agent in your office fails to deliver wire fraud advisories and a client loses money, IREC will examine your supervision systems.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation |
|---|---|---|
| RE-26 present in every transaction file | Per-transaction review | Signed copy in file |
| Form version current | Quarterly check | Compare to IAR forms library |
| Agent training on wire fraud protocols | Annually minimum | Signed attendance record |
| Title company coordination on verification procedures | Per-transaction or per-relationship | Written protocol in office policy manual |
| Follow-up reminder documented | Per-transaction | Email or text record in file |

Brokers should build wire fraud advisory delivery into their [transaction checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rather than relying on individual agents to remember. A system that flags a missing RE-26 before the file moves to the next stage eliminates the most common compliance gap. Monthly file audits should specifically check for the presence and proper dating of wire fraud forms.

Training is not optional. IREC expects brokers to demonstrate that agents received instruction on current wire fraud risks and the office's specific protocols for handling wire instructions. An annual training session with a signed roster provides defensible evidence that supervision was active, not passive. Some Idaho brokerages now require agents to complete wire fraud training before accessing the office's transaction management platform.

## Connecting Wire Verification to Your Transaction Workflow

The [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) is not a separate task from your closing workflow—it is embedded in it. Every time a title company issues closing figures and wiring details, your transaction file should reflect that the client was advised to verify those instructions through a separate communication channel. Phone verification using a number obtained independently (not from the email containing wire instructions) is the gold standard.

Agents who treat wire fraud prevention as a one-time disclosure at contract signing miss the point. The threat is dynamic—criminals monitor email accounts for weeks before striking at the exact moment funds are about to move. Your workflow needs at least two touchpoints: the initial RE-26 delivery and a follow-up communication when wiring details are issued. Many agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on other compliance items also miss this second touchpoint because it is not built into their systems.
