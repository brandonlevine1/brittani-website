## The New Reality of North Dakota Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026

The practice changes stemming from the 2024 NAR settlement have now been in effect long enough to separate agents who adapted from those who are still guessing. North Dakota commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 carry specific documentation, disclosure, and negotiation requirements that differ from what many agents practiced even two years ago. Getting this wrong does not just expose you to a scolding from your broker — it opens the door to deal cancellations, fines from the [North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC)](https://www.realestatend.org/), and civil liability.

North Dakota's market is unique. Many transactions happen in smaller communities where handshake agreements once filled the gap that formal paperwork should have occupied. That era is over. The combination of federal settlement obligations and state-level regulatory expectations means every commission conversation must now be documented, every buyer agreement must be signed before touring, and every MLS entry must be scrubbed of compensation offers to buyer agents.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed Commission Mechanics in North Dakota

The settlement, finalized in late 2024 after the Sitzer/Burnett verdict, imposed two non-negotiable practice changes nationwide. First, offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer appear in any MLS field. Second, MLS participants working with buyers must have a written agreement in place — specifying the compensation the buyer will pay — before touring any property.

**North Dakota adopted these changes through its MLS affiliates and NDREC enforcement guidance without enacting new standalone legislation.** The state's existing statutory framework under North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 43-23 already required written agency agreements, but enforcement was inconsistent. The settlement forced uniform compliance.

The practical effect is a separation of the listing side and the buying side that did not functionally exist before. Listing agents now set their fee with the seller. Buyer agents set their fee with the buyer. Any crossover — where the seller agrees to cover some or all of the buyer-agent fee — must be negotiated outside the MLS and documented on the appropriate forms.

## Forms and Documents North Dakota Agents Must Use

North Dakota agents work primarily with forms approved or recommended by the NDREC and distributed through the North Dakota Association of Realtors (NDAR). The specific forms that intersect with commission compliance deserve close attention.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Commission Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| NDAR Buyer Agency Agreement | Establishes buyer-broker relationship and compensation terms | Must specify exact fee or percentage buyer will pay; required before touring |
| NDAR Listing Agreement | Establishes seller-broker relationship | Must detail listing-side commission; may include seller's voluntary offer to cover buyer-agent compensation |
| NDREC Agency Disclosure Form | Discloses agent's role in transaction | Must be provided at first substantive contact; clarifies who agent represents |
| Purchase Agreement (NDAR Form) | Contract between buyer and seller | May include negotiated terms for seller-paid buyer-agent compensation as a line item |

The NDREC Agency Disclosure Form is required under NDCC 43-23-12.1 and must be presented at the earliest substantive contact with a consumer. Agents who skip this step — or present it after they have already started discussing properties — risk disciplinary proceedings. The form itself does not set commission terms, but it frames the relationship that commission negotiations will follow.

**Buyer Agency Agreements must include a dollar amount or percentage that reflects the buyer-agent's compensation.** Vague language such as "customary commission" or "to be determined" does not satisfy the settlement requirements. The NDAR revised its buyer agreement template in mid-2025 to include explicit fields for these figures, and agents should be using the most current version.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Noncompliance is not theoretical. The consequences fall into three categories: regulatory, contractual, and civil.

On the regulatory side, the NDREC has authority under NDCC 43-23-11.1 to impose fines, require additional education, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely. Since 2025, the Commission has issued guidance letters emphasizing that failure to maintain proper written agreements before showing property constitutes a violation of North Dakota licensing law. Fines for first-time administrative violations can reach $2,000 per occurrence, with escalating penalties for repeat offenders.

From a contractual standpoint, a missing or defective buyer agreement can torpedo a deal at closing. Title companies and closing attorneys in North Dakota have become increasingly diligent about verifying that commission disbursements match the written agreements on file. If a buyer agent cannot produce a valid agreement authorizing the commission they expect to receive, the closing agent may refuse to disburse those funds.

Civil liability is the most expensive risk. The entire premise of the NAR settlement was that the prior system inflated costs for buyers. An agent who reverts to the old model — steering, tying compensation to MLS fields, or pressuring sellers to offer buyer-agent fees as a condition of listing — invites exactly the kind of lawsuit the settlement was designed to prevent. Plaintiffs' attorneys are actively monitoring for these patterns, and the class action structure means individual agents can be pulled into litigation that extends well beyond their own transactions.

## Common Mistakes North Dakota Agents Are Making Right Now

Even well-intentioned agents are stumbling. Five errors keep surfacing in broker audits and NDREC complaint files.

**First, agents are showing property before obtaining a signed buyer agreement.** This is the most frequent violation, particularly in rural North Dakota markets where agents might drive a buyer past multiple listings in a single afternoon. Every property tour — even an informal drive-by that turns into an interior viewing — requires the agreement to already be executed.

Second, some agents are using outdated NDAR form versions that lack the required compensation specificity fields. The 2024 buyer agreement template does not meet the current standard. Agents should confirm they are pulling the most recent forms from the NDAR portal, not relying on saved PDFs from prior transactions.

Third, listing agents are verbally communicating buyer-agent compensation offers and assuming that keeps them compliant because "it's not in the MLS." Verbal offers are difficult to document and even harder to enforce. The better practice is to include any seller-offered buyer-agent concession in writing, either within the listing agreement's compensation section or through a separate written communication between brokerages. This also ties into [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) — ambiguous compensation terms create friction that kills transactions at the eleventh hour.

Fourth, agents are failing to renegotiate buyer agreements when the compensation math changes mid-transaction. If a seller offers to pay part of the buyer-agent commission and the buyer agreement states the buyer will pay the full amount, the agreement needs an amendment reflecting the new arrangement. Otherwise, the buyer could end up paying a fee that was already covered by the seller, creating a potential overcharge and a compliance violation.

Fifth, dual agency situations — still legal in North Dakota under NDCC 43-23-12.1 with proper written consent — are generating messy compensation documentation. When one agent represents both sides, the commission split must be clearly disclosed in both the listing agreement and the buyer agreement, and the dual agency consent form must be signed before the purchase agreement is executed.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under NDCC 43-23-11. If an agent under your license violates commission documentation requirements, the NDREC can hold you accountable even if you did not personally participate in the transaction. That makes internal auditing a non-optional exercise.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agreement execution date | Must predate the first showing for each buyer client | Every transaction |
| Compensation specificity | Dollar amount or percentage clearly stated — no vague terms | Every transaction |
| Form version currency | Agents using the latest NDAR-approved templates | Quarterly |
| MLS compliance | No buyer-agent compensation in listing fields | Monthly spot-check |
| Dual agency documentation | Consent forms signed before purchase agreement execution | Every dual-agency transaction |

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy that specifically addresses NAR settlement compliance. The NDREC has signaled through its 2025-2026 newsletter bulletins that it will look favorably on brokerages that can demonstrate proactive compliance systems when evaluating disciplinary outcomes. A documented policy with agent acknowledgment signatures reduces both regulatory exposure and E&O insurance risk.

The paperwork volume this creates is significant. Transaction files now include more forms, more signatures, and more version-control headaches than they did before the settlement. This is exactly the kind of administrative load that causes [agents to miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and [hate paperwork](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) — and it is exactly where systematized compliance tracking becomes essential rather than aspirational.

## Negotiating Compensation Without Violating the Rules

Nothing in the settlement prohibits agents from earning competitive commissions. The rules govern how those commissions are disclosed, documented, and offered — not what they can be.

Buyer agents should present their value proposition to buyers and negotiate their fee as a standalone conversation, separate from any particular listing. The buyer agreement locks in that fee. If a seller later offers to cover part or all of it, the buyer benefits — but the buyer agent's compensation baseline is already established.

Listing agents should discuss with sellers whether offering buyer-agent compensation makes strategic sense for attracting offers. In a slower market or a rural listing with limited buyer traffic, a seller-paid buyer-agent concession can be a legitimate marketing tool. That offer just cannot appear in the MLS. It can be communicated through broker-to-broker channels, on the listing brokerage's website, or in property marketing materials.

**The key distinction is transparency and documentation.** Every compensation arrangement must be traceable through signed paperwork. If a dispute arises three months after closing, the forms should tell the entire story without anyone needing to recall a phone conversation.
