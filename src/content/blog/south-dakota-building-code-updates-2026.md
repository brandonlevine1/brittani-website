# South Dakota Building Code Updates 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Know Before Closing

The phrase **South Dakota building code updates 2026** is landing in more agent inboxes and broker meeting agendas than at any point in the last decade. Several municipalities across the state have moved to adopt portions of the 2024 International Residential Code (IRC) and the 2024 International Building Code (IBC), and those adoptions are reshaping what agents need to verify before a transaction closes. If you are listing a new-construction home in Sioux Falls or representing a buyer on a renovated property in Rapid City, the code environment underneath that deal may have shifted since you last checked.

South Dakota does not impose a single statewide mandatory building code. Instead, under [SDCL Chapter 11-10](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/Codified_Laws/DisplayStatute.aspx?Type=Statute&Statute=11-10), municipalities and counties hold the authority to adopt, amend, and enforce building codes within their own jurisdictions. That local-control framework means agents must know not just what the latest model codes say, but which version their specific market area has adopted and when enforcement begins.

## Why the 2024 IRC and IBC Adoption Cycle Matters

The International Code Council (ICC) publishes updated model codes on a three-year cycle. The 2024 IRC and 2024 IBC introduced changes to energy efficiency standards, egress window specifications, electrical panel clearance requirements, and fire-resistance ratings for attached garages. Sioux Falls, the state's largest city, has historically adopted updated IRC editions within 18 to 24 months of publication. Rapid City, Mitchell, Aberdeen, and Brookings each follow their own adoption timelines.

**For agents, the practical impact is straightforward: a home permitted under the 2018 IRC carries different baseline standards than one permitted under the 2024 IRC.** Buyers financing with FHA or VA loans may see appraisers flag items that were code-compliant at the time of construction but fall short of current expectations. When you are advising clients, the permit date — not the construction date — is the anchor point for determining which code version applies.

This is also where transaction management details matter. Missed deadlines on inspection contingencies or disclosure amendments can compound code-related issues into deal-killing problems. Agents who have read about [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) will recognize the pattern: one overlooked date cascades into liability exposure.

## How South Dakota's Local-Control Framework Creates Agent Risk

Because SDCL 11-10 delegates code authority to local governments, there is no single state registry where an agent can look up every jurisdiction's current adopted code edition. The City of Sioux Falls publishes its adopted codes through the Planning and Building Services Department. Rapid City maintains its own list through the Community Development Department. Smaller municipalities may rely on county-level adoption or may not have adopted any building code at all.

This patchwork creates two distinct risks for agents. First, an agent working transactions in multiple jurisdictions — say, Sioux Falls and Tea, or Rapid City and Box Elder — may unknowingly apply assumptions from one code environment to another. Second, when a property straddles the line between an incorporated municipality and unincorporated county land, the applicable code (or absence of one) changes entirely.

**Agents are not building inspectors, and no one expects you to interpret fire-separation distances.** But you are expected to avoid material misrepresentation under SDCL 36-21A-72 and to disclose known adverse conditions. If you learn during a transaction that a property has unpermitted work or a code violation flagged by an inspector, your duty to disclose is triggered — regardless of whether the seller wants that information shared.

## Key Changes in the 2024 Model Codes Relevant to Residential Transactions

Not every code change matters to a real estate closing, but several from the 2024 cycle show up in inspection reports and appraisals frequently enough that agents should recognize them. The table below summarizes the changes most likely to surface during a South Dakota residential transaction.

| Code Section | 2021 IRC Standard | 2024 IRC Standard | Agent Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy Efficiency (R402) | R-49 attic insulation in Climate Zone 6 | R-60 attic insulation in Climate Zone 6 | New-construction appraisals may note insulation levels; buyers may request energy audits |
| Egress Windows (R310) | 5.7 sq ft minimum opening | 5.0 sq ft minimum opening (grade-floor rooms) | Older homes with undersized egress may now meet code for ground-floor bedrooms under updated standards |
| EV-Ready Parking (R404.2) | No requirement | One EV-ready outlet per dwelling unit | New construction in adopting jurisdictions must include EV infrastructure |
| Bathroom Ventilation (M1507) | 50 CFM intermittent / 20 CFM continuous | 50 CFM intermittent / 20 CFM continuous (humidity-sensing controls encouraged) | Inspectors may recommend humidity-sensing fans; not a pass/fail item but appears in reports |
| Attached Garage Separation (R302.6) | 1/2-inch gypsum board on garage side | Unchanged, but fire-rated door language tightened | Inspectors flagging hollow-core doors between garage and living space more frequently |

For agents working in jurisdictions that have already adopted the 2024 IRC, these items will appear in inspection reports on homes permitted after the adoption date. For older homes, the standard at the time of the original permit controls — but buyer expectations often don't align with that legal reality, which is where your advisory role matters.

## The Disclosure Obligation: Forms, Statutes, and Liability

South Dakota requires sellers of residential property to complete the **Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement**, as mandated under SDCL 43-4-44. This form asks sellers to disclose known defects in structural components, roofing, electrical systems, plumbing, HVAC, and other material conditions. The South Dakota Association of REALTORS provides a standardized version of this form that most brokerages use.

Agents should ensure the disclosure form is completed before or at the time of listing, not after an offer is received. If a seller discloses a known code violation — for example, unpermitted electrical work that does not meet the adopted code — the agent must transmit that disclosure to prospective buyers. Attempting to minimize or omit that information violates SDCL 36-21A-72, which prohibits licensees from making substantial misrepresentations concerning a property.

**The consequence for noncompliance is not abstract.** The South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) can impose disciplinary action including license suspension or revocation under SDCL 36-21A-24. Beyond regulatory discipline, agents and their brokerages face civil liability. A buyer who discovers an undisclosed code violation post-closing can pursue damages, and the listing agent's errors and omissions insurance may not cover intentional omissions. ## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Issues

Five errors appear repeatedly in SDREC complaint files and E&O claims related to building codes. Each one is avoidable.

**First, agents assume that "grandfathered" means "no problem."** A home built to 1990 code standards is legally compliant for its era, but a buyer's lender or insurer may still require updates. Agents who tell buyers "it's grandfathered, don't worry about it" without explaining the distinction between legal compliance and lender requirements create false expectations that blow up at closing.

Second, agents fail to verify permit histories on renovated properties. A finished basement, an added bathroom, or an enclosed porch may have been completed without permits. SDCL 11-10-5 authorizes municipalities to require permits for construction work, and unpermitted work is a material fact. Telling a buyer the basement is "finished" without checking whether permits were pulled is a disclosure failure waiting to happen.

Third, agents confuse inspection report recommendations with code violations. A home inspector may recommend upgrading a two-prong outlet to a grounded three-prong outlet, but that recommendation is not a code violation if the home was built before grounded outlets were required. Mischaracterizing recommendations as violations — or vice versa — erodes your credibility with both clients and cooperating agents.

Fourth, agents neglect to update the disclosure form when new information surfaces mid-transaction. If a home inspection reveals a code issue the seller did not initially disclose, the seller must amend the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement. Agents who let this slide are exposed to claims of facilitating nondisclosure.

Fifth, agents working new-construction transactions skip the certificate of occupancy verification. A CO confirms the building department has inspected the property and found it compliant with the adopted code. Closing on a new-construction home without a CO in the file is a risk no competent agent should accept.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-69, which requires designated brokers to supervise the activities of their affiliated licensees. When it comes to building code issues, that duty translates into several concrete audit obligations.

| Audit Item | What the Broker Should Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form completion | Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement is in the file before first showing | Every listing |
| Permit verification on renovations | Agent has checked municipal permit records for any disclosed or visible renovations | Every listing with visible improvements |
| CO verification for new construction | Certificate of Occupancy from the local building department is in the closing file | Every new-construction transaction |
| Disclosure amendments | Amended disclosure forms are executed and delivered when inspection reveals new defects | After every inspection that surfaces new issues |
| Agent training on adopted codes | Agents know which code edition their primary market has adopted | Annually, or when a jurisdiction adopts a new code edition |

Brokers who treat building code literacy as "the inspector's problem" are underestimating the regulatory exposure. The SDREC has made clear in past disciplinary actions that broker supervision failures compound agent-level violations. A broker who knew — or should have known — that an agent was routinely skipping permit verification shares in the liability.

This is also where operational systems earn their keep. Brokerages that rely on memory and informal checklists to track disclosure deadlines and file completeness are the ones most likely to face audit gaps. Understanding [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is not a productivity hack — it is a compliance strategy.
