## Louisiana 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: The Framework Agents Must Understand

The phrase "Louisiana 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026" should not make your eyes glaze over. It should trigger a checklist in your head because getting this wrong can collapse a deal, expose your brokerage to liability, and leave your client with an unexpected five-figure tax bill. Louisiana's conformity with federal Section 1031 rules does not mean you can ignore state-specific reporting requirements, nonresident withholding statutes, or the particular timelines that trip up even experienced agents.

**Louisiana follows the Internal Revenue Code for income tax purposes under Louisiana Revised Statutes Title 47, Section 293 (La. R.S. 47:293), which means the state generally recognizes a valid federal 1031 exchange as a deferral for state income tax as well.** But "generally" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence. The Louisiana Department of Revenue (LDR) enforces its own filing requirements, and agents who assume federal compliance equals state compliance are the ones who end up in mediation.

This post covers what you actually need to know: the statutes, the forms, the penalties, and the mistakes that get agents and their brokers into trouble.

## How Louisiana Conforms to Federal 1031 Rules—And Where It Diverges

Louisiana is a conformity state. La. R.S. 47:293(2) defines "Louisiana tax table income" by reference to federal adjusted gross income, which incorporates IRC Section 1031 deferral. When a taxpayer properly executes a like-kind exchange at the federal level, Louisiana recognizes that deferral without requiring a separate state election.

That said, divergence happens in three areas. First, Louisiana imposes its own [capital gains rates](https://revenue.louisiana.gov/IndividualIncomeTax) ranging from 1.85% to 4.25% under the 2024 tax reform (Act 10 of the 2024 First Extraordinary Session), which restructured brackets effective January 1, 2025. If an exchange is later disqualified—even years after closing—the taxpayer owes Louisiana income tax at those rates, plus interest from the original transaction date.

Second, nonresident sellers face a withholding regime under La. R.S. 47:157. Third, Louisiana does not conform to certain federal installment sale provisions when they intersect with failed exchanges, creating a gap that catches taxpayers off guard.

## Nonresident Withholding: The Rule That Catches Out-of-State Sellers

**Any agent representing a nonresident seller of Louisiana real property must understand Form R-1035N.** This is the LDR's Nonresident Seller Withholding Exemption form. When a nonresident sells Louisiana property, the closing attorney or title company is required to withhold Louisiana income tax at a rate determined by the LDR—unless the seller provides a valid exemption certificate.

A properly structured 1031 exchange qualifies as an exemption from withholding. But the seller must file Form R-1035N certifying the exemption before closing, and the qualified intermediary (QI) must be in place. If you have a nonresident client who decides at the last minute to pursue an exchange, the timing on R-1035N becomes extremely tight.

| Scenario | Withholding Required? | Form Needed | Agent Action |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------|
| Nonresident seller, no exchange | Yes | R-1035N not filed | Confirm title company withholds |
| Nonresident seller, valid 1031 exchange | No (exempt) | R-1035N filed with QI documentation | Verify R-1035N submitted pre-closing |
| Resident seller, valid 1031 exchange | No | None for withholding; IT-540 at tax time | Confirm QI engagement letter exists |
| Nonresident seller, exchange fails post-closing | Yes (retroactive) | Amended R-1035N or direct LDR payment | Notify client of tax liability immediately |

The closing attorney bears statutory responsibility for withholding, but agents who fail to flag the issue early create deal delays that result in complaints to the Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC).

## Key Forms and Filing Requirements for Louisiana 1031 Exchanges

Louisiana does not have a standalone state exchange form analogous to IRS Form 8824. Instead, the deferral flows through the taxpayer's annual return. Here is the breakdown of forms involved:

IRS Form 8824 (Like-Kind Exchanges) is filed with the federal return for the year of the exchange. Louisiana Form IT-540 is the resident individual income tax return where the federal AGI—already reflecting the 1031 deferral—flows through. Louisiana Form IT-540B serves the same purpose for nonresidents and part-year residents.

Form R-1035N, discussed above, handles the nonresident withholding exemption. Additionally, if the relinquished or replacement property involves a partnership or LLC taxed as a partnership (extremely common in Louisiana commercial deals), Form IT-565 (Partnership Return of Income) must report the exchange at the entity level.

**Agents do not file these forms themselves, but agents who cannot explain which forms their client needs are agents who lose referrals from CPAs and attorneys.** Your value in a 1031 transaction is coordination—knowing who needs what and when. ## Common Mistakes Louisiana Agents Make in 1031 Exchange Transactions

Five errors recur across LREC complaints and malpractice claims involving exchange transactions.

First, agents advise clients on tax outcomes without proper disclaimers. Louisiana law does not license real estate agents to provide tax advice. When you tell a client "you won't owe any taxes if you do a 1031," you have stepped outside your licensed scope under La. R.S. 37:1431 et seq. The correct statement is always a referral to a CPA or tax attorney, documented in writing.

Second, agents fail to verify that the qualified intermediary is properly bonded and not a disqualified person under IRC 1031(a)(2). Louisiana does not separately regulate QIs at the state level, which means there is no state licensing body checking their qualifications. If your client's QI disappears with the exchange funds—as happened nationally in the LandAmerica/1031 Exchange Services collapse—the deal dies and your client faces immediate Louisiana income tax liability. Always confirm the QI carries fidelity bonding and errors-and-omissions insurance.

Third, agents mishandle the 45-day identification period by assuming weekends or holidays extend the deadline. They do not. The IRS deadline is absolute, and because Louisiana conforms to federal rules, a blown 45-day window triggers state tax liability simultaneously.

Fourth, agents representing both the buy-side and sell-side in an exchange transaction fail to document their role, creating [disclosure problems](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026) that, while that post addresses Wisconsin, mirror Louisiana's own dual agency concerns under LREC Rule 2503.

Fifth, agents neglect to communicate the exchange structure to the title company early enough. Louisiana closings often involve notarial acts (La. C.C. Art. 1839), and the notary must ensure the deed reflects the QI's role. A last-minute revelation that "this is a 1031" can delay closing by days.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Louisiana carry supervisory liability under La. R.S. 37:1455 and LREC Rule 2505. When an agent facilitates a transaction involving a 1031 exchange, the broker's audit obligations extend beyond standard file review.

| Audit Point | What to Verify | Risk if Missed |
|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Tax advice documentation | Agent's written referral to CPA/attorney is in file | Unauthorized practice of law claim |
| QI engagement letter | Copy in transaction file before closing | Client claim if QI fails |
| R-1035N (nonresident deals) | Filed and confirmed by closing attorney | Withholding failure, LDR penalties |
| 45-day identification notice | Copy of written identification in file | Proof of timeline compliance |
| Closing statement accuracy | Exchange proceeds routed to QI, not seller | Exchange disqualification |

Brokers should implement a flag in their transaction management systems for any deal where a 1031 exchange is mentioned. The file review cannot wait until post-closing. ## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

LREC has the authority to suspend or revoke licenses under La. R.S. 37:1454 for incompetence, negligence, or failure to disclose material facts. An agent who misrepresents the tax implications of an exchange—or who fails to disclose that an exchange structure may affect the buyer's timeline—faces disciplinary action ranging from a formal reprimand to license revocation.

Beyond LREC discipline, civil liability is real. Louisiana courts have upheld claims against agents for negligent misrepresentation when clients relied on tax-related statements. The damages in a failed 1031 exchange can be substantial: the client owes both federal capital gains tax (up to 20% plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax) and Louisiana state income tax (up to 4.25%), plus interest running from the original sale date.

**Errors-and-omissions insurance typically covers negligent acts within the scope of licensed activity, but it often excludes claims arising from tax advice.** Check your policy. If your E&O carrier excludes tax-related claims, your personal assets are exposed the moment you opine on exchange qualification.

## The 2026 Federal Landscape and Its Louisiana Impact

Congress periodically considers eliminating or restricting Section 1031 exchanges. As of mid-2026, no legislation has passed that eliminates like-kind exchange treatment, but multiple proposals have circulated that would cap deferral amounts or restrict exchanges to properties under certain values. Any federal change would automatically flow through to Louisiana via La. R.S. 47:293's conformity provision—unless the Louisiana Legislature passes a decoupling statute.

Agents should monitor both federal tax legislation and the Louisiana Legislature's annual sessions. The LDR publishes Revenue Information Bulletins (RIBs) that clarify how new federal changes apply at the state level. Bookmark the [Louisiana Department of Revenue's RIB page](https://revenue.louisiana.gov/LawsPolicies/RevenueInformationBulletins) as part of your professional development routine.
