## Understanding Louisiana Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of "Louisiana who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026" lands on nearly every agent's desk the moment a purchase agreement hits the drafting stage. Unlike states that codify rigid allocations, Louisiana relies heavily on contract language and parish custom, which means the licensee who prepares or reviews the agreement carries real liability. Getting this wrong does not just upset a client — it can blow a deal, trigger a Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC) complaint, or land a brokerage in civil court.

Louisiana's Civil Code governs sales obligations, but the practical enforcement mechanism for agents is La. R.S. 37:1455, which outlines conduct warranting disciplinary action. Agents owe a duty to present accurate cost information to both parties. Failing that duty exposes them to sanctions ranging from fines to license revocation.

## The Default Framework: What Louisiana Law Actually Says

**Louisiana does not impose a state-level real estate transfer tax.** That single fact differentiates it from most Southern states and shifts more attention onto recording fees, title charges, and attorney costs as the primary closing-cost battleground. Parish clerks of court charge recording fees under La. R.S. 13:844, and those fees vary by parish and document type.

Because there is no statutory mandate dictating who pays which cost, the [Louisiana REALTORS](https://www.lrec.gov/) standard forms place the allocation squarely inside the purchase agreement. Form 100 (Louisiana Residential Agreement to Buy or Sell) contains blank fields and check boxes for assigning specific costs. If an agent leaves those fields blank or unchecked, the closing attorney must rely on parish custom — an outcome that breeds last-minute disputes.

The absence of a transfer tax does not mean closings are cheap. Buyers routinely face lender fees, appraisal costs, and prepaid insurance, while sellers typically absorb commission, title policy premiums, and outstanding property tax prorations. ## Form 100 and Form 200: Where Cost Allocation Lives

The two forms agents handle most in residential deals are Form 100 (the purchase agreement) and Form 200 (the counter-offer/amendment). Section 8 of Form 100 expressly addresses closing costs, including who pays for the survey, termite certificate, flood certification, and homeowner's warranty. Every checkbox left unchecked is a potential dispute at the closing table.

**Agents who rely on verbal agreements about cost allocation are gambling with their licenses.** The LREC has made clear in past enforcement actions that oral side agreements about fees do not satisfy the requirement for written disclosure. If it is not in the contract, it does not exist from a regulatory standpoint.

Form 400 (the property disclosure document required under La. R.S. 9:3198) does not address closing cost allocation, but agents sometimes confuse disclosure duties with cost duties. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with mismatched expectations about money — and that mismatch almost always traces back to sloppy form completion.

## Buyer Costs: What Agents Must Communicate Early

Buyers in Louisiana face a cost stack that can range from two to five percent of the purchase price, depending on the loan product and parish. Lender origination fees, credit report charges, flood certifications, and prepaid escrow items (taxes, insurance) make up the bulk. Agents representing buyers should generate an estimated cost sheet before the offer goes in, not after.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Loan Estimate form arrives within three business days of application, but buyer's agents who wait for that document before discussing costs with their clients are already behind. A buyer who sees a $12,000 cost surprise at contract acceptance is more likely to demand seller concessions — or walk entirely.

Recording fees for the mortgage instrument run between $80 and $250 in most Louisiana parishes, but Orleans Parish and East Baton Rouge Parish charge on a sliding scale tied to loan amount. Agents operating across parish lines need to know these differences cold. ## Seller Costs: Commission, Title, and Prorations

Sellers carry the heavier load in most Louisiana transactions. Commission (negotiated between seller and listing broker) remains the largest single expense, followed by the owner's title insurance premium, any outstanding municipal liens, and prorated property taxes. Under Louisiana's fiscal-year property tax system, taxes are assessed January 1 and billed in the fall, meaning sellers closing mid-year must prorate based on the current year's estimated bill.

Title insurance premiums in Louisiana are not regulated by the state Department of Insurance the way they are in some other states, so prices vary between underwriters. That variance means agents should never quote a specific premium without verifying with the selected title company or closing attorney.

**Termite (WDI) inspections present a frequent flashpoint.** Custom in many parishes assigns the WDI report cost to the seller, but Form 100 makes it negotiable. If the seller's agent checks that the seller pays for the inspection but the property fails, the next question — who pays for treatment — must also be addressed in the contract. Leaving it silent invites a dispute that can delay closing by weeks.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Louisiana Closing Costs

Three to five errors recur with alarming frequency in LREC complaint files and transaction audits.

First, agents omit the cost allocation section of Form 100 entirely, assuming "standard custom" will fill the gap. Custom varies by parish and even by closing attorney, so this assumption routinely fails. The listing agent in a Shreveport transaction may assume the seller pays for the survey, while the closing attorney in that parish expects the buyer to cover it.

Second, agents misrepresent seller concessions as "credits" without specifying what the credit covers. Under TRID rules, lender-approved seller contributions must be tied to specific costs on the Closing Disclosure. An agent who writes "seller to credit buyer $5,000 at closing" without linking it to allowable costs can trigger a lender compliance rejection days before closing.

Third, agents fail to update cost allocations when the transaction moves from Form 100 to Form 200 (amendment). If the original agreement assigns the survey to the seller but an amendment changes the closing date and survey provider, the cost allocation must be restated or confirmed. Silence in the amendment does not necessarily preserve the original term if the amendment supersedes conflicting language.

Fourth, agents conflate the closing attorney's fee with the title insurance premium. These are separate line items, and the party responsible for each can differ. Telling a seller "you just pay the attorney fee" when the attorney fee and the title premium together run $2,500 creates a material misrepresentation.

Fifth, agents promise specific closing cost numbers in listing presentations or buyer consultations without disclaiming that figures are estimates. Under La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(5), making a "substantial misrepresentation" is grounds for license discipline — and a $3,000 estimate that balloons to $5,200 at closing can meet that threshold.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under LREC Rule 2503, which requires them to maintain policies ensuring their agents comply with state law and commission rules. When it comes to closing costs, brokers should audit three specific areas on every file.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Form 100 Section 8 completion | All cost allocation boxes checked or initialed | Dispute at closing; LREC complaint |
| Seller net sheet accuracy | Estimate signed by seller matches final costs within reasonable range | Misrepresentation claim; E&O exposure |
| Amendment consistency | Form 200 language does not contradict original cost terms unintentionally | Contract ambiguity; deal collapse |
| Commission disclosure | Agreed commission documented in listing agreement and not conflated with closing costs | LREC Rule 2503 violation |

Brokers who conduct monthly file audits rather than waiting until post-closing dramatically reduce their exposure. A simple checklist — cost allocation confirmed, amendments reviewed for consistency, seller net sheet initialed — catches the majority of errors before they reach the closing table. ## Liability and Consequences for Non-Compliance

The LREC can impose fines up to $2,000 per violation under La. R.S. 37:1455 and can suspend or revoke licenses for negligence or incompetence in handling transactions. Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. A seller who nets $6,000 less than the agent's written estimate may sue for negligent misrepresentation, and Louisiana courts have recognized this cause of action in real estate contexts.

> La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(5): The commission may suspend or revoke a license where the licensee has made a substantial misrepresentation or false promise of a character likely to influence, persuade, or induce.

Deal cancellation is the more immediate practical risk. A buyer who discovers at the closing table that they owe $4,000 more than expected can invoke financing contingency failures (if the lender won't cover the gap) or simply refuse to close. In either case, the agent who failed to document cost responsibility accurately bears the blame — and often the cost of relisting or renegotiating.

E&O insurance does not always cover claims rooted in failure to complete standard forms correctly. Many policies contain exclusions for "known violations of standard procedures." A broker whose agent habitually skips Section 8 on Form 100 may find coverage denied when a claim finally materializes.
