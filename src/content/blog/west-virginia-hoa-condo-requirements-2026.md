## Why West Virginia HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention

Every agent listing or selling a unit in a common interest community faces a specific disclosure framework under West Virginia law. The **West Virginia hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** are rooted in the Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act (UCIOA), codified at West Virginia Code Chapter 36B, which the state adopted with its own modifications. Failing to follow these rules does not just create awkward conversations at closing—it gives buyers a statutory right to walk away from a signed contract.

The West Virginia Real Estate Commission enforces licensing standards, but the disclosure obligations themselves sit in the state's property code. Agents who treat HOA paperwork as an afterthought are exposing their sellers—and their own licenses—to measurable risk. This post breaks down the statutes, the forms, the deadlines, and the enforcement mechanisms you need to internalize before your next transaction involving an association-governed property.

## The Statutory Framework: W. Va. Code Chapter 36B

West Virginia's version of the UCIOA governs condominiums, cooperatives, and planned communities created after the Act's effective date. The key sections for resale transactions are §36B-4-108 (buyer's right to cancel) and §36B-4-109 (resale certificates). These two provisions create the backbone of disclosure obligations when a unit changes hands.

Under §36B-4-109, the seller must furnish a resale certificate prepared by the association. This certificate is not optional commentary—it is a mandated package of financial, legal, and operational data about the association. The association itself is required to provide the certificate within ten days of a request, and the seller must pass it to the buyer before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed.

**Section §36B-4-108 grants the buyer a five-day cancellation window** measured from receipt of the resale certificate or from contract execution, whichever is later. If the certificate never arrives, the cancellation right never expires. That single fact makes timely delivery a closing-or-collapse issue rather than a mere formality.

## What the Resale Certificate Must Contain

The resale certificate is not a single-page form an agent fills out at their desk. It is an association-produced document that must include specific items enumerated in [W. Va. Code §36B-4-109](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/WVCODE/code.cfm?chap=36b&art=4). Among the required disclosures are the current regular and special assessments, any unpaid assessments against the unit, the association's current operating budget, the amount of reserves, and whether the association holds any pending litigation.

The certificate must also identify any capital expenditures anticipated within the current or next fiscal year, any fees charged for a transfer or move-in, and whether there are any outstanding violations attributed to the unit. Insurance coverage details—specifically what the master policy covers versus what the unit owner must insure—round out the disclosure package.

Agents do not prepare this document themselves, but they are responsible for requesting it on time and confirming delivery to the buyer. The association can charge a reasonable fee for preparation, and under §36B-4-109(b) that fee is the seller's responsibility unless the contract says otherwise.

## Deadlines and Cancellation Rights: A Side-by-Side View

Timing errors are the number-one way agents lose deals involving HOA or condo units. The table below maps out the critical deadlines that apply once a listing agent or seller requests the resale certificate.

| Event | Deadline | Authority |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| Association must provide resale certificate after request | 10 days | §36B-4-109(a) |
| Seller delivers certificate to buyer | Before or at contract execution | §36B-4-109 |
| Buyer cancellation period begins | Later of: receipt of certificate or contract execution | §36B-4-108 |
| Buyer cancellation period expires | 5 days after trigger date | §36B-4-108 |
| Cancellation if certificate never delivered | Unlimited until delivery + 5 days | §36B-4-108 |

That bottom row is the one that keeps brokers awake. A deal that closed three months ago can technically be unwound if the buyer never received the certificate and chooses to assert the statutory right. Courts have upheld similar cancellation provisions in other UCIOA-adopting states, and West Virginia's language tracks the uniform act closely.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with HOA and Condo Disclosures

**Mistake 1: Requesting the resale certificate after the contract is ratified.** The statute contemplates delivery before or at contract execution. When an agent waits until after ratification, the buyer's cancellation window starts late, pushing risk into the inspection and financing period. Worse, if the certificate reveals an unexpected special assessment, the buyer can cancel for free during a window the seller thought had already closed.

Mistake 2: Relying on the HOA management company's verbal summary instead of the formal written certificate. A phone call or email from a property manager does not satisfy §36B-4-109. The statute requires a certificate, and the buyer's right to cancel is measured from receipt of that certificate—not from a casual heads-up about dues.

Mistake 3: Confusing the resale certificate with the seller's property condition disclosure. West Virginia requires both. The property condition disclosure is the seller's own statement about the physical condition of the unit (governed by W. Va. Code §36-6D-1 et seq.). The resale certificate is an association-level financial and governance document. One does not replace the other, and delivering only one leaves the transaction exposed.

Mistake 4: Failing to document delivery with a date-stamped acknowledgment. Without proof of when the buyer received the certificate, calculating the five-day cancellation window becomes a dispute rather than a fact. Agents should obtain a signed and dated receipt or use a platform that logs delivery electronically.

Mistake 5: Not verifying whether the property is actually governed by a recorded declaration. Some older communities operate with informal "associations" that were never properly formed under Chapter 36B. If no declaration is recorded, the UCIOA resale certificate requirements may not apply—but the agent still owes the buyer honest disclosure of any fees or restrictions they know about under the West Virginia Real Estate License Act.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under W. Va. Code §30-40-19. If an agent's failure to deliver required HOA disclosures results in a complaint, the broker's supervision practices come under scrutiny. There are three areas every West Virginia brokerage should be auditing on a quarterly basis.

First, audit the timing of resale certificate requests against contract dates. Pull a sample of closed files involving association-governed properties and confirm that the certificate was requested before or concurrent with the listing going active—not days after a contract was ratified. If your agents are consistently late, your compliance training has a gap.

Second, confirm that each file contains a signed buyer acknowledgment of receipt with a legible date. The five-day clock only starts when you can prove it started. A missing receipt is a latent cancellation risk sitting in your closed files.

Third, verify that the resale certificate on file matches the statutory checklist in §36B-4-109. Associations sometimes produce abbreviated summaries that omit required items like pending litigation or insurance coverage details. If the certificate is incomplete, the buyer's cancellation right may survive because the statutory requirement was not fully satisfied. Your agents need training to spot incomplete certificates and push back on the management company before passing them to the buyer.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Certificate request date | Before or at listing activation | Late delivery, extended cancellation window |
| Buyer receipt with date | Signed acknowledgment in file | Unproven cancellation deadline |
| Certificate completeness | All §36B-4-109 items present | Potential perpetual cancellation right |
| Fee allocation | Contract specifies who pays | Closing cost dispute at settlement |
| Declaration verification | Recorded declaration exists | Misapplication of UCIOA rules |

These audits are not busywork. They are the difference between a clean E&O claims history and a six-figure defense bill. Brokers who treat post-closing file reviews as optional are gambling with every association-governed transaction their agents handle.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Miss the Mark

The most immediate consequence of noncompliance is deal cancellation. A buyer who never received a compliant resale certificate can cancel the contract and demand return of the deposit. Under §36B-4-108, cancellation is a matter of right—no cause required—so long as the statutory window remains open.

Beyond cancellation, agents face licensing discipline from the West Virginia Real Estate Commission. Failure to disclose material facts about a property violates W. Va. Code §30-40-24(a)(4), which can result in license suspension, fines up to $5,000 per violation, or revocation. The Commission has broad authority to investigate complaints and impose sanctions without waiting for a civil lawsuit.

Civil liability adds another layer. Buyers who suffer financial harm because they purchased a unit without knowing about a massive pending special assessment or active litigation against the association can sue the seller and the listing agent for damages. If an agent knew or should have known that the resale certificate was incomplete or never delivered, they lose the shield of ignorance. Court costs, settlement payments, and increased E&O premiums follow.

For agents working through [title and closing procedures](/blog/west-virginia-title-and-closing-procedures-2026), the HOA certificate is a prerequisite that title companies increasingly refuse to close without. If the title company flags a missing certificate and the closing is delayed, your seller may have a claim against you for failing to handle the paperwork on time—especially if a rate lock expires or a contingency deadline passes. Understanding [who pays closing costs](/blog/west-virginia-who-pays-closing-costs-2026) in your market also matters here, since disputes over who covers the association's certificate preparation fee can stall a closing if not addressed in the contract.

## Practical Workflow for Compliant Transactions

A defensible workflow starts at listing appointment. When you take a listing on a property governed by a recorded declaration, request the resale certificate immediately—before the property hits the MLS. Use the association's designated request form if one exists, or send a written request referencing §36B-4-109 and the ten-day response obligation.

Build a tracking mechanism into your transaction file. Without those four dates documented, you cannot prove compliance if challenged.

Once you receive the certificate, review it against the statutory checklist before forwarding to the buyer. Flag any blank fields or missing sections and follow up with the association in writing. Deliver the certificate to the buyer with a cover letter that states the date and references the five-day cancellation right under §36B-4-108. Obtain the buyer's dated signature on an acknowledgment form and file it immediately.

## Connecting Disclosure Rules to Recording and Transfer Obligations

HOA disclosures do not exist in a vacuum. They intersect with [transfer tax and recording requirements](/blog/west-virginia-transfer-tax-recording-fees-2026) because some associations require transfer fees that must be reflected on the settlement statement. If the resale certificate identifies a transfer fee and it is not accounted for at closing, the settlement agent may hold the file open—or worse, the association may refuse to update its records to reflect the new owner.

Agents should cross-reference the resale certificate's transfer fee disclosure with the HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure line items before the settlement date. Catching discrepancies early prevents last-minute scrambles that erode client confidence and invite complaints.
