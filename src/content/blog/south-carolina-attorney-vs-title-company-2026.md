## South Carolina Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

South Carolina stands apart from the majority of U.S. states in how it handles real estate closings. If you are asking about South Carolina attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026, the short answer is that a licensed attorney must supervise every residential closing in this state. No exceptions, no workarounds, and no amount of title company involvement changes that requirement.

This rule catches out-of-state agents, new licensees, and even experienced brokers who relocate from states where title companies run the whole show. Understanding why and how South Carolina enforces attorney supervision protects your license, your clients, and your commission check.

## The Statutory Foundation: SC Code Section 40-5-310 and Beyond

**South Carolina defines the preparation of legal instruments tied to real property transfers as the practice of law.** SC Code of Laws Section 40-5-310 broadly prohibits any person or entity from practicing law without a license from the South Carolina Supreme Court. The state Supreme Court further clarified in multiple opinions — most notably *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426 (1987) — that conducting a real estate closing without attorney supervision constitutes unauthorized practice of law (UPL).

Section 40-5-320 grants the circuit courts authority to enjoin and punish UPL as contempt of court. That means a title company employee who attempts to "run" a closing without a supervising attorney is not just violating an administrative rule — they are potentially in contempt.

The South Carolina Bar's UPL Committee actively investigates complaints against non-attorney closing operations. The Bar has issued advisory opinions reinforcing that even the preparation of a HUD-1 successor (now the TRID Closing Disclosure coordination) and deed drafting fall squarely within attorney-only territory when performed in connection with a closing.

## What Title Companies Can and Cannot Do

Title companies remain active participants in South Carolina transactions. They issue title insurance commitments, perform title searches, resolve liens, and handle escrow funds — all legitimate functions. The line is drawn at the closing table itself and the preparation of closing documents.

| Function | Attorney Required? | Title Company Permitted? |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Title search and examination | No | Yes |
| Issuing title insurance commitment | No | Yes |
| Preparing the deed | Yes | No |
| Preparing the closing package | Yes | No |
| Conducting the closing conference | Yes | No |
| Disbursing funds post-closing | Yes (supervision) | Yes (under attorney direction) |
| Recording documents | No | Yes |

Many closings in South Carolina involve both a title company and an attorney working in tandem. The title company handles insurance underwriting while the attorney prepares documents and presides over the closing. Agents should understand this dual-track process so they can set accurate expectations with buyers and sellers — particularly those relocating from [states where title companies handle everything independently](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026).

## Forms Every SC Agent Must Know

The South Carolina REALTORS (SCR) publishes standard transaction forms that presuppose attorney involvement. The most critical for closing purposes include:

**SCR Form 310 — Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate** — contains a specific section designating the closing attorney, including name, firm, address, and phone number. Leaving this blank or writing "TBD" is a common drafting error that creates ambiguity about who controls the closing timeline.

SCR Form 320 (Vacant Land Contract) carries the same closing attorney designation requirement. Form 390 (Short Sale Addendum) and Form 395 (Bank-Owned Property Addendum) both reference attorney involvement for document review. Form 350 (Inspection Addendum) requires attorney coordination when inspection findings trigger contract amendments that alter the closing structure.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC), housed under the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation (LLR), does not itself mandate the attorney requirement — that flows from the Supreme Court's UPL authority. However, SCREC enforces licensee conduct standards under SC Code 40-57-145, and an agent who facilitates a closing without proper attorney involvement may face disciplinary action for conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Get This Wrong

Agents do not have authority to prepare closing documents, explain legal implications of deeds or mortgages to clients, or offer opinions on title. Doing so, even informally, crosses into UPL territory.

**The consequences stack up fast.** If an agent drafts or modifies a deed, explains a title exception's legal significance, or coordinates a closing without ensuring attorney involvement, they face potential SCREC disciplinary action (fines up to $10,000 per violation under SC Code 40-57-145), civil liability for any damages resulting from defective documents, and possible contempt proceedings from circuit court if the Bar's UPL Committee gets involved.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a buyer or seller discovers post-closing that no attorney supervised the transaction, the aggrieved party may have grounds to challenge the validity of documents executed without proper legal oversight. Title insurance companies may deny claims arising from closings conducted outside the statutory framework, leaving your client uninsured on a defective title.

Your E&O insurance likely excludes claims arising from unauthorized practice of law. That means personal liability with no backstop.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in South Carolina Closings

**First: recommending a specific attorney based solely on speed or low cost without disclosing the relationship.** SCREC requires agents to disclose any financial relationship or incentive arrangement with closing attorneys under SC Code 40-57-135(B). RESPA Section 8 prohibitions on kickbacks still apply federally, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau actively enforces affiliated business arrangement disclosure requirements. Failing here triggers both state and federal penalties.

Second: preparing or modifying contract language beyond filling in blanks on approved SCR forms. The South Carolina Supreme Court has repeatedly held that drafting legal instruments — including contract amendments that go beyond the standardized form fields — constitutes the practice of law. Agents who write custom addenda without attorney review expose themselves and their clients to both UPL claims and contract enforceability challenges.

Third: telling clients an attorney is "optional" or that a title company can handle everything. This misrepresentation exposes agents to SCREC discipline for providing inaccurate information about the transaction process. It also sets up a failed closing when the title company correctly refuses to conduct the closing conference without attorney involvement.

Fourth: failing to confirm that the designated closing attorney is actively licensed and in good standing with the South Carolina Bar. Agents can verify attorney status through the SC Bar's online directory. A closing "supervised" by a suspended or disbarred attorney provides no legal protection — the closing may be treated as if no attorney participated at all.

Fifth: coordinating directly with the lender on closing document preparation without routing through the closing attorney. The attorney must review and approve all closing documents before execution. Agents who [miss these coordination deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) by communicating directly with lenders about document changes create confusion and potential UPL exposure.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge (BIC) carry supervisory liability under SCREC regulations. SC Code 40-57-350 requires BICs to ensure that agents under their supervision comply with all applicable laws, including the attorney requirement for closings.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Form 310 attorney designation | Filled in with licensed attorney info | Every transaction |
| Agent communications pre-closing | No legal advice or document drafting | Quarterly file review |
| Affiliated business disclosures | RESPA-compliant AfBA disclosures on file | Every referral |
| Attorney license verification | Current SC Bar status confirmed | Annually per attorney relationship |
| Closing attendance documentation | Attorney or attorney-designee present | Every transaction |

**Brokers should implement a mandatory file checklist that includes proof of attorney involvement before any commission is disbursed.** A transaction file lacking documentation of attorney supervision is a ticking regulatory bomb — one SCREC audit or client complaint can trigger an investigation covering every file in the office.

Brokers also need to train agents that "the closing attorney handles it" is not a blanket excuse for disengagement. Agents remain responsible for ensuring their clients understand the [process and their own contractual deadlines](/blog/where-deals-break-down). The attorney handles legal aspects; the agent handles the transaction management, communication, and compliance documentation that supports the deal reaching the closing table intact.

Regular training sessions — at minimum during the mandatory continuing education cycle — should cover the distinction between permitted agent activities (filling in form blanks, communicating timelines, coordinating inspections) and prohibited activities (drafting legal documents, interpreting title exceptions, advising on deed structures).

## How 2026 Changes Affect the Attorney Requirement

The core attorney supervision mandate has not changed for 2026 — it remains rooted in South Carolina Supreme Court authority that predates most current licensees' careers. However, several practical shifts affect how agents interact with closing attorneys this year.

The [South Carolina Bar's updated guidance](https://www.scbar.org/lawyers/legal-resources-info/ethics-advisory-opinions/) on remote closings, issued in response to post-pandemic flexibility, now permits attorney-supervised remote notarization under SC Code 26-2-410 (Uniform Electronic Transactions Act as amended). This means an attorney can supervise a closing via secure video conference with a remote online notary, but only if the attorney maintains real-time involvement throughout the signing. Agents cannot substitute a Zoom link for attorney presence.

SCREC's 2025-2026 continuing education requirements now include a mandatory core course module covering UPL boundaries and closing procedures. Agents renewing their licenses in 2026 must complete this module — a signal that the Commission is seeing enough violations to warrant heightened education.
