The state's title insurance framework, escrow requirements, and recording statutes carry specific obligations that fall squarely on agents and their brokers. ## How Montana Structures Its Title and Closing Process

Montana is a title-theory state, meaning legal title passes through a deed of trust rather than a mortgage in most residential transactions. The trustee—typically a title company—holds bare legal title until the borrower satisfies the debt. This distinction matters because foreclosure procedures, reconveyance requirements, and title insurance commitments all flow from this structure.

**The Montana Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Department of Labor and Industry, oversees agent conduct during transactions, while title insurers answer to the Montana Commissioner of Securities and Insurance (CSI).** Agents sit between two regulatory bodies, which means a procedural error can trigger scrutiny from either or both. The relevant statutes live primarily in Title 30, Chapter 14 (Montana Title Insurance Act) and Title 70, Chapter 21 (conveyances and recording) of the Montana Code Annotated (MCA).

Closings in Montana are predominantly handled by title companies rather than attorneys. The escrow closing model dominates, with a title officer or escrow agent coordinating document execution, fund disbursement, and recording. Agents do not conduct closings themselves, but they are responsible for delivering correct contract documents and ensuring their clients meet contractual obligations before the closing date.

## Key Forms and Contracts Agents Must Handle

The Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) publishes the standard forms used in most residential transactions. The Residential Real Estate Purchase Agreement (MAR Form #1) is the backbone of every deal. It contains built-in timelines for title commitment delivery, inspection contingencies, and closing date specifications that agents must track with precision.

Form #3, the Buyer's Agency Agreement, and Form #4, the Seller's Listing Agreement, both contain provisions related to closing cost allocation and title-related responsibilities. The Title Contingency Addendum (Form #9) gives buyers a defined window—typically 15 days unless modified—to review the title commitment and raise objections. Agents who fail to deliver this addendum when applicable risk losing their client's only contractual mechanism to address title defects.

| Form | Purpose | Critical Deadline |
|------|---------|-------------------|
| MAR Form #1 | Purchase Agreement | Defines closing date and all contingency periods |
| MAR Form #9 | Title Contingency Addendum | 15 days (default) for title objection |
| MAR Form #14 | Extension of Time Addendum | Must be executed before original deadline expires |
| MAR Form #17 | Counteroffer | Alters closing terms; resets timelines if accepted |
| MAR Form #21 | Wire Fraud Advisory | Must be signed by all parties at contract execution |

When title defects surface, agents should also be familiar with MCA 70-21-304, which governs the recording of corrective instruments, and MCA 70-21-201, which establishes the requirements for a valid deed in Montana. A deed that lacks proper acknowledgment under MCA 1-5-601 will be rejected at recording, killing your closing timeline.

## The Title Commitment: What Agents Must Verify

Once a purchase agreement is executed, the title company issues a preliminary title commitment. Under the Montana Title Insurance Act (MCA 33-25-301 et seq.), this commitment must disclose all encumbrances, liens, easements, and exceptions that the insurer intends to exclude from coverage. Agents are not title examiners, but they bear a professional duty to review Schedule B exceptions with their clients and flag anything unusual.

**Common Schedule B items in Montana include mineral reservations (extremely common given the state's mining history), irrigation ditch easements, and federal land patent restrictions.** Missing one of these during your client review creates liability exposure. If a buyer discovers post-closing that their property is burdened by an unreviewed easement that materially affects use, the listing agent and buyer's agent may both face claims of negligent representation.

The title commitment delivery deadline—governed by whatever the parties agreed to in MAR Form #1 or Form #9—is absolute when time-is-of-the-essence language is included. Montana courts have consistently upheld strict deadline enforcement under MCA 28-1-105, which states that time provisions in contracts are presumed material unless the contract expressly provides otherwise.

For agents working in rural Montana counties—Garfield, Prairie, Carter, Treasure—expect longer title search timelines. Many of these county clerks and recorders maintain only partial digital records, and chain-of-title gaps are more common on properties that changed hands before 1970. Build at least 14 business days into your purchase agreement for title delivery in these areas.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of procedural failures in Montana closings are not theoretical. They range from deal cancellation to license discipline to civil liability.

**If an agent allows a contractual deadline to lapse without obtaining a written extension (MAR Form #14), the opposing party can terminate the contract and claim the earnest money.** Under MAR Form #1's default language, earnest money disputes go to the title company holding the funds, and the company will typically interplead the funds into court if both parties claim entitlement. That process costs your client time and legal fees regardless of who ultimately prevails.

The Montana Board of Realty Regulation can discipline licensees under Administrative Rules of Montana (ARM) 24.210.641 for incompetence or negligence in a transaction. Penalties include license suspension, mandatory continuing education, fines up to $1,000 per violation, and in extreme cases, revocation. The Board's disciplinary database is public, and a single action on your record can destroy referral relationships overnight.

Civil liability is the bigger financial exposure. Under MCA 27-1-701 (negligence) and MCA 28-3-102 (breach of duty), agents who mishandle closing procedures—failing to disclose known title issues, missing deadlines that cause buyer harm, or providing unauthorized legal advice about title matters—face lawsuits that E&O insurance may not fully cover. Montana does not cap compensatory damages in professional negligence actions.

Wire fraud adds another dimension of risk. Montana agents must use the [Wire Fraud Advisory form](/blog/wisconsin-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) and verbally confirm wiring instructions through an independently verified phone number. The FBI's IC3 reports that real estate wire fraud losses exceeded $145 million nationally in 2025, and Montana's smaller title companies are increasingly targeted precisely because they lack enterprise-level security infrastructure.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make at Closing

Five specific errors surface repeatedly in Montana Board complaints and E&O claims related to closings.

First, agents fail to calendar title contingency deadlines separately from inspection deadlines. These are distinct periods under MAR Form #9, and letting the title contingency expire before your client reviews Schedule B exceptions eliminates their contractual right to object. Once that window closes, the buyer proceeds with whatever title defects exist or forfeits earnest money by backing out.

Second, agents provide opinions on title matters that constitute unauthorized practice of law. Under MCA 37-61-210, only licensed attorneys may advise clients on the legal significance of title exceptions, lien priority, or boundary disputes. When an agent tells a buyer "that easement won't affect you," they have crossed a legal line and created liability that sits entirely outside their professional authority.

Third, agents neglect to verify that the correct legal description matches across all documents—purchase agreement, deed, title commitment, and lender instructions. Montana's reliance on metes-and-bounds descriptions in rural areas (rather than simple lot-and-block references) makes transcription errors common. A single transposed bearing or distance measurement can cloud title for years.

Fourth, agents skip confirming that property taxes are current before closing. Under MCA 15-17-214, delinquent property taxes become a lien that survives transfer. Title companies catch this during the search, but if an agent has encouraged their client to waive the title contingency or shorten the review period, tax liens can surface too late to resolve before closing.

Fifth, agents fail to coordinate the [timeline between all parties](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) involved—lender, title company, opposing agent, and clients—leading to last-minute scrambles that jeopardize funding and recording. Montana county recorders have firm cutoff times (typically 4:00 p.m.), and missing same-day recording means your client does not hold title until the next business day.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Montana brokers carry direct supervisory liability under MCA 37-51-321 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. This is not an abstract obligation. The Board of Realty Regulation has sanctioned brokers personally for failures to supervise, even when the broker had no direct involvement in the flawed transaction.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Deadline tracking | Verify agents use calendaring systems for all contingency periods | Every transaction |
| Form version compliance | Confirm agents use current-year MAR forms, not outdated versions | Quarterly |
| Wire fraud protocol | Review that Wire Fraud Advisory is signed and filed in every transaction file | Every transaction |
| Earnest money handling | Confirm deposit receipt and timely delivery to escrow per ARM 24.210.625 | Within 2 business days of acceptance |
| Title commitment review documentation | Ensure agents document client review of Schedule B in writing | Every transaction |

ARM 24.210.625 specifically requires that [earnest money deposits](/blog/wisconsin-escrow-regulations-2026) be delivered to the party designated in the purchase agreement within the timeframe stated—typically two business days of contract acceptance. Brokers who discover that agents are holding checks in desk drawers or car consoles face Board scrutiny and potential trust account violations.

**Brokers should also audit for unauthorized dual-role situations where their agents attempt to act as both the real estate licensee and the closing agent.** Montana does not prohibit this explicitly, but the inherent conflicts of interest and the distinct regulatory frameworks (CSI for title/escrow, Board of Realty for licensees) create a compliance minefield that most E&O carriers will not cover.

The Montana Department of Labor and Industry publishes Board meeting minutes and disciplinary actions at [its official website](https://boards.bsd.dol.mt.gov/realestate), and brokers should review these quarterly to stay current on enforcement trends.
