# Iowa 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: What Every Agent Must Know Before Closing

Understanding Iowa 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is no longer optional knowledge for licensed agents handling investment property transactions. The gap between federal conformity and state-specific reporting trips up even experienced practitioners, and the consequences land on your client's doorstep—sometimes years after closing. This article breaks down the precise Iowa statutes, forms, compliance risks, and broker-level audit responsibilities that matter right now.

## How Iowa Conforms to Federal Section 1031—and Where It Diverges

Iowa Code Chapter 422, Section 422.7 establishes the state's individual income tax provisions by reference to federal adjusted gross income. Because Iowa starts its tax computation from the federal base, any gain properly deferred under IRC Section 1031 at the federal level flows through as deferred at the state level as well. This is full conformity, but it comes with strings attached.

**The critical difference is documentation.** The Iowa Department of Revenue (IDR) requires that taxpayers attach a copy of federal Form 8824 ("Like-Kind Exchanges") to their Iowa return and properly report the exchange on Iowa Schedule D. Failure to include this documentation can trigger an IDR audit, even when the federal return is clean.

Iowa also imposes withholding obligations on certain nonresident sellers under Iowa Code Section 422.16B. If the seller is a nonresident and does not provide a completed Iowa Nonresident Real Estate Withholding Exemption form (Form IA 44-007), the closing agent must withhold the greater of 3% of the gross sale price or the seller's entire gain. A valid 1031 exchange is one of the enumerated exemptions—but only if properly documented at closing.

## Specific Iowa Forms and Filing Requirements for 1031 Exchanges

The paperwork chain starts at the closing table and ends with the Iowa Department of Revenue. Here is the precise sequence agents should understand:

| Stage | Form / Document | Who Files | Deadline |
|-------|----------------|-----------|----------|
| Closing | IA 44-007 (Nonresident Withholding Exemption) | Seller, via closing agent | At closing |
| Federal Return | Form 8824 (Like-Kind Exchanges) | Taxpayer / CPA | April 15 (or extension) |
| Iowa Return | IA 1040 Schedule D or IA 1120 Schedule D | Taxpayer / CPA | April 30 (or extension) |
| Attachment | Copy of Form 8824 attached to Iowa return | Taxpayer / CPA | With return filing |
| IDR Audit Response | IA 843 (Claim for Refund) if reclassified | Taxpayer / CPA | Within 3 years of assessment |

For entity-level transactions (LLCs, partnerships, S-corps), Iowa Form IA 1065 Schedule K-1 passes through the deferral to individual partners. Each partner must then report on their own Iowa return. The chain breaks if even one partner fails to attach Form 8824.

Agents do not file these forms themselves, but agents who advise clients on the exchange timeline must understand that the 45-day identification period and 180-day exchange period from IRC Section 1031(a)(3) also govern the Iowa deferral. Miss either federal deadline and Iowa treats the transaction as a taxable sale.

## Iowa Nonresident Withholding: The Hidden Trap

**Iowa's nonresident withholding requirement catches more agents off guard than any other state-level issue.** Under Iowa Administrative Code Rule 701-46.4, the buyer's closing agent—typically a title company—must withhold on sales by nonresidents unless an exemption applies. A properly structured 1031 exchange qualifies as an exemption under Form IA 44-007, but only if the seller certifies the exchange in writing at closing.

Here is where agents create liability for themselves: if you represent a nonresident seller who claims they are doing a 1031 exchange but never actually identifies replacement property within 45 days, the exchange fails. The withholding exemption was improperly claimed. The IDR can then pursue the closing agent for the unpaid withholding, plus penalties.

The safe harbor for agents is straightforward. Confirm that your client has engaged a qualified intermediary (QI) before closing and that the QI's exchange agreement is referenced in the closing documents. Do not accept a verbal assurance that "we're doing a 1031" without written QI documentation. This is similar to the verification discipline required in [wire instructions verification processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process)—trust nothing that is not in writing.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Agent liability in Iowa 1031 exchanges is indirect but real. You will not receive a fine from the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) specifically for botching a 1031 disclosure. However, IREC's administrative rules under [Iowa Administrative Code 193E](https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/administrativeRules/rules?agency=193E) allow disciplinary action for incompetence, negligence, or failure to disclose material facts.

If an agent advises a client that a transaction qualifies for 1031 treatment without disclosing the nonresident withholding requirement, and the client later owes back taxes plus a 75% fraud penalty under Iowa Code Section 422.25(2), that client has grounds for a negligence claim against the agent. The Iowa Real Estate Commission can investigate, and errors and omissions insurance does not always cover tax advice that falls outside the agent's licensed scope of practice.

Deal cancellation is also a risk. If a buyer discovers mid-transaction that a seller's 1031 exchange structure is invalid—say, because the seller is using exchange proceeds to pay off personal debts before acquiring replacement property—the buyer may invoke contingency protections to exit. The deal dies, the commission evaporates, and the agent's reputation takes the hit. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) helps agents anticipate these failure points.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make on 1031 Exchanges

**Five specific errors recur in Iowa 1031 transactions, and each one is preventable.**

The first mistake is conflating "like-kind" with "same type of property." Under IRC Section 1031, any real property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment qualifies as like-kind to any other such property. An Iowa farmland parcel can exchange for a commercial building in Des Moines. Agents who tell clients they need to find "another farm" are wrong, and that misinformation costs clients time during the 45-day identification window.

The second mistake is ignoring boot. When a seller receives cash or non-like-kind property as part of the exchange (called "boot"), that portion is taxable at both the federal and Iowa level. Agents who do not flag this at the listing stage leave clients blindsided at tax time. Iowa taxes boot as ordinary income to the extent of depreciation recapture under Iowa Code Section 422.7(39A).

The third mistake involves timing the closing without coordinating with the QI. If a seller's relinquished property closes before the QI's exchange agreement is fully executed, the entire exchange is disqualified. The proceeds must go directly to the QI—never to the seller, not even for a day.

The fourth mistake is assuming all Iowa counties handle nonresident withholding identically. Some closing agents are more aggressive about requiring Form IA 44-007 than others, but the statutory obligation is uniform. Agents who work across county lines in Iowa (Cedar Rapids to Iowa City, for instance) sometimes encounter different title company practices and assume the less strict office is correct.

The fifth mistake is failing to document the exchange in the purchase agreement. The Iowa Association of Realtors standard purchase agreement includes a cooperation clause for 1031 exchanges, but agents often skip filling it out or mark it as not applicable during the listing stage—only to scramble when the seller decides to exchange weeks before closing.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents who handle investment properties owe a heightened duty of oversight under Iowa Administrative Code 193E-11.2, which requires brokers to maintain adequate records and supervise all transactions. For 1031 exchanges specifically, brokers should audit three areas.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|---------------|----------|
| QI Documentation | Exchange agreement in transaction file | Missing or dated after closing |
| Nonresident Withholding | IA 44-007 present in file | Absent for out-of-state seller |
| Purchase Agreement | 1031 cooperation clause completed | Blank or marked N/A when exchange is happening |
| Timeline Tracking | 45-day and 180-day deadlines documented | No timeline memo in file |
| Boot Calculations | Net equity worksheet showing cash or debt relief | Unexplained cash disbursements to seller at closing |

Brokers who discover a missing IA 44-007 after closing face a choice: report the gap to the title company so withholding can be collected, or risk that the IDR traces the deficiency back to the closing file. The latter scenario exposes the brokerage to liability if the seller has disappeared or refuses to pay.

Training is not optional here. Brokers should require annual continuing education on 1031 exchange mechanics for any agent listing investment property. The Iowa Real Estate Commission's CE requirements under 193E-6.1 allow elective topic selection—adding a 1031 module protects the brokerage and the client.
