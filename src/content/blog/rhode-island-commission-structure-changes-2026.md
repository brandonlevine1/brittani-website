The Rhode Island commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have reshaped how every agent in the state handles compensation disclosures, buyer agreements, and listing presentations. Since August 2024, when the practice changes took effect nationally, Rhode Island's regulatory framework has layered state-specific requirements on top of NAR's baseline mandates. Agents who operated on autopilot through 2025 are now facing a tighter enforcement environment heading into the second half of 2026.

Rhode Island's relatively small market—roughly 12,000 annual residential transactions—means the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation (DBR) can scrutinize complaints more quickly than larger states. The margin for error is thin.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed Rhode Island's Commission Framework

The national settlement eliminated cooperative compensation offers through MLS systems. In Rhode Island, State Line MLS (the dominant Multiple Listing Service covering the state) implemented this prohibition by removing all commission-related fields from listing input screens effective August 17, 2024. That date marked the hard cutoff.

Before the settlement, a listing agent could enter "2.5% to buyer's agent" in the MLS, and that served as a blanket offer of compensation. Now, any compensation discussion between a seller and a buyer's agent must happen outside the MLS entirely. Rhode Island General Laws Chapter 5-20.5, which governs real estate brokerage, already required written disclosure of agency relationships; the settlement added new written compensation disclosure layers on top.

**The DBR issued Bulletin 2024-7 clarifying that all compensation agreements must be documented in writing before services are rendered.** This applies to both buyer-agent agreements and any seller-offered compensation to a cooperating broker. Verbal agreements are not enforceable and constitute a violation of R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-10, which addresses fiduciary duties and disclosure obligations.

## Written Buyer Representation Agreements: Rhode Island Requirements

Every buyer's agent in Rhode Island must now execute a written buyer representation agreement before touring properties. The [standard practice changes mirror those in other states](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026), but Rhode Island's implementation has distinct elements.

The Rhode Island Association of Realtors (RIAR) released updated Form 700 (Buyer Representation Agreement) in late 2024, with a further revision in January 2026 adding a mandatory compensation disclosure addendum (Form 700A). Form 700 must specify the exact amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, stated as either a flat dollar figure or a percentage of the purchase price. Ranges or "up to" language are not permitted under the current RIAR guidelines.

Form 700A functions as a standalone disclosure that can be presented to the buyer at any point before the first showing. It explicitly states whether the buyer is responsible for agent compensation, whether the seller has agreed to pay it, or whether compensation will be split between both parties. The buyer must initial each compensation scenario that applies.

**Failure to execute Form 700 before showing a property can trigger a formal complaint to the DBR.** The penalty structure under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-18 includes fines up to $1,000 per violation, license suspension for repeat offenders, and mandatory continuing education hours. The DBR has processed 23 formal complaints related to buyer agreement violations since January 2025, according to its public enforcement log.

## Seller-Side Compensation Disclosure Requirements

On the listing side, Rhode Island agents must address commission structure in the listing agreement itself—not through MLS fields. RIAR Form 500 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) now includes Section 11B, added in the 2025 revision, which requires the seller to indicate whether they are willing to offer compensation to a buyer's agent and in what amount.

| Document | Form Number | Required Timing | Key Disclosure |
|----------|-------------|----------------|----------------|
| Buyer Representation Agreement | RIAR Form 700 | Before first showing | Agent compensation amount |
| Buyer Compensation Addendum | RIAR Form 700A | Before first showing | Who pays agent compensation |
| Listing Agreement | RIAR Form 500 | At listing execution | Seller's offer to buyer agent (if any) |
| Purchase & Sale Agreement | RIAR Form 100 | At offer submission | Compensation terms in contract |
| Agency Disclosure | RIAR Form 300 | At first substantive contact | Agency relationship type |

Section 11B does not mandate that sellers offer buyer-agent compensation. It simply requires documentation of the seller's decision either way. If a seller elects to offer compensation, the listing agent must communicate that offer directly to inquiring buyer's agents—phone, email, or written addendum—rather than broadcasting it through MLS.

## Common Mistakes Rhode Island Agents Are Making

After reviewing DBR enforcement actions and speaking with compliance officers at Rhode Island brokerages, five recurring errors stand out.

**First, agents are inserting compensation language into MLS "Agent Remarks" or "Private Remarks" fields.** State Line MLS monitors these fields with keyword filters. Phrases like "bonus," "2.5 to BA," or "seller will contribute to buyer costs" trigger automatic flagging. Fines start at $500 per listing for first offenses and escalate to $5,000 and MLS suspension for repeat violations.

Second, buyer's agents are presenting Form 700 at the offer stage rather than before showings. The DBR has been clear: the agreement must predate any property tours. Presenting it alongside an offer creates a retroactive documentation problem that exposes the agent to claims of undisclosed compensation. This mistake also opens the door to deal cancellation if the buyer later argues they were not informed of their financial obligation before committing.

Third, agents are using outdated form versions. The January 2026 revision of Form 700A added specific language about concession requests and how they interact with compensation caps under lender guidelines. Agents still using the 2024 version are technically non-compliant, because the older form lacks the required lender-coordination disclosure paragraph. Similar [form version issues arise in other states' compliance frameworks](/blog/wisconsin-commission-and-licensing-rules-2026).

Fourth, dual agency situations are creating compounded disclosure failures. Under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-8, dual agency requires written informed consent from both parties via RIAR Form 305 (Dual Agency Disclosure and Consent). When combined with the new commission rules, the dual agent must separately disclose compensation from both the listing side and buyer side on each respective agreement. Many agents are disclosing total compensation only once, which violates the per-party disclosure requirement.

Fifth, agents are failing to update compensation terms when deal terms change. If a buyer negotiates a price reduction and the agent's compensation is percentage-based, the resulting change in dollar amount must be acknowledged in writing before closing. A simple amendment to Form 700 suffices, but skipping this step creates a discrepancy between the agreement and the closing disclosure—a red flag for auditors.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Rhode Island carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-16. The statute makes the broker responsible for "adequate supervision" of all licensees operating under their license. A single agent's form error can trigger a brokerage-wide audit.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Red Flag |
|------------|-----------|----------|
| Form 700 execution date vs. first showing date | Every transaction | Showing predates agreement |
| Form 700A buyer initials present | Every transaction | Missing initials on any scenario |
| Form 500 Section 11B completed | Every listing | Blank or "TBD" entries |
| MLS listing for compensation language | Weekly | Keywords in any remarks field |
| Dual agency Form 305 with compensation breakdown | Every dual-agency deal | Single-line total instead of per-party |
| Form version verification | Monthly | Pre-2026 forms in active use |

**Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint at three stages: listing execution, accepted offer, and pre-closing.** The listing execution review confirms Form 500 Section 11B is complete and no compensation language appears in the MLS input. The accepted-offer review confirms Form 700 and 700A predate the first showing and match the compensation terms in RIAR Form 100 (Purchase and Sale Agreement). The pre-closing review confirms any amendments reflecting price changes or concessions are documented.

The DBR's [Real Estate Section](https://dbr.ri.gov/divisions/real-estate) publishes quarterly enforcement summaries. Brokers should review these to identify emerging compliance patterns. The Q1 2026 summary flagged 14 brokerage audits triggered by buyer complaints about undisclosed compensation—a 60% increase over Q1 2025.

## Liability Exposure and Deal Cancellation Risk

The consequences of non-compliance extend beyond fines. A buyer who was not properly informed of their compensation obligation before touring properties has grounds to void a purchase and sale agreement under Rhode Island's consumer protection statutes—specifically R.I. Gen. Laws Chapter 6-13.1 (Deceptive Trade Practices Act). Courts have not yet ruled on a post-settlement case in Rhode Island, but the statutory framework clearly supports rescission claims.

For agents, the immediate risk is license discipline. The DBR can issue a cease-and-desist, impose fines, require additional CE hours, or suspend/revoke a license. For brokers, the exposure multiplies across every non-compliant transaction in their office. Errors and omissions insurance policies increasingly include exclusions for "known regulatory violations," meaning intentional non-compliance with post-settlement rules may not be covered.

Deal cancellation also triggers practical fallout: earnest money disputes, wasted inspection and appraisal costs, and potential tortious interference claims if a competing agent reports the violation to gain advantage. The reputational damage in a market as small as Rhode Island—where agents frequently encounter the same counterparts—compounds quickly.
