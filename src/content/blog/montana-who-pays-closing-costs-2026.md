## Understanding Montana Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Montana who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by statute alone. Montana follows a custom-driven model where local practice, contract language, and negotiation determine which party absorbs each fee at closing. Agents who assume "that's just how it's done here" without documenting the allocation in the purchase agreement are creating liability for themselves and their brokerages.

Montana's Board of Realty Regulation, housed under the Department of Labor and Industry, enforces license law under Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Title 37, Chapter 51. The board expects licensees to demonstrate competency in advising clients on closing cost responsibilities. Failing to properly disclose, document, or explain cost allocation can trigger disciplinary action under MCA 37-51-321, which covers incompetence, negligence, and misrepresentation.

The Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) publishes standardized forms that provide the contractual framework for closing cost allocation. The form most agents work with daily is the MAR Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement, and the cost allocation checkboxes and blanks within it are where deals either stay clean or fall apart.

## Typical Cost Allocation in Montana Transactions

**Montana has no state-mandated transfer tax, which makes it different from most neighboring states.** This absence means the closing cost conversation focuses almost entirely on title insurance, escrow fees, recording fees, and lender-required charges. Local custom varies by county, but the table below reflects the predominant allocation across the state.

This table reflects custom, not law. Every item marked "Yes" under negotiable means the purchase agreement must specify the allocation explicitly. Agents who rely on "the title company will figure it out" are abdicating their fiduciary duty.

In many western Montana counties — Missoula, Flathead, Ravalli — the seller traditionally pays for the owner's title insurance policy. In eastern Montana, the split is less uniform. Regardless of geography, custom does not override contract language, and agents must confirm the allocation in writing using the appropriate MAR form fields.

## The Forms That Control Cost Allocation

The MAR Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement is the primary document where closing cost responsibilities are established. Section 8 of this form addresses title insurance and closing costs directly. Agents must fill in which party pays for owner's title insurance, how escrow fees are divided, and whether either party is contributing toward the other's costs (such as seller concessions toward buyer's closing costs).

**If Section 8 is left blank or incomplete, the deal has an ambiguity problem that title companies will flag — and that neither party's attorney will let slide.** The MAR Buyer's Closing Cost Addendum (sometimes used alongside seller concession language) provides additional specificity when the buyer is requesting a credit from the seller. This form must state the dollar amount or percentage cap, and it must align with whatever the buyer's lender permits.

Agents also need to work with the MAR Counteroffer form when closing cost allocation becomes a negotiation point after the initial offer. Each counteroffer that modifies cost responsibilities must clearly reference the specific line items being changed. A vague counteroffer that says "seller to pay buyer's closing costs" without a cap or specification creates an unenforceable obligation. Title officers in Montana routinely reject settlement statements that don't match the contract language precisely.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

Montana's Board of Realty Regulation has authority under [MCA 37-51-321](https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0370/chapter_0510/part_0030/section_0210/0370-0510-0030-0210.html) to discipline licensees for incompetence, negligence, failure to disclose material facts, and breach of fiduciary duty. Misrepresenting or failing to clarify closing cost responsibilities falls squarely within these categories.

The consequences are real. A first offense typically results in a letter of warning or mandatory continuing education. Repeated violations — or a single violation that causes measurable harm to a client — can lead to fines up to $1,000 per violation, license suspension, or revocation. The board also has authority to require restitution to harmed parties.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. A buyer who discovers at the closing table that they owe $4,000 more than their agent represented has grounds for a negligence claim. The E&O carrier may cover it, but the premium increase and reputation damage follow the agent for years. Brokerages face vicarious liability under Montana's supervisory requirements, making this a firm-wide compliance issue.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Montana Closing Costs

**The first and most frequent error is relying on verbal agreements about cost allocation.** An agent might tell their client "don't worry, the seller always pays for title insurance around here" without confirming it in the contract. When the other side's agent writes the counteroffer differently, the verbal assurance becomes worthless and the client feels misled.

The second mistake is failing to account for lender restrictions on seller concessions. FHA loans cap seller contributions at 6% of the purchase price. VA loans cap at 4% for concessions beyond normal seller-paid costs. Conventional loans vary by LTV ratio. When an agent negotiates a seller concession that exceeds lender limits, the deal either gets restructured at the last minute or falls apart entirely. Agents must verify lender guidelines before writing the number into the MAR form.

Third, agents routinely neglect to update the closing cost allocation when contract amendments occur. If an inspection addendum changes the purchase price, the dollar amounts allocated for closing cost credits may no longer make mathematical sense or may exceed lender caps. Every amendment should trigger a review of Section 8 allocations.

Fourth, some agents confuse prorated items (property taxes, HOA dues) with closing costs. Prorations are calculated by the title company based on the closing date, not negotiated in the purchase agreement the same way closing costs are. Misquoting proration amounts in a net sheet creates false expectations.

Fifth, agents occasionally tell clients that Montana has "no closing costs for sellers" because there is no transfer tax. This is flatly incorrect. Sellers routinely pay real estate commissions, owner's title insurance, their half of escrow fees, recording of satisfaction documents, and prorated taxes. The absence of a transfer tax does not mean sellers walk away without paying anything at closing.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Montana carry an independent obligation under MCA 37-51-313 to supervise affiliated licensees. This is not a suggestion — it is a statutory requirement that creates direct liability for the broker if an agent's error harms a consumer.

Brokers should audit every purchase agreement file for the following: that Section 8 of the MAR sale agreement is fully completed with no blanks; that any seller concession amount falls within the lender's published limits for the loan type; that counteroffers modifying cost allocation reference specific dollar amounts and line items; and that amendments triggered by inspection negotiations or price changes include a corresponding review of cost allocation.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Look For | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Section 8 completion | All cost allocation fields filled | Ambiguous contract, title company rejection |
| Seller concession caps | Amount within lender limits (FHA 6%, VA 4%, Conv. varies) | Loan denial, deal collapse |
| Counteroffer specificity | Dollar amounts, not vague language | Unenforceable terms, closing delay |
| Amendment consistency | Cost allocation reviewed after price changes | Exceeds lender caps, client surprise at closing |
| Net sheet accuracy | Prorations separated from negotiated costs | Client trust erosion, potential misrep claim |

Brokers who implement a systematic file review process catch these errors before they reach the title company. ## How Market Conditions Shift the Negotiation

Montana's housing market in 2026 reflects regional variation that directly impacts who has leverage on closing costs. In Bozeman and the Gallatin Valley, inventory constraints persist, giving sellers little reason to offer concessions. Buyers in those markets typically absorb all of their own closing costs and may even waive requests for seller credits to make their offers more competitive.

In contrast, markets like Great Falls, Billings, and parts of eastern Montana have seen inventory stabilize or increase. Sellers in those areas are more frequently agreeing to pay buyer closing costs — sometimes up to the lender-permitted maximum — as a way to attract offers without reducing list price. Agents working these markets need fluency in how concessions interact with appraisal values and lender requirements.

**Agents should never present a seller concession as "free money" to buyers.** The concession is typically built into a higher purchase price, which affects the buyer's loan amount, monthly payment, and appraisal risk. Explaining this clearly is part of the fiduciary duty agents owe under Montana license law. Agents who gloss over it to close deals faster are creating future complaints.
