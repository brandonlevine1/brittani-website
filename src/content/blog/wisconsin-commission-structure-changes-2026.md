## How Wisconsin Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026 Reshape Daily Practice

The phrase "Wisconsin commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026" now dominates continuing education courses, broker meetings, and bar-stool conversations across the state. Since the settlement's practice changes took full effect in August 2024, Wisconsin's Department of Safety and Professional Services (DSPS) has issued guidance clarifying how existing state law intersects with the new national framework. Agents who treat these changes as a minor paperwork update are misreading the risk.

Wisconsin was already a state that required written buyer agency agreements under Wis. Stat. 452.135, which mandates that licensees disclose their agency relationships at the earliest opportunity. The NAR settlement layered additional requirements on top of that statutory base—specifically around when, where, and how compensation is disclosed, negotiated, and documented.

This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, compliance timelines, and enforcement consequences that Wisconsin agents face right now.

## The Legal Foundation: Wisconsin Statutes and DSPS Rules That Govern Commission

**Wisconsin's statutory framework for commission disclosure predates the NAR settlement, but its interpretation has shifted.** Wis. Stat. 452.135 requires written disclosure of agency relationships. Wis. Stat. 452.133 governs the duties owed by agents, including the duty to disclose material facts—and the DSPS has clarified that compensation terms qualify as material facts that must be documented before substantive services begin.

The Wisconsin REALTORS Association (WRA) updated its standardized forms in late 2024 and again in early 2026 to align with these requirements. The [DSPS Real Estate Examining Board](https://dsps.wi.gov/Pages/BoardsCouncils/RealEstateExaminingBoard/Default.aspx) retains enforcement authority over licensees who violate these provisions. Disciplinary actions range from formal reprimands to license revocation, depending on severity and pattern.

Wis. Admin. Code RL 24.03 further requires that any agreement to compensate a licensee be in writing and signed before the licensee is entitled to that compensation. This was always the rule. The settlement simply made it impossible to rely on blanket MLS offers as the backstop.

## Key Form Changes: WB-36 and WB-1 Revisions

Wisconsin agents work with a standardized form library maintained by the WRA. Two forms carry the most weight in commission compliance after the settlement.

The **WB-36 Buyer Agency/Tenant Representation Agreement** must now include a specific, disclosed amount or rate of compensation that the buyer's agent will receive. Vague language like "customary commission" or "to be determined" no longer satisfies the settlement's requirements or DSPS expectations. The form must be signed before the agent shows properties or performs substantive brokerage services.

The **WB-1 Residential Listing Contract** was revised to remove assumptions about seller-funded buyer-agent compensation. Sellers can still agree to offer concessions, but the WB-1 now separates listing-side compensation from any optional concession field. Agents must explain this distinction to sellers and document the election clearly.

| Form | Purpose After Settlement | Key Change |
|------|--------------------------|------------|
| WB-36 | Buyer agency and compensation disclosure | Must state specific compensation amount/rate before services begin |
| WB-1 | Listing agreement | Seller concession offer separated from listing-agent compensation |
| WB-11 | Offer to purchase | May include buyer request for seller concession toward agent compensation |
| WB-40 | Amendment | Used to modify compensation terms if deal structure changes |

The WB-11 Offer to Purchase can include a request for seller concessions, and buyers may direct those concessions toward their agent's compensation. But the WB-36 must already be signed before the WB-11 is drafted. Sequencing matters.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Non-compliance carries consequences at three levels: regulatory, contractual, and civil.** The DSPS Real Estate Examining Board can impose disciplinary sanctions under Wis. Stat. 452.14 for violations of licensee duties. These include formal reprimands, mandatory additional education, fines, license suspension, and revocation. A first offense involving documentation failures typically results in a formal reprimand and a corrective education order, but repeat violations escalate quickly.

At the contractual level, a buyer agency agreement that fails to meet the specificity requirements may be deemed unenforceable. If compensation is not properly documented, the agent may have no legal basis to collect it—even if the buyer closes on a property. Wisconsin courts have historically enforced Wis. Admin. Code RL 24.03's requirement that compensation agreements be in writing; without a compliant WB-36, a commission claim is on thin ice.

Civil liability is the third vector. If a buyer or seller later claims they were not informed about commission structures, they may pursue claims for breach of fiduciary duty or misrepresentation. Post-settlement, plaintiff attorneys have specifically targeted transactions where commission disclosures were absent or ambiguous. The settlement itself does not create a private right of action, but Wisconsin's existing consumer protection statutes under Wis. Stat. Chapter 100 provide a pathway for injured parties.

## Common Mistakes Wisconsin Agents Are Making Right Now

Eighteen months into the new reality, certain errors keep appearing in transaction files across the state. These are not hypothetical—they surface in DSPS complaints, broker audits, and E&O claims.

**First, agents are signing the WB-36 at the offer stage instead of before showings.** The settlement requires the agreement to be in place before the agent provides substantive brokerage services. Showing a property is a substantive service. Signing the WB-36 on the same day as the offer creates a compliance gap that exposes both the agent and the brokerage.

Second, agents are inserting "up to X%" language in the WB-36 compensation field without explaining what determines the actual amount. The DSPS has indicated that "up to" language is permissible only if paired with clear criteria for how the final figure is determined. A bare "up to 3%" without further specification fails the specificity test.

Third, listing agents are verbally communicating buyer-agent compensation offers to cooperating brokers—effectively circumventing the MLS prohibition through phone calls or emails. This conduct violates the spirit and potentially the letter of the settlement. If documented in a complaint, it exposes the listing agent's brokerage to settlement enforcement actions.

Fourth, agents are neglecting to amend the WB-36 when compensation terms change mid-transaction. If a seller offers a concession that alters the buyer's out-of-pocket obligation for agent compensation, the WB-36 should be amended via WB-40 to reflect the new terms. Leaving the original figure in place creates a discrepancy that can trigger disputes at closing.

Fifth, some agents are failing to provide the [required agency disclosure](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) at first substantive contact, conflating it with the buyer agency agreement. These are separate obligations under Wisconsin law. The agency disclosure (form WB-46) must come first; the WB-36 follows once the buyer elects to enter into a representation relationship.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Wisconsin bear direct liability for the compliance failures of their licensees under Wis. Stat. 452.14(3)(n). A broker who fails to maintain reasonable supervisory systems faces the same disciplinary exposure as the agent who committed the violation. This is not theoretical—the DSPS has sanctioned brokers for systemic supervision failures in prior enforcement actions.

**Brokers should audit five specific elements in every transaction file.** First, confirm the WB-36 execution date precedes the first showing date. Second, verify the compensation amount or rate is specific and unconditional (or, if conditional, that the conditions are clearly articulated). Third, check that the WB-46 agency disclosure was delivered before or simultaneously with first substantive contact. Fourth, confirm that any WB-11 concession request aligns with the WB-36 compensation terms. Fifth, verify that no MLS listing under the brokerage's name contains a buyer-agent compensation offer in a prohibited field.

| Audit Item | Document to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|-------------------|----------|
| WB-36 timing | Buyer agency agreement | Execution date same as or after first showing |
| Compensation specificity | WB-36 Section 5 | "Up to" language without criteria |
| Agency disclosure | WB-46 | Missing or dated after first contact |
| Concession alignment | WB-11 and WB-36 | Dollar amounts contradict each other |
| MLS compliance | Listing data | Buyer-agent comp field populated |

Brokers who rely on agents to self-report compliance are exposed. Quarterly file audits—or better, real-time transaction tracking—are the minimum standard. ## How Seller Concessions Work Under the New Framework

Sellers in Wisconsin retain the right to offer concessions that buyers may direct toward their agent's compensation. This mechanism is explicitly permitted under the settlement terms. The key constraint is that these offers cannot appear in MLS compensation fields—they must be communicated through other channels such as the listing remarks (where permitted by local MLS rules), the seller's agent's communications, or the property marketing materials.

When a seller elects to offer a concession on the WB-1, it should be documented in the appropriate addendum or noted in a designated field that does not function as a blanket offer of cooperation compensation. The buyer then requests the concession on the WB-11, and the buyer's agent compensation is funded from that concession per the terms of the WB-36.

This three-document chain—WB-1 concession election, WB-11 concession request, WB-36 compensation agreement—must be internally consistent. A mismatch between what the seller offered, what the buyer requested, and what the agent's agreement specifies is a closing-day disaster waiting to happen. Title companies in Wisconsin are increasingly scrutinizing these figures before disbursement.

## The Intersection with Wisconsin Property Disclosures

Commission structure compliance does not exist in a vacuum. Agents juggling the new compensation documentation must still meet all existing disclosure obligations under Wisconsin law. The [Wisconsin property condition disclosure](/blog/wisconsin-property-condition-disclosures-2026) requirements remain fully in effect, and the [seller disclosure obligations](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) operate on their own timeline. Missing a compensation form while focusing on condition disclosures—or vice versa—is exactly the kind of distracted error that generates complaints.

The DSPS does not evaluate violations in isolation. An agent who fails on both compensation documentation and property disclosures in the same transaction faces compounded sanctions. Building a workflow that sequences all required documents—agency disclosures, compensation agreements, property disclosures, and offer documents—in the correct order is not optional. It is the baseline expectation for licensure in Wisconsin in 2026.
