## Understanding Kentucky Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The Kentucky buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 represent a significant operational shift for agents who previously relied on informal handshake arrangements before touring properties. Since the NAR settlement practice changes took effect in August 2024, the [Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC)](https://krec.ky.gov/) has enforced mandatory written buyer representation agreements with increasing rigor. Agents who fail to adapt face real consequences — not theoretical ones.

**Kentucky's regulatory framework for buyer agency is governed by KRS Chapter 324 and the administrative regulations in 201 KAR 11.** The Commission's enforcement arm has made clear that compliance is not optional, and that brokerage-level accountability is the standard. Every agent showing property to a buyer must have a signed, written agreement specifying the terms of representation, the duration, and the compensation structure before any touring begins.

This article breaks down the exact forms, statutes, deadlines, and pitfalls agents and brokers in the Commonwealth must master to stay compliant and keep deals from falling apart.

## The Legal Framework: KRS 324 and 201 KAR 11:121

Kentucky's statutory foundation for agency relationships lives in KRS 324.360 through KRS 324.370, which define the duties owed by licensees to their clients and customers. The administrative regulation 201 KAR 11:121 further specifies the disclosure and documentation requirements for establishing an agency relationship. Together, these provisions require that any buyer-agent relationship be memorialized in writing before the agent provides brokerage services beyond ministerial acts.

The distinction between "client" and "customer" matters enormously here. A buyer who has not signed a representation agreement is legally a customer, meaning the agent owes them honesty and fair dealing but not fiduciary duties like loyalty, confidentiality, and full disclosure of material facts. The moment an agent begins advising a buyer on pricing strategy, negotiation tactics, or property deficiencies without a signed agreement, they risk creating an implied agency — a liability minefield.

KREC's position, reinforced in its 2025 guidance bulletin, is unambiguous: implied agency does not satisfy the written agreement requirement. If the relationship is not on paper with signatures and dates, it does not exist in a form the Commission will recognize as compliant.

## Required Forms and Their Specific Functions

The Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) publishes the standard forms most brokerages adopt. For buyer representation, two primary forms dominate practice across the state.

| Form | Name | Use Case | Key Feature |
|------|------|----------|-------------|
| KAR Form 220 | Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Single-agent exclusive relationship | Buyer commits to one agent for a set period |
| KAR Form 225 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Non-exclusive, property-specific | Can be used for single showings or limited engagements |
| KAR Form 210 | Agency Disclosure | All initial contacts | Explains agency options before representation is chosen |
| KAR Form 230 | Consent to Dual Agency | When listing agent also represents buyer | Must be signed before dual agency commences |

**Form 210 — the Agency Disclosure — must be presented at the first substantive contact with any prospective buyer.** This is the gateway document. Without it, nothing that follows has proper foundational disclosure. Agents who skip Form 210 and jump straight to Form 220 are technically out of sequence, which creates an exploitable gap in any later dispute.

Form 220 requires specific fields be completed: the names of all parties, the brokerage firm name and license number, a definite start and end date, the geographic or property-type scope of representation, and the compensation terms including the exact amount or method of calculating the buyer's agent fee. Blank fields or "TBD" entries do not satisfy the regulation.

## Compensation Disclosure: The Post-Settlement Reality

Since August 2024, buyer agent compensation can no longer be communicated through MLS cooperative compensation offers in Kentucky. This means Form 220 and Form 225 must contain clear, specific compensation terms that the buyer acknowledges and agrees to before touring.

The compensation section must disclose one of three structures: a flat fee, an hourly rate, or a percentage of the purchase price. The form must also state whether the buyer will owe any compensation if the seller agrees to pay the buyer's agent fee at closing. Kentucky agents cannot leave this ambiguous — the buyer must understand their potential financial obligation before signing.

**Agents should note that KRS 324.160 prohibits any licensee from receiving compensation from more than one party in a transaction without the written consent of all parties.** This means that if a seller offers to pay the buyer's agent and the buyer has also agreed to pay, the agent must disclose this double-compensation scenario and obtain written consent from both sides. Failure to do so is a license violation, full stop.

This is an area where [understanding how deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) becomes essential knowledge — compensation disputes rooted in inadequate disclosure are one of the fastest-growing categories of KREC complaints.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for noncompliance fall into three categories: regulatory, transactional, and civil.

On the regulatory side, KREC can issue formal reprimands, impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under KRS 324.160, require additional continuing education, suspend a license for up to three years, or permanently revoke it. The Commission's 2025 enforcement data shows a 34% increase in buyer agency-related complaints compared to 2023, and the trend is accelerating.

Transactionally, a missing or defective buyer agency agreement can void a purchase contract. Kentucky courts have held that an agent operating without proper written authority may not be entitled to enforce contractual provisions — including commission claims — against either party. If a seller's attorney discovers the buyer's agent never had a valid representation agreement, it provides grounds to challenge the agent's standing in the transaction entirely.

Civil liability is the third rail. Buyers who feel they were inadequately informed about compensation obligations or who believe an implied agency existed without proper documentation can pursue claims for breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, or negligent misrepresentation. These claims survive regardless of whether the transaction closes.

## Common Mistakes Kentucky Agents Make

After reviewing KREC disciplinary actions and speaking with compliance officers at Kentucky brokerages, five recurring errors stand out.

**First, agents present Form 220 at the wrong time.** The regulation requires the agreement to be signed before touring activity begins. Agents who have buyers sign at the offer stage — or worse, at closing — have operated without valid authorization for the entire showing period. KREC treats retroactive agreements as equivalent to no agreement at all.

Second, agents fail to complete the compensation section with specificity. Writing "standard commission" or "per MLS" no longer works. The form must state a concrete number or a precise calculation method. KREC has flagged multiple cases where vague language was deemed a material omission.

Third, agents do not update or re-execute agreements when terms change. If a buyer initially agrees to a 2.5% fee and the parties later negotiate a different arrangement, the original Form 220 must be amended in writing. Verbal modifications are unenforceable and create audit failures.

Fourth, agents use outdated form versions. KAR updated Forms 220 and 225 in early 2025 to incorporate post-settlement language. Agents using pre-2025 versions may be missing required disclosure fields, which KREC considers a deficiency even if the agent verbally covers the missing information.

Fifth, agents serving on teams fail to identify the correct licensee on the form. The agreement must name the principal broker or the specific agent authorized to provide services — not just a team name. [Team supervision rules](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) vary by state, but Kentucky requires individual licensee identification on all client agreements.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear direct supervisory liability under KRS 324.141 for the actions of their affiliated agents. A principal broker cannot claim ignorance of an agent's failure to obtain proper buyer representation agreements. KREC holds the broker accountable for maintaining systems that ensure compliance.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency | Red Flag |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|----------|
| Agreement timing | Signed date vs. first showing date | Every transaction | Showing occurred before agreement date |
| Compensation specificity | Dollar amount or percentage stated clearly | Every transaction | "TBD," blanks, or references to MLS |
| Expiration dates | Definite end date present | Monthly review | Open-ended or missing dates |
| Form version | Current KAR-approved version used | Quarterly | Pre-2025 forms in active files |
| Dual agency consent | Form 230 executed when applicable | Every dual agency file | Dual agency without separate signed consent |

**Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint that triggers before any offer is submitted on behalf of a buyer.** This single checkpoint catches the vast majority of compliance failures before they become regulatory problems. If the buyer agency agreement is missing, expired, or defective at the offer stage, the brokerage still has time to correct it — barely.

Training alone is insufficient. Brokers need automated tracking systems that flag files where a buyer agency agreement has not been uploaded within 24 hours of a showing being scheduled. This is where [building systems that prevent missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) becomes a brokerage-level priority rather than an individual agent's responsibility.

## Duration, Termination, and Renewal

Kentucky does not impose a statutory maximum duration on buyer agency agreements, but KREC's published guidance strongly discourages agreements exceeding six months. Agreements with no end date are per se deficient under 201 KAR 11:121, which requires a "definite termination date."

Either party may terminate the agreement according to its terms. Form 220 includes a termination provision that typically requires written notice. However, agents must understand that termination does not eliminate the protection period clause — a provision stating that if the buyer purchases a property the agent showed during the agreement term, the agent is still owed compensation for a specified period after termination.

Protection period clauses must be reasonable. KREC has taken the position that protection periods exceeding 90 days after termination are presumptively unreasonable and may constitute an unfair practice. Agents drafting custom agreements should keep this guidance in mind to avoid disciplinary scrutiny.
