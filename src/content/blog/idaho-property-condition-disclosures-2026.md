# Idaho Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Getting Idaho property condition & environmental disclosures 2026 requirements wrong does not just expose your client to risk — it exposes your license. Idaho's statutory framework for property disclosures has remained relatively stable, but the enforcement environment and litigation patterns have shifted enough that complacency is a genuine threat. This post breaks down the exact forms, statutes, and procedures that Idaho agents and brokers must follow to stay compliant.

## The Statutory Foundation: Idaho Code Title 55, Chapter 25

Idaho's property condition disclosure requirements live in [Idaho Code Sections 55-2501 through 55-2518](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title55/T55CH25/). This chapter mandates that sellers of residential real property (defined as one to four dwelling units) provide buyers with a written property condition disclosure form before or at the time of contract execution.

**The statute is not optional, and it does not leave room for agents to "forget."** Section 55-2502 explicitly states that no transfer of residential property shall be made unless the transferor delivers the required disclosure. The only exceptions carved out in Section 55-2504 include foreclosure transfers, court-ordered sales, transfers to co-owners or spouses, and transfers by fiduciaries in estate administration.

Agents sometimes assume that "as-is" sales eliminate the disclosure obligation. They do not. An as-is clause affects remedies and negotiation, but it does not override the statutory duty to disclose known material defects under Idaho law.

## The Forms: RE-25 and Federal Lead Paint Requirements

The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) oversees the forms used in residential transactions. The primary disclosure document is the **RE-25 Property Condition Disclosure Form**, which sellers must complete. This form covers structural systems, roof condition, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, water supply, sewage disposal, environmental hazards, and any known defects that materially affect the property's value.

The RE-25 is organized into categorical sections requiring the seller to mark conditions as "Yes," "No," or "Don't Know." Agents must understand that checking "Don't Know" does not insulate the seller from liability if a court later determines the seller reasonably should have known about a condition. Your job is to ensure sellers take the form seriously and complete every section honestly.

For properties built before 1978, federal law adds another layer. The lead-based paint disclosure under 42 U.S.C. Section 4852d requires sellers to disclose known lead-based paint or hazards, provide buyers with the EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home," and give buyers a 10-day opportunity to conduct a lead inspection. In Idaho, agents typically use the **RE-26 Lead-Based Paint Disclosure Form** to document compliance with this federal requirement.

| Form | Purpose | Required When | Governing Law |
|------|---------|---------------|---------------|
| RE-25 | Property Condition Disclosure | All residential sales (1-4 units) | Idaho Code 55-2502 |
| RE-26 | Lead-Based Paint Disclosure | Pre-1978 construction | 42 U.S.C. 4852d / 24 CFR 35 |
| EPA Pamphlet | Buyer education on lead hazards | Pre-1978 construction | 40 CFR 745.107 |

## Environmental Disclosures: Wells, Septic, and Beyond

Idaho does not have a freestanding environmental disclosure statute separate from the RE-25. However, the RE-25 form itself contains sections that function as environmental disclosures. Sellers must identify the water source (municipal, private well, shared well) and the sewage system (public sewer, septic tank, cesspool). Any known contamination, failed percolation tests, or water quality issues must be disclosed.

**For rural Idaho properties — which represent a massive share of transactions outside Ada and Canyon Counties — well and septic disclosures are where deals blow up.** A seller who knows the well tested positive for nitrates or that the septic drain field was flagged by the Central District Health Department has an affirmative duty to disclose. Silence is not a defense.

Radon is another environmental factor that surfaces in Idaho transactions. While Idaho does not mandate radon testing or disclosure by statute, the RE-25 asks whether the seller has knowledge of radon gas presence. If testing has been conducted, results should be disclosed. Agents working in areas with known elevated radon levels — much of the Treasure Valley and eastern Idaho — should counsel sellers to answer this section carefully.

Mold, asbestos, underground storage tanks, and proximity to Superfund sites (Idaho has several, including portions of the Bunker Hill Mining District in Shoshone County) all fall under the "environmental hazards" section of the RE-25. If a seller has actual knowledge of any of these conditions, non-disclosure creates liability.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance break into three categories: statutory rescission rights, civil liability, and disciplinary action from the Idaho Real Estate Commission.

Under Section 55-2512, if a seller fails to deliver the property condition disclosure, the buyer may terminate the purchase and sale agreement. This rescission right exists until three business days after the buyer receives the disclosure or until the closing, whichever occurs first. Deals have fallen apart at the closing table because an agent realized the disclosure was never delivered and the buyer exercised their statutory right to walk.

Civil liability can be far more expensive. Section 55-2517 preserves the buyer's right to sue for damages caused by a seller's failure to disclose material defects. Courts in Idaho have held that agents can share in this liability when they knew or should have known about a defect and failed to ensure proper disclosure. The Idaho Supreme Court's analysis in *Soward v. Rathbun* reinforced that agents owe duties that run parallel to, not merely derivative of, their client's obligations.

The Idaho Real Estate Commission can impose disciplinary sanctions under Idaho Code Section 54-2059, including fines, license suspension, or revocation. IREC has historically focused on disclosure failures as a category of "incompetence" or "dishonest dealing," both of which are grounds for discipline under the statute.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make With Disclosures

**First, agents frequently deliver the RE-25 after the purchase and sale agreement is already signed.** Idaho Code Section 55-2507 requires delivery "not later than the time the transferee makes a written offer." While some transactions proceed with post-offer delivery (triggering the buyer's rescission window), best practice — and the safest legal position — is pre-offer delivery. Agents who routinely deliver disclosures after acceptance are playing a timing game they will eventually lose.

Second, agents fill out portions of the RE-25 on behalf of sellers. This is a liability trap. The disclosure is the seller's document and the seller's representations. When an agent writes in answers based on their own inspection or conversations, they take ownership of those statements. If the information is wrong, the agent has just created independent liability for themselves and their brokerage.

Third, agents neglect the lead-based paint disclosure on pre-1978 properties, particularly in Idaho's older downtown neighborhoods in Boise, Nampa, Pocatello, and Idaho Falls. Skipping the RE-26 or failing to provide the EPA pamphlet triggers federal penalties of up to $19,507 per violation under HUD enforcement guidelines updated for 2026 inflation adjustments. This is not a state-level slap on the wrist — it is a federal fine.

Fourth, agents treat the "Don't Know" column as a safe harbor for sellers who simply do not want to answer questions. There is a difference between genuinely not knowing and willful ignorance. If a seller lived in the home for twenty years and checks "Don't Know" about whether the basement floods, that answer will not survive scrutiny in litigation. Agents should coach sellers on the legal distinction without filling in answers for them.

Fifth, agents fail to document delivery of the disclosure. Without a signed acknowledgment showing the date the buyer received the RE-25, proving compliance becomes a credibility contest. Always obtain dated signatures from the buyer confirming receipt, and keep copies in the transaction file.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Idaho Code Section 54-2038, which imposes a duty to supervise licensees. A broker who does not audit disclosure compliance across their agents' transactions is not just negligent — they are potentially liable for damages arising from their agents' failures.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| RE-25 delivery timing | Signed before or concurrent with offer | Every transaction |
| RE-26 presence for pre-1978 homes | Form completed, EPA pamphlet delivered, 10-day window documented | Every applicable transaction |
| Seller vs. agent handwriting | Ensure seller completed the form | Spot-check monthly |
| Buyer acknowledgment signatures | Dated receipts in file | Every transaction |
| Environmental section completeness | No blank fields on well, septic, radon, hazards | Every transaction |

Brokers should implement a file review process that catches missing or incomplete disclosures before closing, not after a complaint is filed. If your brokerage handles volume that makes manual review impractical, [automated compliance tracking](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) becomes essential rather than optional. The risk-to-cost ratio simply does not justify relying on agent self-policing.

Training matters too. Quarterly reviews of IREC disciplinary actions — which are published on the Commission's website — give brokers real-world examples to share with their teams. When agents see that other licensees lost their licenses over disclosure failures in their own state, the lesson sticks.
