## Understanding Idaho Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026

Missing a single contingency deadline in an Idaho residential transaction can cost your client their earnest money, expose your brokerage to an Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) complaint, or kill a deal that took months to build. Idaho buyer contingency deadlines 2026 are governed by a combination of state statute, IREC administrative rules, and the standardized forms published by Idaho REALTORS. If you are not tracking every date with precision, you are gambling with your license.

The stakes have increased in recent years as IREC has ramped up enforcement actions related to agent negligence on contractual timelines. The Commission processed over 130 formal complaints in the 2024-2025 cycle, and deadline mismanagement appeared in roughly one-third of those cases according to IREC annual reporting. This post breaks down the specific forms, deadlines, and compliance protocols every Idaho licensee needs to follow.

## The Forms That Control Idaho Contingency Timelines

Idaho REALTORS publishes the standard purchase and sale agreement form RE-21, which is the primary document establishing buyer contingency deadlines in most residential transactions. The RE-21 is not a state-mandated form — Idaho Code does not require its use — but the overwhelming majority of MLS-listed transactions in the state rely on it. When agents deviate from RE-21 by drafting custom language, they often create ambiguity that leads to disputes.

**Section 11 of the RE-21 governs inspection contingencies, Section 8 addresses financing contingencies, and Section 4 covers the appraisal contingency.** Each section requires a specific number of business days to be filled in, and those blanks cannot be left empty without creating a compliance risk. The companion form RE-10 (Buyer's Inspection Response) and RE-11 (Seller's Response to Buyer's Inspection) create the back-and-forth timeline that controls how inspection negotiations proceed after the initial deadline triggers.

Additional forms that interface with contingency deadlines include the RE-13 (Addendum), RE-16 (Counter Offer), and the RE-45 (Contingency Release). Agents must understand that each of these forms carries its own timing requirements that either extend or terminate the rights created under the original RE-21.

## How Idaho Business Day Calculations Work

The RE-21 defines business days as Monday through Friday, excluding Idaho state legal holidays. This definition matters enormously when a deadline falls near a holiday weekend. Idaho recognizes several state-specific holidays — including Idaho Human Rights Day (January) and an extended holiday schedule that differs from federal calendars — that can shift deadlines by one or more days.

**Day one of any contingency period begins the first business day after mutual acceptance of the purchase agreement.** Mutual acceptance is defined as the moment the last party signs the final version of the contract — not when the agent communicates acceptance, and not when the document is delivered. This distinction has been the subject of multiple IREC disciplinary proceedings.

| Contingency Type | Typical RE-21 Default | Day Count Begins | Form to Respond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inspection (Section 11) | 15 business days | First business day after acceptance | RE-10 |
| Financing (Section 8) | Negotiable (often 21-25 business days) | First business day after acceptance | Written notice |
| Appraisal (Section 4) | Tied to financing deadline | First business day after acceptance | Written notice |
| Sale of Buyer's Property | Negotiable | First business day after acceptance | RE-13 Addendum |
| HOA Document Review | 10 business days (if applicable) | Receipt of HOA documents | Written notice |

Counting errors are the single most common source of deadline disputes in Idaho transactions. Agents who count from the date of acceptance rather than the first business day after acceptance consistently arrive at the wrong date.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

Idaho Code [Section 54-2038](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title54/T54CH20/SECT54-2038/) grants IREC broad authority to discipline licensees for negligence, incompetence, or failure to exercise reasonable skill and care. Missing a contingency deadline on behalf of a client falls squarely within these grounds. The consequences range from informal citations to license revocation, depending on the severity and pattern of violations.

When a buyer's agent fails to submit a timely inspection response via RE-10, the buyer's inspection contingency is deemed satisfied under RE-21 language. The buyer loses their right to request repairs, renegotiate price, or terminate without penalty. If the buyer subsequently walks away, the seller may claim the earnest money — and the buyer's agent may face a malpractice claim for the amount of that deposit plus additional damages.

IREC fines for negligence-related violations typically range from $500 to $2,500 per violation, but the real financial exposure comes from civil liability. Idaho courts have consistently held that agents owe a fiduciary duty to track and enforce contractual deadlines, and failure to do so constitutes a breach of that duty. Errors and omissions insurance will often cover these claims, but repeated violations lead to policy cancellation or dramatically increased premiums.

> Idaho Code Section 54-2051(1)(a) provides that a licensee shall be subject to discipline for "performing or attempting to perform any of the acts regulated by this chapter in a manner that constitutes negligence, incompetence or breach of a fiduciary duty."

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make With Contingency Deadlines

The following errors appear repeatedly in IREC complaint files and in transaction audits conducted by managing brokers across the state. Each one is preventable with proper systems and attention to form language.

**First, agents frequently leave deadline blanks empty on the RE-21.** When Section 11 or Section 8 contains no number in the business days blank, the contingency period becomes ambiguous. IREC has taken the position that blank fields do not default to any standard number — they create a contract with an undefined term. Both parties then dispute whether the contingency ever existed at all, and the agent who prepared the form bears responsibility for the error.

Second, agents miscalculate business days by including weekends or failing to account for Idaho-specific holidays. A deadline that an agent believes falls on Thursday may actually fall on Friday if a state holiday shifted the count. This one-day error has ended transactions and triggered earnest money disputes that required mediation under IREC's dispute resolution process.

Third, agents deliver the RE-10 inspection response to the wrong party or via an unapproved delivery method. The RE-21 specifies acceptable delivery methods in its definitions section. Sending a text message with a photo of a signed RE-10 does not constitute proper delivery unless the parties have explicitly agreed to that method in writing. Agents who rely on informal communication channels risk having their timely response deemed untimely.

Fourth, agents confuse the inspection deadline with the inspection response deadline. The buyer must complete inspections and deliver the RE-10 within the same contingency period — these are not two separate timelines. Scheduling an inspection on day 14 of a 15-day period leaves no time to prepare and deliver a response form if issues arise.

Fifth, agents fail to confirm mutual acceptance timestamps. When multiple counter offers fly back and forth via RE-16 forms, identifying the exact moment of mutual acceptance requires tracing the signature chain. Agents who assume acceptance occurred "sometime Monday" rather than pinpointing the exact time on Monday risk starting their count from the wrong day. For more on [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the answer almost always traces back to poor documentation of these foundational timestamps.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in Idaho carry supervisory liability under Idaho Code Section 54-2038(1)(n), which allows IREC to discipline a broker who fails to adequately supervise licensees. This is not a theoretical risk. IREC has sanctioned brokers who had no system for reviewing active transaction deadlines, even when the individual agent bore primary fault for the missed date.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| RE-21 blank fields | All deadline blanks filled in with specific numbers | Every new contract |
| Mutual acceptance documentation | Timestamp of final signature confirmed in file | Within 24 hours of acceptance |
| Contingency calendar | Business day calculation verified against Idaho holiday schedule | Weekly review |
| RE-10 / RE-11 delivery confirmation | Proof of delivery method and timestamp in file | At each deadline |
| RE-45 Contingency Release | Filed before closing if contingencies satisfied | Pre-closing audit |

Brokers should establish a weekly pipeline review system that specifically flags upcoming contingency deadlines within the next five business days. A reactive approach — waiting until an agent reports a problem — is insufficient under IREC's supervisory standard. The [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) model works particularly well for Idaho brokerages managing 20 or more concurrent transactions.

**Transaction files must contain written evidence that each contingency deadline was either met or formally waived.** An IREC auditor reviewing a closed file will look for the RE-45 Contingency Release form or written documentation showing the buyer performed within the stated timeline. Files that lack this documentation create presumptive evidence of supervisory failure, even if the transaction closed without dispute.

Brokers should also audit their agents' use of the RE-13 Addendum when extending deadlines. Extensions must be signed by all parties before the original deadline expires — not after. A retroactive extension signed two days after the original deadline passed does not cure the buyer's failure to perform, and brokers who accept these documents into the file without noting the timing issue are compounding the compliance failure.

## Idaho-Specific Statutory Requirements Beyond the Forms

While the RE-21 controls most deadline mechanics, Idaho statute imposes additional timing requirements that intersect with buyer contingencies. Idaho Code Section 55-2504 through 55-2517 governs property condition disclosures, and the buyer has a right to receive and review the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Form within specific timeframes. If the seller delivers this form late, the buyer may gain additional time to evaluate the property — effectively extending their practical inspection window even if the RE-21 deadline has passed.

Idaho's lead-based paint disclosure requirements under federal law (42 USC 4852d) grant buyers a minimum 10-day inspection period for lead hazards in pre-1978 properties. This federal floor cannot be shortened by the RE-21, and agents who write inspection periods shorter than 10 days on qualifying properties violate federal law regardless of what the state form says. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with recognizing these overlapping regulatory layers.

The Idaho Property Condition Disclosure Act requires sellers to deliver disclosures "as soon as practicable" but does not set a hard calendar deadline. This ambiguity means that buyer agents must track disclosure receipt independently and understand how late disclosures interact with their client's contingency rights under the purchase agreement.
