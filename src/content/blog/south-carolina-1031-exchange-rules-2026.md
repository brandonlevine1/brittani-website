## Why South Carolina 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The phrase "South Carolina 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026" should be on every listing agent's radar this year—not because the state overhauled its tax code, but because federal proposals to cap or eliminate Section 1031 deferrals have pushed more investors to close exchanges before potential legislative deadlines. South Carolina's conformity to the Internal Revenue Code means state-level deferral hinges entirely on whether the federal exchange holds up. If an exchange collapses, your client faces both federal and state capital gains tax, and you face a very uncomfortable phone call.

**South Carolina follows IRC Section 1031 through its general conformity statute, S.C. Code Ann. Section 12-6-50.** This provision adopts the Internal Revenue Code as amended through the most recent taxable year, which means any qualifying like-kind exchange recognized by the IRS is also recognized by the South Carolina Department of Revenue (SCDOR). There is no separate state-level 1031 form or state-specific qualification test—but that simplicity is deceptive, because the withholding rules create traps that catch unprepared agents every quarter.

## South Carolina's Nonresident Withholding and the 1031 Exemption

The single biggest compliance issue agents encounter is [nonresident withholding under S.C. Code Ann. Section 12-8-580](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t12c008.php). South Carolina requires withholding agents—typically the closing attorney—to collect 7% of the gain (or, if gain cannot be determined, 7% of the gross sales price) from nonresident sellers at closing. This withholding applies to individuals, partnerships, and S corporations selling real property in the state.

For 1031 exchanges, the seller may claim an exemption from withholding by executing SCDOR Form I-295, the "Nonresident Real Estate Withholding Exemption" affidavit. The seller must certify under penalty of perjury that the transaction qualifies as a like-kind exchange under IRC Section 1031. Once the closing attorney receives a properly completed I-295, the withholding obligation is released.

Here is where agents get involved: you are not the withholding agent, but you are often the person who explains these requirements to out-of-state investors or refers them to a qualified intermediary (QI). If you tell a seller they "don't need to worry about South Carolina taxes" without mentioning the I-295 or the 7% withholding, you've set them up for a cash shortfall at closing—or worse, a failed exchange because funds were incorrectly disbursed.

## Critical Deadlines That Overlap With State Filing Obligations

Federal 1031 timelines are well-known: 45 days to identify replacement property, 180 days to close. South Carolina adds no additional timeline requirements, but state filing obligations can intersect with those deadlines in ways that confuse clients and agents alike.

| Deadline | Federal Requirement | South Carolina Requirement |
|----------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Day 0 | Relinquished property closes; proceeds go to QI | I-295 filed with closing attorney OR 7% withheld and remitted on Form SC-1041 |
| Day 45 | Identification of replacement property | No state-specific action required |
| Day 180 | Replacement property acquired | No state-specific action required |
| April 15 (or extension) | Form 8824 filed with IRS | SC1040 (or SC1065/SC1120S) filed conforming to federal treatment |
| Within 30 days of closing | N/A | Closing attorney remits withheld amount to SCDOR if I-295 was NOT filed |

**Agents must understand that the I-295 must be executed before or at the closing of the relinquished property—not after.** If the closing attorney withholds the 7% because no affidavit was provided, those funds are sent to SCDOR. The seller can recover them by filing a South Carolina return, but the money is tied up for months, potentially disrupting the replacement purchase.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on South Carolina 1031 Exchanges

Experienced agents still stumble on these issues because 1031 exchanges sit at the intersection of tax law, real estate law, and transaction management. Here are five specific errors that surface repeatedly in South Carolina transactions.

**First, agents confuse the closing attorney's role with the qualified intermediary's role.** In South Carolina, attorneys handle closings—not title companies acting independently. The closing attorney serves as the withholding agent under Section 12-8-580, but the QI is the party that holds exchange proceeds under the exchange agreement. When agents direct their clients to "just have the attorney handle the 1031," they're conflating two distinct functions, which can lead to constructive receipt problems that disqualify the entire exchange.

Second, agents fail to flag the I-295 requirement during listing consultations with nonresident sellers. By the time the seller's CPA learns about the 7% withholding at the closing table, it's too late to get proper documentation in place. The deal doesn't fall apart—but the relationship does.

Third, agents attempt to give tax advice about whether a property qualifies as "like-kind." Under South Carolina Real Estate Commission Rule 27-1301 through 27-1399, licensees may not provide legal or tax advice beyond their competence. Telling a client that their vacation rental "definitely qualifies" for a 1031 exchange is practicing tax law without a license—and it exposes your brokerage to liability if the exchange is later disqualified by SCDOR or the IRS.

Fourth, agents overlook partial exchanges. When a client pulls some cash (boot) out of the transaction, that boot is taxable at both the federal and state level. South Carolina taxes boot as ordinary capital gain income at the applicable individual rate (currently up to 6.5%, though the state is phasing rates down under H.4880). Agents who don't mention this possibility leave clients blindsided at tax time.

Fifth, agents neglect to confirm that the QI is properly bonded and meets the safe harbor requirements of Treasury Regulation 1.1031(k)-1(g)(4). South Carolina has no state-specific QI licensing requirement, which means the due diligence burden falls entirely on the seller and their advisors—including you, the recommending agent.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

If you manage a brokerage in South Carolina, your compliance exposure on 1031 exchanges is real. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) can discipline licensees for misrepresentation, incompetence, or conduct demonstrating unfitness under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-145. A pattern of agents giving improper 1031 advice reflects on the broker's supervision.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk If Missed |
|------------------|---------------|----------------|
| Agent communications about tax treatment | Email/text references to "tax-free" language | Unauthorized practice of law; SCREC complaint |
| I-295 coordination with closing attorneys | Confirm nonresident sellers were advised pre-listing | Withheld funds, delayed closings, client complaints |
| QI referral documentation | Verify agents are not receiving referral fees from QIs | Undisclosed compensation; Section 40-57-145 violation |
| File documentation of exchange status | Transaction files note 1031 intent from contract stage | E&O claims if exchange fails due to procedural error |
| Continuing education tracking | Agents advising on exchanges have completed relevant CE | Competency defense weakened in disciplinary hearing |

**Brokers should require that any transaction flagged as a 1031 exchange includes a written disclaimer in the file.** This disclaimer—signed by the client—should state that the agent has not provided tax or legal advice and that the client is relying on their own CPA, attorney, and QI for exchange compliance. This single document can prevent an E&O claim from gaining traction.

Tracking these details across dozens of active files is exactly the kind of [operational challenge that causes agents to miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines). A structured system—whether manual or automated—keeps the brokerage's risk profile manageable.

## Liability Exposure When Things Go Wrong

What actually happens when an agent's error contributes to a failed 1031 exchange? The financial consequences are severe. A seller who expected to defer $150,000 in capital gains now owes federal tax (up to 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax) and South Carolina income tax on the full gain. That's potentially $35,700 or more in combined taxes that the seller blames on you.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission has authority to issue fines, require additional education, suspend licenses, or revoke licenses entirely under Section 40-57-145. While SCREC typically reserves revocation for fraud or repeated violations, a single well-documented complaint about misleading 1031 guidance can result in a formal reprimand and mandatory coursework. Your E&O insurance may cover the financial claim, but it won't cover the reputational damage or the license action.

Beyond SCREC, the South Carolina Department of Revenue can pursue the closing attorney (as withholding agent) for failure to collect under Section 12-8-590, which imposes liability equal to the amount that should have been withheld, plus penalties and interest. If the closing attorney can demonstrate that the I-295 affidavit was improperly obtained due to an agent's misinformation, the attorney may pursue the agent or brokerage for contribution. This is not theoretical—it happens in multi-million-dollar investment transactions where the stakes justify litigation.

## How to Protect Yourself and Your Clients in 2026

The best protection is clear boundaries. Tell clients that you can identify a transaction as a potential 1031 exchange candidate. Tell them you can coordinate timing, introduce them to qualified intermediaries, and ensure the closing attorney has the right documentation. Then stop. Do not calculate their tax liability. Do not guarantee deferral. Do not tell them whether their mixed-use property qualifies.

Build a referral network of exchange-experienced CPAs and QIs. Document every referral. Keep a template in your transaction files that captures the client's stated exchange intent at the contract stage, so the closing attorney knows to prepare for I-295 execution well before the closing date.
