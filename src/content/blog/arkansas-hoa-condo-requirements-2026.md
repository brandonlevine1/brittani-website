## Understanding Arkansas HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Arkansas agents who handle condominium or HOA-governed properties face a disclosure framework that sits across two separate statutory schemes—and most agents conflate them. The **Arkansas hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** affect every transaction involving deed-restricted communities, from a $90,000 townhome in Jonesboro to a $1.2 million condo in Bentonville. Getting the paperwork wrong doesn't just delay closings; it exposes your license to formal complaints with the Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC).

The governing statutes haven't changed dramatically in structure since the Arkansas Condominium Act was enacted, but fee schedules, AREC enforcement posture, and contract addendum language have all shifted heading into 2026. This piece breaks down what you actually owe your client, where the liability sits, and what your broker should be auditing on every HOA-affected file.

## The Two Statutes You Must Know

Arkansas splits its HOA/condo disclosure obligations between two code sections, and each applies to different property types. Conflating them is the fastest way to deliver the wrong documents.

**The Arkansas Condominium Act (A.C.A. 18-13-101 through 18-13-120)** governs units within a declared condominium regime. It requires the seller—or the association on the seller's behalf—to furnish a resale certificate that includes current assessments, any pending special assessments, the association's financial statements, and copies of the declaration and bylaws. The buyer's right to cancel is tied directly to receipt of this certificate.

The **Arkansas Planned Community Act (A.C.A. 18-12-101 et seq.)** covers non-condominium planned unit developments—subdivisions with mandatory HOA membership but without the condominium declaration structure. Disclosure obligations under this section focus on assessment amounts, lien rights, and governing restrictions. Agents who only think about "condo docs" miss an entire category of transactions where disclosure is still legally required.

| Statute | Property Type | Key Document | Buyer Cancel Right |
|---------|--------------|--------------|-------------------|
| A.C.A. 18-13-104 | Condominiums | Resale Certificate | Yes, if not received before contract execution or within agreed period |
| A.C.A. 18-12-104 | Planned Communities (HOA) | Association Disclosure Package | Yes, per contract terms; no standalone statutory rescission period |
| AREC Contract Addendum | Both | HOA/Condo Addendum (Form 5) | Per negotiated terms in addendum |

The distinction matters because condo buyers have a slightly stronger statutory cancellation right than planned-community buyers, whose rights depend more heavily on contract language. If you represent a buyer in a non-condo HOA community and fail to negotiate a contingency period in writing, your client may have no statutory escape hatch if damaging information surfaces late.

## AREC Forms and the Addendum Agents Must Use

The Arkansas Real Estate Commission publishes standardized contract forms through its approved forms program. For HOA and condo transactions, the relevant addendum is the **AREC HOA/PUD Addendum**, sometimes referenced internally as Form 5 in brokerage compliance systems. This addendum attaches to the standard AREC Purchase Agreement and covers the disclosure delivery timeline, which party bears the cost of obtaining documents, and the buyer's review period.

**Agents must attach this addendum to every purchase contract involving a property governed by a mandatory association—no exceptions.** Omitting it doesn't void the disclosure obligation under state law, but it does create an ambiguity gap that makes disputes nearly impossible to resolve cleanly. AREC's [published forms and practice standards](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/) are the authoritative source.

Within the addendum, the parties negotiate a specific number of days for the seller to deliver the resale certificate or disclosure package. If that deadline passes without delivery, the buyer may terminate and recover earnest money. The addendum also addresses transfer fees, working capital contributions, and any outstanding violations the association has levied against the property.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Liability for missing or late HOA disclosures in Arkansas flows to multiple parties, but the listing agent and their supervising broker are the first targets when AREC investigates.

Under AREC Rule 10.10, licensees must exercise reasonable skill and care in handling documents material to a transaction. Failing to attach the HOA addendum, neglecting to order the resale certificate, or misrepresenting association fees all constitute potential violations. Sanctions range from a formal letter of reprimand to license suspension, depending on the severity and pattern of behavior.

Beyond AREC discipline, agents face civil liability. A buyer who closes without receiving mandated disclosures—and later discovers a $15,000 special assessment or pending litigation against the association—can pursue damages against the seller and potentially the listing agent for negligent omission. Arkansas courts have recognized that agents owe a duty to disclose material facts they know or should have known, and HOA financial distress qualifies.

| Violation | Potential Consequence |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Failure to attach HOA/PUD Addendum | AREC reprimand; contract ambiguity exposing both parties |
| Late delivery of resale certificate | Buyer termination right; earnest money refund; possible AREC complaint |
| Misrepresenting monthly dues or special assessments | Civil fraud claim; AREC license suspension |
| Failing to disclose known association litigation | Civil liability for damages; AREC investigation |
| Broker failure to audit HOA compliance on files | Supervisory liability under A.C.A. 17-42-311 |

Deal cancellation is the most common immediate consequence. Agents who have experienced a deal falling apart 48 hours before closing because the resale certificate revealed a roof replacement levy understand the financial pain. That commission loss often dwarfs any fine AREC would impose.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA Disclosures

Three to five errors recur with alarming frequency in Arkansas HOA transactions. Recognizing them before they hit your file is the difference between a smooth close and a regulatory headache.

**First, agents assume the title company will order the resale certificate.** Title companies in Arkansas do not universally handle this task. Some will order estoppel letters for payoff purposes, but the full resale certificate required under A.C.A. 18-13-104—including financial statements, meeting minutes, and pending litigation disclosures—must be separately requested. If your listing agreement doesn't specify who orders it, the responsibility defaults to the seller, and by extension, to the listing agent who should be advising them.

Second, agents confuse estoppel letters with resale certificates. An estoppel letter confirms the current balance owed by the seller. A resale certificate is a far more detailed document that includes the association's budget, reserve fund status, insurance coverage, and any known defects in common elements. Delivering only the estoppel letter does not satisfy the statute.

Third, agents negotiate unrealistic delivery timelines in the addendum. Many Arkansas HOAs are self-managed by volunteer boards that may take 10 to 14 business days to compile documents. Writing a five-day delivery window into the addendum sets up an automatic termination trigger that benefits no one except a buyer looking for an exit.

Fourth, agents fail to review the documents once received. Simply forwarding a PDF to the buyer without reading it yourself means you won't catch red flags—deferred maintenance reserves below 10 percent, pending lawsuits, or annual assessment increases that contradict what you listed in the MLS. Your duty of reasonable skill and care under [AREC rules](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) doesn't end at document delivery; it includes flagging material concerns.

Fifth, agents neglect to document delivery in the transaction file. AREC investigators who audit files want to see dated proof that the buyer received the resale certificate within the contracted timeframe. A verbal handoff at the kitchen table won't survive scrutiny. Use email delivery with read receipts, or a transaction management platform that timestamps document access.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Arkansas carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under A.C.A. 17-42-311. This isn't theoretical—AREC has disciplined brokers who failed to maintain adequate supervisory systems, even when the individual agent was the one who omitted the disclosure.

**Brokers should build a mandatory checklist item into their transaction review process that flags any property within a mandatory association.** That flag should trigger verification of three things: the HOA/PUD Addendum is attached to the executed contract, the resale certificate or disclosure package has been ordered with a tracked delivery date, and the buyer's signed acknowledgment of receipt is in the file before the contingency deadline expires.

Quarterly file audits should specifically sample HOA transactions. Pull five to ten closed files per quarter and verify that the addendum was used, that the resale certificate is present in the file, and that the timeline documented in the addendum was actually honored. Brokers who discover a pattern of missing documents can intervene with training before AREC discovers the gap during a routine office audit.

Brokers should also maintain a reference list of management companies and self-managed associations in their market area, including typical turnaround times for document requests. ## How 2026 Enforcement Trends Affect Your Practice

AREC's enforcement posture has shifted measurably toward documentation audits in the past two years. The commission's 2025 annual report flagged incomplete transaction files—particularly missing addenda and disclosure acknowledgments—as a top area of concern. Heading into 2026, brokers should expect more frequent random audits and specific attention to HOA-related paperwork.

Additionally, several large management companies operating in Northwest Arkansas and Central Arkansas have increased their resale certificate fees, with some now charging $350 to $400 per package. This cost increase makes the addendum's fee-allocation provision more contentious in negotiations. Listing agents who fail to disclose these costs upfront create surprises that erode client trust and can trigger renegotiation demands at the worst possible time.

The trend toward electronic delivery has also created an unexpected compliance gap. Some management companies deliver resale certificates through portal links that expire after 30 days. If your file doesn't contain a permanent copy—either a downloaded PDF or a printed version—you'll have nothing to produce during an AREC audit two years post-closing. ## Practical Workflow for Arkansas HOA Transactions

A disciplined workflow eliminates most disclosure failures before they start. On the listing side, confirm HOA or condo status at the listing appointment. Request a copy of the most recent budget and assessment schedule from the seller immediately—don't wait for a buyer to materialize. Pre-ordering the resale certificate, or at least confirming the management company's turnaround time, puts you weeks ahead of the contract timeline.

On the buyer side, write realistic review periods into the addendum. Seven to ten calendar days after delivery of the resale certificate gives your client time to consult with their lender about assessment impacts on qualification and to ask questions about reserve adequacy. Build a reminder system that pings you two days before the buyer's review period expires, because silence does not equal acceptance under the addendum language—the buyer must affirmatively approve or terminate.
