## Understanding New Hampshire Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Real estate closings in New Hampshire follow a pattern that differs meaningfully from neighboring states, and agents who treat the process as generic risk both their licenses and their clients' money. The framework governing **New Hampshire title & closing procedures 2026** sits primarily within RSA 356-C (the Real Estate Practice Act), RSA 478 (recording requirements), and RSA 311:7 (unauthorized practice of law). Agents operating in the Granite State must understand where their role ends and where attorney or title company authority begins.

New Hampshire is not a pure "attorney state" like Massachusetts, nor is it a fully deregulated title-company state like many in the West. The result is a hybrid system where closings may be conducted by attorneys, title companies, or lender-approved settlement agents — but certain functions remain exclusively within the domain of licensed attorneys. This distinction trips up agents who relocate from other jurisdictions or hold multi-state licenses.

The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), operating under RSA 331-A, enforces agent conduct during transactions. Agents should also be aware that the NH Joint Board of Licensure and Certification oversees administrative enforcement actions that can result in fines up to $2,000 per violation under RSA 331-A:28.

## The Title Search Process: What Agents Must Know

**Title searches in New Hampshire are conducted through the county Registry of Deeds, and the state's ten counties each maintain separate recording systems.** Some counties — notably Hillsborough and Rockingham — have digitized records extensively, while others still require in-person examination for older instruments. Agents should set client expectations accordingly when properties have chains of title extending before the digital cutoff.

A standard title examination in NH covers a minimum 50-year search period, though many title companies and attorneys search back to the original patent. The search reviews recorded deeds, mortgages, liens, attachments, judgments, tax liens, and encumbrances filed at both the county registry and the federal district court. New Hampshire does not have a state-level lien database, so searchers must cross-reference multiple sources.

Title insurance in New Hampshire is regulated by the NH Insurance Department, not the Real Estate Commission. Rates are not filed or promulgated by the state, meaning they are negotiable between the insurer and the consumer. Agents should never represent that title insurance rates are "standard" or "fixed" — doing so could constitute a misrepresentation under [RSA 331-A:26](https://www.gencourt.state.nh.us/rsa/html/xxx/331-a/331-a-mrg.htm).

## Forms and Documents Agents Handle in New Hampshire

The New Hampshire Association of Realtors (NHAR) publishes standardized transaction forms that most brokerages require agents to use. The key forms touching title and closing include NHAR Form 60 (Purchase and Sale Agreement), NHAR Form 62 (Addendum to Purchase and Sale Agreement), and NHAR Form 50 (Offer to Purchase/Deposit Receipt). Each form contains provisions related to title quality, deed type, and closing timeline that agents must understand before presenting to clients.

NHAR Form 60, Section 12, specifically addresses title standards — requiring the seller to deliver marketable title by warranty deed unless otherwise specified. Agents who modify this section without attorney guidance risk creating ambiguities that kill deals at closing. The form also references the NH Title Examination Standards published by the Real Property Section of the NH Bar Association, which define what constitutes a "marketable" title defect versus one a buyer must accept.

Recording fees in New Hampshire follow RSA 478:17-g, which sets the per-page fee for documents recorded at the Registry of Deeds. As of 2026, the standard recording fee is $15 for the first page and $4 for each additional page, plus a $25 surcharge for documents that transfer real property interests. Agents frequently underestimate these costs on net sheets, particularly when multiple documents require recording.

## Closing Day: Roles, Responsibilities, and Compliance

| Responsibility | Buyer's Side | Seller's Side |
|---|---|---|
| Title search and commitment | Buyer pays (customary) | Provides access to prior title policy |
| Deed preparation | Attorney or title company | Seller's attorney drafts |
| Transfer tax (RSA 78-B) | Split equally by custom | Split equally by custom |
| Recording fees | Buyer pays for deed/mortgage | Seller pays for discharge |
| Settlement statement review | Agent reviews with buyer | Agent reviews with seller |
| Wire transfer coordination | Buyer's lender initiates | Seller confirms receipt |

**New Hampshire's real estate transfer tax under RSA 78-B is currently $15.00 per $1,000 of the price or consideration, split equally between buyer and seller.** This tax applies to all transfers unless a specific statutory exemption exists (such as transfers between spouses or to certain trusts). Agents who misquote this figure or fail to disclose it on preliminary net sheets face complaints to NHREC.

The closing itself typically occurs at the office of the title company, the buyer's attorney, or — in some cases — the lender's designated settlement agent. New Hampshire permits remote online notarization (RON) under RSA 456-B:8-a, enacted in 2020, which allows certain documents to be executed via audio-visual technology. Agents should confirm with the settlement agent whether RON is available for a specific transaction, as not all counties have uniformly accepted RON-executed documents for recording.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in New Hampshire Closings

The first error is drafting or modifying deed language. Even adding a seemingly minor clause — like specifying a right-of-way or correcting a legal description — constitutes the practice of law under RSA 311:7. NHREC has disciplined agents for this, and title companies will refuse to insure transactions where non-attorneys have altered deed instruments. The fix is straightforward: any deed modification must go through a licensed New Hampshire attorney.

The second mistake involves [missing critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) tied to title contingency periods. Under NHAR Form 60, buyers typically have a specified number of days to review the title commitment and raise objections. If the agent fails to calendar this deadline and the period lapses, the buyer may lose the right to object to title defects — effectively waiving the contingency and exposing the client to undisclosed encumbrances.

**Third, agents routinely miscalculate the transfer tax or omit it entirely from preliminary estimates.** At $15 per $1,000, a $450,000 property generates $6,750 in transfer tax — $3,375 per side. Errors here damage client trust and can derail closings when buyers discover unexpected costs at the table. Agents should double-check their math against the Department of Revenue Administration's published calculation worksheets.

Fourth, agents sometimes direct earnest money deposits into accounts that do not comply with RSA 331-A:22, which requires that deposits be placed in a New Hampshire bank or credit union escrow account within five banking days. Mishandling escrow triggers automatic NHREC investigation authority and potential license suspension. For a deeper look at [how deals fall apart at the transaction level](/blog/where-deals-break-down), this particular failure is among the most avoidable.

Fifth, agents provide informal "title opinions" to clients by reviewing registry records themselves and offering assurances about title quality. This unauthorized practice exposes the agent to both regulatory discipline and civil malpractice claims if the opinion proves incorrect.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in New Hampshire bear supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:25-b. If an agent under their supervision commits a violation during a title or closing procedure, the broker may face separate disciplinary action for failure to supervise. This is not theoretical — NHREC has issued sanctions against brokers who lacked systems to catch agent non-compliance.

| Audit Area | What Brokers Should Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Escrow account deposits | Deposited within 5 banking days per RSA 331-A:22 | Every transaction |
| Form usage | NHAR Forms 50, 60, 62 used without unauthorized modification | Monthly file review |
| Deadline tracking | Title contingency dates calendared and communicated | Weekly check-ins |
| Transfer tax estimates | Net sheets match RSA 78-B calculations | Before client delivery |
| Attorney referral compliance | No kickbacks or undisclosed relationships per RESPA | Quarterly audit |

**Brokers should implement a file-review system that flags any transaction where the title contingency deadline passes without documented buyer acknowledgment.** Relying on agents to self-report missed deadlines invites liability. A structured [checklist-based approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) catches gaps before they become complaints.

Additionally, brokers must verify that their agents are not receiving undisclosed referral fees from title companies or settlement agents. The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA), 12 USC 2607, prohibits kickbacks for referrals of settlement services. NHREC treats RESPA violations as grounds for state-level discipline independent of any federal enforcement action.

## Wire Fraud and Settlement Fund Security

Wire fraud targeting real estate closings has increased sharply across New Hampshire, with sophisticated schemes impersonating title companies, attorneys, and even agents. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported over $145 million in real estate wire fraud losses nationally in recent years, and New Hampshire is not immune. Agents must verify wire instructions through independent phone calls — never relying solely on email confirmations.

RSA 359-E (the Consumer Protection Act) provides a cause of action for buyers harmed by negligent handling of settlement funds, and agents who forward unverified wire instructions may find themselves named in civil suits. Best practice dictates confirming wiring details by calling the title company at a number independently obtained — not one provided in the suspicious email. Settlement agents in NH increasingly require verbal callbacks before releasing funds.

Brokers should enforce a firm-wide policy requiring dual verification of all wire instructions. Training on social engineering tactics should occur at least annually, with documentation that agents have acknowledged the policy in writing.
