The way Hawaii regulates real estate teams differs from the mainland in several material ways, and the consequences for getting it wrong range from deal cancellations to license revocation. Understanding Hawaii teams & supervision rules 2026 is not optional — it is the baseline for operating legally in the state. The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC), operating under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), has tightened its enforcement posture in recent years, and teams that operated informally in the past are now squarely in the crosshairs.

## How Hawaii Defines Teams Under HRS Chapter 467

Hawaii does not have a standalone "team license." The state's real estate licensing framework, codified in Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467, recognizes only three license categories: principal broker, broker-salesperson, and salesperson. Every licensee must be affiliated with a principal broker, and every team — regardless of branding — is legally nothing more than a group of individual licensees operating under one brokerage.

This means a team leader who is a salesperson holds no additional legal authority over other salespersons on the team. The principal broker remains the sole supervisory authority under HRS Section 467-1.6. Team leaders cannot sign listing agreements on behalf of the brokerage, cannot hold earnest money, and cannot independently authorize advertising without principal broker approval.

HIREC has issued multiple guidance letters reinforcing that team names are permissible only when they include the brokerage name or are used in conjunction with it. Hawaii Administrative Rules (HAR) Section 16-99-3(f) specifically requires that all advertising — including team advertising — prominently display the name under which the principal broker is registered.

## Supervision Standards the Principal Broker Must Meet

**The principal broker's supervisory duty in Hawaii is among the most expansive in the nation.** HAR Section 16-99-3 imposes a "reasonable supervision" standard, which HIREC has interpreted to mean direct, documented oversight of every transaction file, every advertising piece, and every client communication that could create liability.

This is not a rubber-stamp obligation. In disciplinary proceedings published by HIREC in 2024 and 2025, the Commission sanctioned principal brokers who relied exclusively on team leaders to review files. The Commission held that delegation of review duties is permissible only when the principal broker maintains a secondary audit layer and documents that audit in writing.

Principal brokers must also ensure compliance with HIREC's mandatory disclosure forms. The Disclosure of Agency Relationships form (DCCA Form REC-7) and the Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement (DCCA Form SRPD-2) must appear in every applicable transaction file. Missing forms are not just a paperwork issue — they can void contracts under HRS Section 508D-4 if a buyer was not properly informed.

## Advertising Rules That Trip Up Teams

Hawaii's advertising rules are deceptively specific. Under HAR Section 16-99-3(f), every advertisement must include the brokerage name as registered with HIREC. Team names and individual agent names may appear, but they cannot be more prominent than the brokerage name. Social media posts, yard signs, and digital ads all fall under this rule.

| Advertising Element | Required? | Rule Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name as registered | Yes | HAR 16-99-3(f) |
| Team name | Optional | Must not exceed brokerage name prominence |
| Individual agent name | Optional | Must include license status |
| Principal broker name | Not required in ads | Required on contracts |
| Phone/email | Optional | Must route to brokerage-controlled channels |

The distinction between "team branding" and "brokerage identity" causes more violations than any other single rule. HIREC's enforcement database shows that advertising non-compliance accounts for roughly 30 percent of all disciplinary actions against salespersons affiliated with teams. Fines start at $500 per occurrence and can escalate to $2,500 for repeat violations, with license suspension possible after three documented violations within a 12-month period.

Teams operating across multiple islands face additional complexity. Each advertisement must reference the brokerage location that holds supervisory responsibility, and HIREC has flagged instances where teams used a Maui brokerage address in ads for Oahu listings without proper branch office registration.

## Common Mistakes That Lead to Enforcement Actions

**Agents operating on teams in Hawaii repeatedly make the same five errors, and most are entirely preventable.** Here is what HIREC enforcement records reveal as the top violations among team-affiliated licensees.

First, team leaders signing listing agreements as if they hold broker authority. Only the principal broker or a broker-salesperson with explicit written authorization from the principal broker may execute listing agreements on behalf of the firm. When a salesperson team leader signs a listing, the contract may be voidable, and the seller could refuse to pay commission at closing.

Second, teams maintaining shadow trust accounts. Some high-producing teams have attempted to hold earnest money in accounts controlled by the team leader rather than the brokerage's official trust account. This violates HRS Section 467-14(8) and constitutes grounds for immediate license revocation. HIREC treats commingling and conversion of trust funds as among the most serious offenses.

Third, failing to disclose the supervisory relationship on the Disclosure of Agency Relationships form (REC-7). The form requires identification of the principal broker, and agents who list only their team leader as the "supervising broker" create a material misrepresentation. Buyers and sellers are entitled to know who actually holds supervisory responsibility.

Fourth, using unlicensed team assistants to perform activities that require a license. Under HRS Section 467-7, any person who negotiates, lists, sells, or offers to sell real property must hold a valid license. Teams that allow unlicensed assistants to conduct showings, discuss pricing with clients, or draft counteroffers expose the entire brokerage to liability.

Fifth, teams failing to provide the Consumer Guide to Agency Relationships at first substantive contact. This is a separate obligation from the REC-7 form and must occur before any confidential information is exchanged. Teams with rapid lead-response systems sometimes skip this step when an ISA or team member responds to an inquiry.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in 2026

The principal broker's audit obligations are not aspirational — they carry personal liability. If a team member violates HRS Chapter 467 or the associated administrative rules, the principal broker faces disciplinary action regardless of whether the broker had actual knowledge of the violation. The standard is constructive knowledge: what a reasonable principal broker should have known given the systems in place.

| Audit Area | Minimum Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction file review | Every critical milestone | Written sign-off with date |
| Advertising compliance | Monthly | Screenshot archive with review notes |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly (HAR 16-99-3.1) | Bank statements + ledger match |
| Disclosure form verification | Per transaction | Checklist confirming REC-7 and SRPD-2 |
| License status of team members | Quarterly | DCCA PVL verification printout |
| Unlicensed activity review | Ongoing | Job description audit for assistants |

**Brokers who rely on team leaders to self-police are accepting liability without control.** The 2025 HIREC disciplinary case In re: Nakamura Realty established that a principal broker's delegation of file review to a team leader, without independent verification, did not satisfy the "reasonable supervision" standard. The broker received a 60-day suspension and a $5,000 fine.

Quarterly license verification through the DCCA's [Professional and Vocational Licensing portal](https://cca.hawaii.gov/pvl/) is non-negotiable. Licensees whose renewals lapse mid-transaction create immediate legal exposure, and any commission earned during a lapse period may be subject to disgorgement.

## Liability Consequences When Teams Violate Supervision Rules

The consequences for non-compliance extend well beyond administrative fines. Under HRS Section 467-14, HIREC may deny, suspend, or revoke any license for violations including failure to supervise, misrepresentation, and commingling of funds. But the financial exposure goes further.

Civil liability attaches when a client suffers damages due to a supervision failure. If a team member provides incorrect disclosure or mishandles a transaction, the principal broker's Errors and Omissions policy may not cover the claim if the insurer demonstrates a systemic supervision failure. Several Hawaii courts have allowed piercing of the brokerage entity to reach the principal broker personally in cases involving willful neglect of supervisory duties.

Agents who have been through [deal breakdowns caused by missed deadlines](/blog/where-deals-break-down) or [compliance failures rooted in poor systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) understand that prevention costs a fraction of remediation. The average HIREC enforcement proceeding costs respondents between $8,000 and $25,000 in legal fees alone, even when the outcome is a consent order rather than full revocation.

Transaction cancellation is another real risk. Under HRS Section 508D-15, a buyer who did not receive the required SRPD-2 disclosure may rescind the purchase contract within certain timeframes. If a team member failed to deliver the disclosure because the team's internal systems lacked a proper [deadline tracking mechanism](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the cancellation falls squarely on the brokerage.

## Building Compliant Team Structures Under Hawaii Law

Structuring a team that satisfies HIREC's requirements starts with documentation. Every team should operate under a written team agreement that explicitly states the principal broker retains all supervisory authority, that the team leader's role is administrative rather than supervisory in the legal sense, and that all trust funds flow exclusively through the brokerage trust account.

The team agreement should also define advertising approval workflows. No social media post, print ad, or digital campaign should go live without verification that the brokerage name appears as required. This is an area where automation can prevent violations entirely — systems that flag non-compliant content before publication eliminate the risk of a $2,500 fine from a single Instagram post.
