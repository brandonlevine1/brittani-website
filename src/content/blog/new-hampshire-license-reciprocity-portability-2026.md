## Understanding New Hampshire Real Estate License Reciprocity & Portability 2026

The rules governing New Hampshire real estate license reciprocity & portability 2026 are not as straightforward as many border-state agents assume. New Hampshire does not maintain blanket reciprocity agreements with any state, a fact that regularly surprises agents licensed in neighboring Vermont, Maine, and Massachusetts. Instead, the state operates under a limited recognition framework codified in RSA 331-A that permits exam waivers—not automatic licensure.

The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), housed within the Office of Professional Licensure and Certification, administers all out-of-state applications. Agents who misunderstand the distinction between reciprocity (mutual recognition) and portability (carrying a license across borders) expose themselves—and their brokers—to enforcement actions that can unravel closed transactions.

This post breaks down exactly how the system works in 2026, which forms you need, what can go wrong, and what brokers should be auditing on their teams right now.

## The Legal Framework: RSA 331-A and NHREC Authority

**New Hampshire's licensing statute, RSA 331-A, grants the Real Estate Commission authority to recognize out-of-state licenses under specific conditions outlined in Section 8.** Under RSA 331-A:8, an applicant who holds an active license in another jurisdiction may receive a waiver of the national portion of the licensing exam if that state's requirements are substantially equivalent to New Hampshire's. The state-specific portion of the exam is never waived.

This is a critical distinction. The statute does not permit an agent to simply register their existing license and begin practicing. Every out-of-state agent must submit a formal application, pass the New Hampshire state exam, clear a criminal background check, and affiliate with a supervising broker licensed in New Hampshire before conducting any real estate activity.

The [NHREC application portal](https://www.oplc.nh.gov/real-estate-commission) contains current instructions, fee schedules, and the forms referenced throughout this article. Agents coming from states with cooperative licensing frameworks—like the ARELLO-backed compact model some states have adopted—should note that New Hampshire has not joined any multi-state compact as of mid-2026.

## Forms and Filing Requirements for Out-of-State Agents

The primary application form for out-of-state agents seeking licensure in New Hampshire is Form RELO-1, titled "Application for Licensure by Reciprocal Recognition." Despite the name, this form applies to both reciprocity-eligible applicants and those seeking exam waivers under RSA 331-A:8. You must submit this form directly to the NHREC along with several supporting documents.

Here is a comparison of what in-state and out-of-state applicants must provide:

| Requirement | NH Resident Applicant | Out-of-State (RELO-1) Applicant |
|---|---|---|
| Form RELO-1 | Not required | Required |
| License Certification (from home state) | Not applicable | Required within 90 days of issue |
| NH State Exam | Required | Required |
| National Exam | Required | May be waived under RSA 331-A:8 |
| Fingerprint-Based Background Check | Required | Required |
| Errors & Omissions Insurance | Required | Required |
| Broker Affiliation (Form BA-1) | Required | Required |
| Application Fee | $150 | $150 |

Form BA-1, the Broker Affiliation form, must be filed concurrently with or prior to Form RELO-1. The Commission will not issue an active license without verified broker sponsorship. Agents who plan to operate as independent brokers must instead submit Form IB-2 and demonstrate two years of full-time experience, or equivalent, under RSA 331-A:7.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Practicing real estate in New Hampshire without a valid New Hampshire license is a violation of RSA 331-A:28, which classifies unlicensed practice as a misdemeanor.** The statute authorizes fines of up to $2,000 per violation, and each transaction or act of brokerage constitutes a separate offense. The Commission may also seek injunctive relief through the Superior Court to prevent continued violations.

Beyond fines, the transactional fallout can be severe. A buyer who discovers their agent was unlicensed during the transaction may seek rescission of the purchase contract. Courts in New Hampshire have historically viewed unlicensed practice as a material defect in the agency relationship, meaning the entire deal can be voided—not just the commission agreement.

Brokers face additional liability. Under RSA 331-A:16, a supervising broker who permits unlicensed activity within their brokerage may face license suspension or revocation. The Commission has used this provision against firms that failed to verify the New Hampshire licensure status of agents transferring from out of state, particularly in the Upper Valley and Seacoast markets where cross-border practice is common. ## Common Mistakes Agents Make With NH Reciprocity

Mistake one: assuming a border-state license is enough. Agents in Vermont and Maine frequently list New Hampshire properties on the MLS or attend showings without holding a New Hampshire license, believing proximity or referral relationships create an exemption. They do not. Any compensated real estate activity within New Hampshire's borders requires New Hampshire licensure, full stop.

Mistake two: submitting an expired license certification. Form RELO-1 requires a license certification from the applicant's home state, and that certification must be dated within 90 days of submission. Agents who begin the application process but delay filing often discover their certification has lapsed, forcing them to request a new one and resetting the processing timeline.

**Mistake three: failing to complete continuing education in both states.** New Hampshire requires 15 hours of CE every two years under NHREC Rule REA 501. Agents who hold licenses in multiple states sometimes allow their New Hampshire CE to lapse while staying current in their home state. An expired CE cycle results in license inactivation—not a grace period—and any transactions closed during that window are technically unlicensed practice.

Mistake four: not updating E&O insurance to cover New Hampshire transactions. Some carriers issue policies tied to a specific state of practice. An agent whose errors and omissions policy explicitly covers only Vermont transactions has no valid coverage for New Hampshire deals, which leaves the agent personally exposed and may violate the broker's risk management requirements.

Mistake five: using a home-state broker affiliation for New Hampshire deals. Even if your Vermont broker has a New Hampshire license, you cannot practice under their umbrella unless they have filed the appropriate paperwork with NHREC designating themselves as your supervising broker in New Hampshire specifically. The Form BA-1 must reflect the New Hampshire relationship.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Brokers operating in New Hampshire's border markets have a heightened duty to verify licensure status for every agent on their roster.** This is not a one-time check at onboarding. RSA 331-A:16 places ongoing supervisory responsibility on the principal broker, meaning that if an agent's license lapses mid-year, the broker bears liability for any transactions closed during the lapsed period.

Here is a practical audit checklist brokers should run quarterly:

| Audit Item | Where to Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Active NH license status | NHREC online verification portal | Quarterly |
| CE completion (15 hrs/2 yrs) | Agent self-certification + NHREC records | Annually |
| E&O insurance coverage for NH | Insurance carrier certificate | At renewal |
| Form BA-1 on file with NHREC | Commission records | At onboarding + annually |
| Out-of-state license status (if dual-licensed) | Home state commission portal | Quarterly |

Brokers should also audit their transaction files for any deals where a dual-licensed agent acted in a capacity that exceeded their New Hampshire licensure level. An agent licensed as a salesperson in New Hampshire but as a broker in Vermont cannot perform broker-level functions—such as holding escrow funds—in a New Hampshire transaction. This distinction trips up small firms where agents wear multiple hats.

## Portability vs. Reciprocity: The Distinction That Matters

Portability, in real estate licensing, typically refers to cooperative agreements that allow an agent to conduct limited activities—like showing property or writing offers—across state lines under their home-state license. New Hampshire does not participate in any portability compact. There is no provision under RSA 331-A that permits a non-New Hampshire licensee to perform any compensated real estate activity within the state, even on a temporary or one-time basis.

Some agents confuse referral fees with portability. An out-of-state agent can receive a referral fee from a New Hampshire licensee without holding a New Hampshire license, provided they perform no brokerage activity within the state. The moment they attend a showing, negotiate terms, or advise a client on a New Hampshire transaction, they have crossed into unlicensed practice. The Commission has issued multiple advisory rulings reinforcing this boundary.

This hard line distinguishes New Hampshire from states like [Wisconsin, which has adopted a more structured reciprocity framework](/blog/wisconsin-license-reciprocity-portability-2026). Agents moving between New England states should treat each jurisdiction as entirely independent and budget both time and money for separate applications.

## Planning Your 2026 Cross-Border Strategy

Agents who regularly work both sides of the Connecticut River, the Salmon Falls River, or the Massachusetts border need a documented compliance strategy—not assumptions based on past practice. Start by confirming your New Hampshire license status through the NHREC verification system. If you hold a license, verify that your CE cycle is current and your E&O policy explicitly names New Hampshire as a covered jurisdiction.

If you are applying for the first time in 2026, expect a 4-6 week processing window after submitting a complete RELO-1 package. Incomplete applications—particularly those missing the fingerprint card or license certification—are returned without processing, and there is no expedited review option. Plan your application timing around your transaction pipeline, not the other way around.
