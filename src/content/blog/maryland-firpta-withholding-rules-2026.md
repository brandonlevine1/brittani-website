## Understanding Maryland FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The Maryland firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 create a dual-layer compliance obligation that catches agents off guard every quarter. Federal law under IRC Section 1445 requires buyers to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when purchasing real property from a foreign person. Maryland layers its own nonresident withholding on top, governed by Maryland Tax-General Article Section 10-912, which demands an additional 8.25% withholding on the total payment to nonresident sellers.

Most agents assume the settlement attorney handles everything. That assumption is where liability begins—because when withholding fails, the IRS looks at the buyer first, and the Maryland Comptroller looks at whoever controlled the disbursement.

The distinction between federal FIRPTA and Maryland's state-level nonresident withholding matters enormously at the closing table. They operate under different statutes, different forms, and different deadlines. An agent who conflates the two will misadvise clients and risk personal exposure under Maryland's regulatory framework.

## Who Qualifies as a "Foreign Seller" Under Federal and Maryland Law

**A "foreign person" for FIRPTA purposes includes nonresident alien individuals, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates.** U.S. residents holding green cards are not subject to FIRPTA, but agents cannot rely on a seller's verbal representation of status. The determination must be documented through IRS Form W-8BEN or a FIRPTA affidavit certifying non-foreign status.

Maryland's nonresident withholding casts a wider net. Under Tax-General Article Section 10-912, the state applies its withholding to any seller who is not a resident of Maryland—including U.S. citizens living in other states and foreign nationals alike. This means a foreign seller triggers both the federal 15% and the Maryland 8.25%, while a U.S. citizen living in Virginia only triggers the state portion.

The practical implication for listing agents is straightforward: you must identify the seller's residency status early in the listing process, not at the pre-closing walkthrough. A seller who is both foreign and nonresident to Maryland faces a combined withholding of 23.25% of the gross sales price held back at settlement.

## Federal FIRPTA Mechanics: The 15% Withholding

The buyer—not the seller, not the agent, not the broker—bears primary responsibility for federal FIRPTA withholding under IRC Section 1445. If the buyer fails to withhold and the seller does not pay the tax, the IRS can assess the full 15% against the buyer plus penalties and interest. This is strict liability, meaning good faith reliance on a seller's false claim of U.S. status does not protect the buyer unless proper affidavit procedures were followed.

The withholding is remitted to the IRS using [Form 8288](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8288) (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). These must be filed within 20 days after the transfer date. The settlement agent typically handles the mechanical filing, but legal liability remains with the buyer.

Reduced withholding is available. If the seller applies using IRS Form 8288-B before closing, the IRS may issue a withholding certificate authorizing a lower amount or zero withholding. Processing takes approximately 90 days, which means agents representing foreign sellers should initiate this conversation at listing, not under contract.

## Maryland's State-Level Withholding: Form MW506NRS

**Maryland's nonresident withholding operates independently from FIRPTA and applies regardless of the seller's nationality.** The Comptroller of Maryland requires that 8.25% of the total payment be withheld from any nonresident individual seller at the time of property transfer. The form used is MW506NRS (Return of Income Tax Withholding for Nonresident Sale of Real Property), filed by the person responsible for closing—typically the settlement agent or title company.

The withholding rate of 8.25% applies to the total payment, which Maryland defines as the sales price minus the seller's costs of sale. If the seller is a nonresident entity rather than an individual, the rate may differ. The MW506NRS must be submitted along with the payment to the Comptroller.

Maryland also provides an exemption mechanism. A nonresident seller can avoid or reduce withholding by filing Form MW506AE (Application for Certificate of Full or Partial Exemption) with the Comptroller prior to closing. Approval of this form allows the settlement agent to disburse proceeds without the 8.25% holdback. Agents who understand this process add genuine value to foreign seller clients facing combined withholding rates that approach one-quarter of the sales price.

| Requirement | Federal FIRPTA | Maryland State |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding Rate | 15% of gross sales price | 8.25% of total payment |
| Responsible Party | Buyer | Person responsible for closing |
| Filing Form | IRS Form 8288 + 8288-A | Maryland Form MW506NRS |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after transfer | At time of closing/settlement |
| Reduction Form | IRS Form 8288-B | Maryland Form MW506AE |
| Governing Statute | IRC Section 1445 | MD Tax-General Article 10-912 |

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

Agents sometimes believe FIRPTA compliance falls entirely outside their scope. Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) regulations and NAR's Code of Ethics both impose duties of competent service, and an agent who fails to flag a foreign seller's withholding obligations may face disciplinary action, civil liability, or both.

**If the buyer's agent does not ensure proper withholding documentation, the buyer—their client—becomes personally liable to the IRS for the full 15%.** That is not a theoretical risk. The IRS enforces FIRPTA assessments aggressively, and buyers who later discover they owe six-figure tax bills due to their agent's oversight have grounds for malpractice claims. Similar exposure exists on the listing side if a seller's agent promises net proceeds that ignore the withholding obligation.

Maryland's settlement procedures provide some backstop because attorneys or title companies control disbursement. But in transactions where the agent selects or recommends a settlement provider unfamiliar with foreign seller transactions, the entire chain breaks. Deals have been cancelled days before closing when title underwriters discover undocumented foreign seller status and refuse to insure without proper withholding compliance. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the transaction timeline helps agents prevent these last-minute disasters.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Foreign Seller Transactions

The first mistake is failing to ask about citizenship or residency status at listing intake. Agents who wait until a contract is ratified lose the 90-day window needed for IRS Form 8288-B processing. By the time the issue surfaces, the seller either accepts full withholding or the closing date must extend—often killing the deal.

The second mistake is confusing FIRPTA with Maryland's nonresident withholding. These are separate obligations with separate forms, separate filing deadlines, and separate responsible parties. An agent who tells a foreign seller "you'll have 15% withheld" has understated the obligation by 8.25 percentage points. That misinformation affects the seller's decision to accept or reject offers.

The third mistake is relying on a seller's verbal statement that they are a U.S. person. Without a properly executed FIRPTA affidavit (also called a non-foreign affidavit or certificate of non-foreign status), the buyer has no safe harbor protection. Agents must ensure the settlement agent obtains this document in writing, signed under penalty of perjury.

The fourth mistake is failing to coordinate with a tax professional early. Reduced withholding applications require a U.S. taxpayer identification number (ITIN if the seller lacks an SSN), estimated gain calculations, and proper supporting documentation. Agents who refer sellers to CPAs specializing in nonresident taxation at the point of listing—not the point of closing—save deals.

The fifth mistake involves [missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on exemption applications. Both the IRS Form 8288-B and Maryland Form MW506AE require lead time. Filing them after contract ratification when a 30-day close is expected virtually guarantees they will not be processed before settlement.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Maryland carry supervisory responsibility under COMAR 09.11.02 (Code of Maryland Regulations governing real estate brokers). When a brokerage handles transactions involving foreign sellers, the broker must ensure agents are not providing tax advice beyond their competence while still identifying the compliance triggers that require professional referrals.

| Broker Audit Item | Action Required | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake forms | Confirm citizenship/residency question is included | Every listing |
| Transaction file review | Verify FIRPTA affidavit or withholding docs present | Before closing |
| Agent training | Annual FIRPTA/nonresident withholding CLE or meeting | Annually |
| Settlement agent coordination | Confirm selected provider has foreign seller experience | Per transaction |

**Brokers should implement a written office policy requiring agents to disclose foreign seller status to the settlement agent within five business days of contract ratification.** This gives the title company adequate time to coordinate withholding, order additional documentation, and prevent last-minute deal failures. The policy should specify that agents are not to calculate withholding amounts themselves—that responsibility belongs to the settlement agent and the seller's tax advisor.

Audit trails matter. If a deal goes sideways because a foreign seller's withholding was not properly handled, the broker needs documentation showing the agent followed office procedures. A [transaction coordination system](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) that tracks compliance milestones provides that paper trail automatically. Without it, the broker's supervisory liability under MREC rules becomes difficult to defend.

## Practical Timeline for Agents Representing Foreign Sellers

The day a foreign seller signs a listing agreement, the agent should confirm residency status in writing and refer the seller to a CPA or tax attorney experienced in FIRPTA and Maryland nonresident taxation. Within that first week, the seller's tax advisor should determine whether to file IRS Form 8288-B for reduced federal withholding and Maryland Form MW506AE for state exemption.

Once under contract, the agent must notify the settlement agent of the seller's foreign status immediately. The settlement agent then prepares to withhold 15% federal plus 8.25% state from proceeds unless exemption certificates are received. If the seller's Form 8288-B application is still pending with the IRS at the time of closing, the full 15% must be withheld—there is no discretion to reduce it without the certificate in hand.

At settlement, the disbursement statement must reflect the withholding amounts as line items. The seller receives the net proceeds minus both withholding amounts and standard closing costs. After closing, the settlement agent files Form 8288 with the IRS within 20 days and remits the MW506NRS payment to the Maryland Comptroller. The seller can later file a U.S. tax return to claim a refund of any overwithholding, but that process takes months and requires professional tax preparation.
