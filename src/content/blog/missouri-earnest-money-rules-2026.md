## Understanding Missouri Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Missouri earnest money & escrow laws 2026 remain one of the most consequential compliance areas for agents and managing brokers alike. A single misstep—depositing a day late, cutting a refund check without written release, or failing to disclose an escrow dispute—can trigger disciplinary action from the Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC). The stakes are not abstract: license suspension, civil liability, and collapsed deals all sit on the other side of sloppy escrow management.

The governing authority here is Chapter 339 of the Missouri Revised Statutes (RSMo 339.100 et seq.) combined with the administrative rules codified in 20 CSR 2250-8.060. These statutes and rules set out who may hold funds, how quickly they must be deposited, what recordkeeping is required, and what happens when two parties disagree over who gets the money back. Every agent operating in the state needs to understand these provisions—not in general terms, but line by line.

## Statutory Framework: RSMo 339.100 and 20 CSR 2250-8.060

**RSMo 339.100 establishes that only a licensed broker—never a salesperson acting independently—may maintain an escrow or trust account.** The statute explicitly prohibits comingling client funds with the broker's personal or business operating funds. Violation constitutes grounds for disciplinary action up to and including revocation of the broker's license.

The administrative rule 20 CSR 2250-8.060 fills in the operational details. It mandates that earnest money be deposited into the broker's escrow account no later than the close of the fifth business day following mutual execution of the contract, unless the parties' written agreement provides a different timeline. The rule also requires brokers to maintain complete records—deposit slips, bank statements, individual ledger cards—for a minimum of five years.

One critical nuance: Missouri law does not require earnest money for a contract to be enforceable. Earnest money is customary, not legally mandatory. But when it exists, every dollar must be handled according to these statutes. The [MREC's official guidance](https://pr.mo.gov/realestate.asp) reinforces that ignorance of escrow rules is never an acceptable defense during a disciplinary hearing.

## Missouri Association of Realtors Forms You Need to Know

Missouri agents overwhelmingly rely on the Missouri Association of Realtors (MAR) standard forms suite. For earnest money and escrow purposes, the following forms matter most in 2026:

| Form Number | Form Name | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| MAR Form 100 | Residential Sale Contract | Specifies earnest money amount, holder, and deposit deadline |
| MAR Form 115 | Earnest Money Receipt | Acknowledges broker's receipt of funds |
| MAR Form 135 | Release of Earnest Money | Mutual written authorization for disbursement |
| MAR Form 150 | Amendment to Contract | Used to modify escrow terms post-execution |

**MAR Form 135 is the single most important document when a deal falls apart.** Without both parties signing this release, the broker has no authority to disburse funds to either side. Agents who attempt to "shortcut" this step by verbally relaying instructions or texting screenshots of one party's consent are violating the written-release requirement.

Form 100, Section 5 is where the earnest money amount, form of payment, holder identity, and deposit timeline are established. Leaving any of these fields blank—or filling them in after execution—creates ambiguity that can later be weaponized in a dispute. Agents should treat this section with the same care they give to the purchase price.

## Deposit Deadlines and the Five-Business-Day Rule

The clock starts ticking the moment both parties have executed the contract. Under 20 CSR 2250-8.060, the broker must deposit the earnest money check (or process the electronic funds) by close of business on the fifth business day. Weekends and state holidays do not count.

Here is where agents frequently miscalculate. If a contract is fully executed on a Friday evening, Day One is Monday—not Saturday. A deposit made the following Friday is compliant. A deposit made the following Monday is not. This sounds elementary, but MREC complaint files show deposit-timing violations ranking among the top five most common infractions year after year.

Agents handling checks should also understand that "deposit" means delivering the funds to the bank, not handing the check to the office manager. If the brokerage's internal workflow adds a day of administrative processing, that day still counts toward the five-day window. Brokers who rely on a [structured checklist-based system](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rarely encounter this problem; those who depend on individual agent memory often do.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Noncompliance with Missouri escrow laws carries consequences that range from formal reprimand to criminal prosecution.** The MREC can impose any combination of the following penalties under RSMo 339.100 and 339.170:

License censure or formal reprimand placed on the agent's permanent record. License suspension for a defined period, during which the agent cannot practice. License revocation, which requires the agent to reapply and pass the exam anew. Civil penalties of up to $2,500 per violation under RSMo 339.170.

In cases involving conversion—where a broker or agent intentionally misappropriates escrow funds—Missouri law treats the conduct as a Class D felony under RSMo 570.030 (theft). This is not a theoretical risk. MREC publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and fund-handling violations appear in nearly every report.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. Either party to the transaction can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, and Missouri courts have historically awarded compensatory damages plus attorney fees in escrow mishandling cases. The cost of defending such a lawsuit—even if the agent prevails—often exceeds $15,000 in legal fees alone.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make with Earnest Money

Mistake number one: holding a check "per buyer's request" without depositing it. Some agents believe that if the buyer asks them to hold the check until inspection is complete, the agent can comply. They cannot. The five-business-day rule applies regardless of informal buyer instructions. The only way to delay deposit is a written provision in the contract itself.

Mistake number two: releasing funds based on a single party's written demand without mutual consent. When a buyer sends a cancellation notice and demands the earnest money back, agents sometimes issue a refund immediately. Unless the seller has also signed MAR Form 135 (or a comparable written release), disbursement violates MREC rules. The broker must hold the funds until both parties agree or a court orders release.

Mistake number three: depositing earnest money into the wrong account. This typically happens at brokerages with multiple accounts—a general operating account and a separate escrow account. Accidentally depositing into the operating account constitutes comingling, even if the funds are transferred the same day. MREC auditors look at initial deposit records, not subsequent corrections.

Mistake number four: failing to document a change in escrow holder. If the parties agree mid-transaction to transfer funds from the listing broker's escrow to a title company, that change must be documented via MAR Form 150 or an equivalent written amendment. Verbal agreements are unenforceable and leave the original holder exposed to liability.

Mistake number five: not disclosing to the buyer that earnest money is held in a non-interest-bearing account. While Missouri does not require brokers to place escrow funds in interest-bearing accounts, agents must accurately represent how funds are held when asked. Misrepresentation—even by omission—can form the basis of a MREC complaint.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry personal liability for escrow compliance across their entire office. MREC holds the broker—not the individual agent—ultimately responsible for every dollar in the trust account. This means quarterly self-audits are not optional; they are a survival mechanism.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Bank reconciliation | Monthly | Escrow account balance matches sum of all individual ledgers |
| Deposit timing | Per transaction | Funds deposited within five business days of execution |
| Release documentation | Per disbursement | MAR Form 135 signed by both parties before any check is cut |
| Ledger accuracy | Monthly | Every deposit and disbursement tied to a specific transaction file |
| Record retention | Annually | Files older than five years archived; files younger than five years accessible |

**Brokers should also verify that no agent in the office is personally holding uncashed checks in desk drawers or car consoles.** This sounds absurd, but it is the most common finding in surprise MREC audits. A physical check sitting in an agent's briefcase for six days is an automatic violation—and it is the broker who will answer for it.

## Disputed Earnest Money: Interpleader and Broker Obligations

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who receives the earnest money, the broker is stuck in the middle. Missouri law does not authorize the broker to make a unilateral decision. The broker's options are limited: continue holding the funds while encouraging a mutual release, or file an interpleader action in circuit court.

An interpleader action (authorized under Missouri Supreme Court Rule 52.07) allows the broker to deposit the disputed funds with the court and ask to be dismissed from the dispute. The court then decides who receives the money. Filing an interpleader costs the brokerage attorney fees and court costs—typically $1,500 to $3,000—but it eliminates the broker's ongoing liability for the funds.

Agents should note that brokers are not required to wait indefinitely before filing interpleader. If both parties are unresponsive for 30 days after a [transaction falls apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down), most Missouri real estate attorneys recommend initiating the action promptly. Sitting on disputed funds for months invites regulatory scrutiny and increases the likelihood of a complaint from either side.
