## Hawaii Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Statutory Framework

The question of Hawaii who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by custom alone. It is governed by a combination of statute, contract terms, and lender requirements that every licensed agent must understand before presenting offers or advising clients. Getting this wrong does not just embarrass you at the closing table — it exposes you to commission complaints, deal cancellations, and disciplinary action from the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC).

Hawaii's closing cost landscape differs materially from the mainland. Escrow practices, conveyance tax obligations, and title insurance conventions here follow patterns shaped by HRS Chapter 247, HRS Chapter 502, and HIREC's administrative rules under Title 16, Chapter 99. Agents who relocated from other states or who treat Hawaii closings like California or Texas closings will eventually lose a deal over preventable errors.

This post breaks down which party typically pays what, what the law actually mandates versus what is merely customary, and where agents and their brokers face real liability exposure.

## The Legal Baseline: What Hawaii Law Requires

**Hawaii Revised Statutes Section 247-2 places the conveyance tax squarely on the seller (grantor).** This is not negotiable by contract — the tax is the statutory responsibility of the party conveying title. The rate structure, updated through Act 242, applies a tiered schedule that ranges from $0.10 per $100 of value for properties under $600,000 sold by owner-occupants to $1.25 per $100 for properties over $10 million sold by nonresidents.

HRS Section 502-31 governs recording fees, which are typically split or allocated to the buyer since the buyer records the new deed and mortgage. The statute sets per-document fees that the Bureau of Conveyances collects, currently $41 for the first page and $5 for each additional page for regular system documents.

Under HRS Chapter 467 and HIREC's Rules Section 16-99-3, agents owe fiduciary and disclosure obligations regarding all material terms of a transaction, including cost allocation. An agent who leads a client to believe a cost is "always" paid by the other party — when it is actually negotiable — has potentially violated the duty of care.

## Standard Cost Allocation: Buyer vs. Seller Breakdown

The Hawaii Association of Realtors (HAR) Purchase Contract form RR403 and its related addenda set the contractual framework agents use in most residential transactions. Paragraph 14 of RR403 addresses closing costs and allocates responsibilities unless the parties agree otherwise.

Here is how costs typically break down in practice:

| Closing Cost Item | Typically Paid By | Statutory or Customary |
|---|---|---|
| Conveyance tax | Seller | Statutory (HRS 247-2) |
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | Customary |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Customary |
| Escrow fees | Split 50/50 | Customary |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer | Customary (HRS 502-31) |
| Recording fees (mortgage release) | Seller | Customary |
| Home inspection | Buyer | Customary |
| Pest inspection (termite) | Negotiable | Varies by island/county |

Agents must understand that "customary" means negotiable. Only the conveyance tax has a statutory mandate on allocation. Every other item can shift depending on how the purchase contract is drafted and what the parties agree to in Paragraph 14 or through addendum.

## Forms Agents Must Know

**The RR403 (Revised 2024) Purchase Contract remains the primary residential form through 2026, and agents who use outdated versions risk enforceability challenges.** Paragraph 14 of the current form explicitly states which costs each party bears "unless otherwise agreed in writing." Any deviation from the standard allocation must be memorialized through the form itself or a signed addendum.

Additional HAR forms relevant to closing cost allocation include:

RR111 — Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement, which while primarily about physical condition, can surface expected costs that affect net proceeds. RR254 — Counter Offer form, frequently used to shift specific closing costs as part of negotiations. RR305 — Estimated Closing Statement, which agents should review with clients before acceptance to avoid post-contract disputes over cost allocation.

The DROA (Deposit Receipt Offer and Acceptance) for commercial or non-standard transactions uses different language and gives parties more latitude to define cost-sharing. Agents handling mixed-use or investment properties on forms other than RR403 need to pay close attention to whether default allocations are even present in those documents.

## The Conveyance Tax: Hawaii's Distinctive Closing Cost

No discussion of [closing costs in Hawaii](/blog/wisconsin-who-pays-closing-costs-2026) is complete without a detailed look at the conveyance tax, which functions as a transfer tax but operates under a complex tiered and residency-based structure unlike any other state.

The tiered rates apply differently depending on whether the property is owner-occupied and whether the seller is a Hawaii resident or nonresident. The distinction matters enormously for investment property sales to foreign buyers and for agents representing out-of-state sellers.

| Sale Price Range | Resident Rate (per $100) | Nonresident Rate (per $100) |
|---|---|---|
| Under $600,000 | $0.10 | $0.15 |
| $600,001 - $1,000,000 | $0.20 | $0.25 |
| $1,000,001 - $2,000,000 | $0.30 | $0.40 |
| $2,000,001 - $4,000,000 | $0.50 | $0.70 |
| $4,000,001 - $6,000,000 | $0.70 | $1.00 |
| $6,000,001 - $10,000,000 | $0.90 | $1.05 |
| Over $10,000,000 | $1.00 | $1.25 |

Agents should note that HARPTA (Hawaii Real Property Tax Act, HRS Section 235-68) imposes a separate withholding obligation when the seller is a nonresident. The buyer (or the buyer's agent, through escrow) must withhold 7.25% of the gross sales price. This is not a closing cost in the traditional sense but affects closing proceeds and must be disclosed early.

For reference, the [Hawaii Department of Taxation](https://tax.hawaii.gov/forms/) publishes current rates, forms, and guidance on both the conveyance tax and HARPTA withholding.

## Common Mistakes Hawaii Agents Make on Closing Costs

**Experienced agents are not immune to these errors — they simply have not been caught yet.** Here are five specific mistakes that lead to complaints, lawsuits, or deal collapses.

First, telling a buyer that the seller "always pays" for owner's title insurance. While customary on Oahu and in most residential transactions, this is not universal across all islands or property types. On the Big Island and Maui, some transactions allocate this differently, and commercial deals almost never follow residential custom. Presenting custom as law misleads the client.

Second, failing to account for HARPTA withholding when estimating the seller's net proceeds. An agent representing a nonresident seller who does not flag the 7.25% withholding requirement until two weeks before closing creates a crisis. The seller suddenly faces a six-figure reduction in proceeds they did not anticipate, and the deal may fall apart.

Third, neglecting to clarify escrow fee splits in the purchase contract. Many agents assume the 50/50 split is automatic, but if the contract is silent or if a counter offer modifies terms without addressing escrow fees, disputes arise at the closing table. Escrow companies will not close without clear instructions, and last-minute negotiations over a $2,000 escrow fee have killed deals worth millions.

Fourth, using mainland-style net sheet templates that do not reflect Hawaii's tiered conveyance tax structure. A flat-rate transfer tax estimate understates the seller's costs on high-value properties and overstates them on lower-priced units. [Missed deadlines and mismanaged paperwork](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) compound when agents work from inaccurate numbers.

Fifth, assuming buyer-paid closing costs are fixed. Buyers often receive seller concessions, lender credits, or negotiated cost shifts that require precise documentation in the contract. An agent who does not track these moving pieces through to the final HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure invites post-closing disputes.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge bear direct supervisory responsibility under HRS Section 467-1.6 and HIREC Rules 16-99-5. If an agent in the brokerage misrepresents closing cost obligations, the broker faces joint liability and potential disciplinary action.

**Every brokerage should audit three things on every transaction before closing.** First, confirm that the executed purchase contract clearly identifies who pays each category of closing cost. If Paragraph 14 of RR403 was modified or if an addendum shifts costs, the file should contain that documentation in unambiguous language.

Second, verify that net sheets or estimated closing statements provided to clients match the contractual terms. An agent who verbally promises a seller that "the buyer is picking up escrow" but does not memorialize it in writing creates liability for the entire brokerage. [Broken deals often trace back to exactly this kind of gap](/blog/where-deals-break-down) between what was said and what was documented.

Third, confirm HARPTA compliance for any transaction involving a nonresident seller. The withholding obligation falls on the buyer, but the listing agent's failure to disclose this cost early can constitute negligent misrepresentation. Brokers should require a HARPTA checklist for every listing where the seller's residency status is uncertain.

HIREC's disciplinary process allows fines up to $2,500 per violation under HRS Section 467-14, and repeated violations lead to license suspension or revocation. In 2024 and 2025, HIREC increased enforcement actions related to disclosure failures, and brokers who assume their agents know the rules are taking on unnecessary risk.

## Negotiation Strategy: How Cost Allocation Affects Offers

Smart agents use closing cost allocation as a strategic tool rather than treating it as an afterthought. A seller concession of 3% toward the buyer's closing costs can make a higher purchase price viable for a buyer with limited cash reserves, while the seller's net proceeds may remain identical or even improve due to the higher contract price.

However, lender guidelines cap seller concessions. Conventional loans typically allow 3% for investment properties, 6% for primary residences with less than 25% down, and 9% for primary residences with 25% or more down. FHA and VA loans have their own caps. Agents who promise buyers a 6% seller credit without confirming the lender's guidelines set up a renegotiation that damages trust.

The pest inspection (termite report) is a frequent negotiation flashpoint in Hawaii due to the islands' climate. Unlike many mainland markets where the seller customarily provides a termite clearance, Hawaii practice varies. On Oahu, sellers often pay for the report; on neighbor islands, this is negotiated case by case. Agents should address this explicitly in the initial offer rather than assuming one side will absorb it.
