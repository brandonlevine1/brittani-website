## Understanding Vermont Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The regulatory framework governing real estate commissions and licensing in Vermont has shifted meaningfully over the past two years. Agents and brokers operating under **Vermont commission & licensing rules 2026** face updated disclosure obligations, tighter enforcement from the Vermont Real Estate Commission, and ongoing ripple effects from the national commission lawsuits that reshaped buyer representation agreements. Ignoring any of these changes puts your license — and your next closing — at risk.

Vermont's real estate licensing is governed primarily by [26 V.S.A. Chapter 41](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/26/041) and administered through the Office of Professional Regulation (OPR) under the Secretary of State. The Vermont Real Estate Commission, a seven-member body appointed by the Governor, sets rules, adjudicates complaints, and approves continuing education providers. Every agent and broker in the state answers to both.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, and compliance pitfalls that matter most heading into the back half of 2026.

## Licensing Structure and Requirements

Vermont issues two tiers of real estate licensure: salesperson and broker. A salesperson must work under a licensed principal broker and cannot operate independently. A broker can supervise salespersons, hold escrow funds, and operate a brokerage firm.

**To obtain a salesperson license, applicants must complete 40 hours of pre-licensure education from a Commission-approved provider, pass the state and national portions of the licensing exam, and submit an application to the OPR.** Broker candidates need an additional 24 hours of broker-specific coursework plus two years of active practice. The current application is filed through the OPR's online licensing portal, and the license fee is $100 for a two-year term.

Renewal happens biennially. Every licensee must complete 24 hours of continuing education during each renewal cycle, with at least 4 hours in Commission-designated core topics. Failure to renew on time results in an automatic lapse — not a grace period. An agent whose license has lapsed cannot lawfully perform any licensed activity, including receiving compensation on pending deals.

## Commission Disclosure and Negotiation Rules

Vermont law has always treated real estate commissions as negotiable. There is no statutory cap, no minimum, and no standard rate set by the Commission. What the state does require is transparency about how compensation flows between parties.

Under the post-settlement landscape, buyer-broker compensation can no longer be communicated through MLS offer-of-compensation fields in many Vermont MLS systems, including the New England Real Estate Network (NEREN). Agents representing buyers must instead establish compensation terms directly with their clients through a written agreement. Listing agents who want to offer compensation to a buyer's broker must do so outside the MLS, typically through broker-to-broker communication or concession language in the purchase contract.

**The Vermont Real Estate Commission requires that all brokerage relationships be disclosed in writing before the agent provides substantive services.** This is documented on the Commission's mandatory Disclosure of Brokerage Relationships form, which agents must present to all parties at initial contact. Failing to provide this form is one of the most frequently cited violations in Commission disciplinary actions. The form itself is available on the OPR website and must be signed or acknowledged by the consumer.

## How Commission Splits Actually Work in Vermont

Commission splits between brokerages are contractual, not statutory. The listing agreement — typically based on a standard form provided by the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) — specifies the total commission rate and any cooperating broker split the seller authorizes. The VAR Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement (VAR Form 400 series) is the most widely used template in the state.

On the buyer side, the Buyer Agency Agreement (VAR Form 300 series) spells out the compensation the buyer's agent will receive and from which source. If the listing broker offers compensation, the buyer-broker may accept it, but the agreement must disclose that possibility and explain what happens if the offered amount falls short of the agreed rate. This is where many agents trip up — vague language in these forms can expose the brokerage to disputes after closing.

All commission payments must flow through the licensee's principal broker. Vermont law prohibits a salesperson from receiving compensation directly from a consumer or from another brokerage. The principal broker is responsible for disbursing earned commissions to affiliated agents according to their internal agreements. This rule applies even when teams are involved, a structure that raises its own [supervision and compliance questions](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026).

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalties for violating Vermont's licensing and commission rules range from a written reprimand to permanent license revocation. The OPR investigates complaints, and the Real Estate Commission conducts adjudicatory hearings under the Vermont Administrative Procedures Act (3 V.S.A. Chapter 25).

**Practicing without a valid license — whether through expiration, lapse, or failure to affiliate with a broker — is a criminal offense under 26 V.S.A. Section 2295.** It is classified as a misdemeanor carrying fines of up to $1,000 per violation. Beyond criminal exposure, any commission earned during an unlicensed period is legally unenforceable. A court will not help you collect it.

Commission disputes that arise from inadequate disclosure can also torpedo transactions. If a buyer discovers mid-closing that their agent failed to execute a proper buyer agency agreement, the buyer can refuse to pay the agent's fee and potentially seek rescission of the entire agreement. Sellers have brought claims against listing agents for unauthorized commission splits that reduced net proceeds without prior written consent. These disputes frequently end up in mediation or small claims court, and they almost always trigger an OPR complaint.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make

After reviewing recent OPR disciplinary orders and talking with compliance officers at Vermont brokerages, five errors surface repeatedly.

**First, agents forget to re-present the Disclosure of Brokerage Relationships form when their role changes.** If you start as a seller's agent and the buyer approaches you directly, you owe a fresh disclosure before engaging in any dual or designated agency arrangement. The Commission has sanctioned agents who assumed the original form covered all subsequent relationship shifts.

Second, agents rely on verbal commission agreements. Vermont's statute of frauds requires that agreements authorizing an agent to earn a commission on real property be in writing. A handshake deal, a text message, or even an email thread may not satisfy this requirement. The safest path is a signed VAR form — period.

Third, continuing education procrastination leads to involuntary license lapses. The OPR does not send multiple warnings. Agents who miss the deadline find their license inactive the morning after expiration, and any transaction they touch during that window is tainted. This is similar to the [deadline management problems](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that plague agents across multiple states.

Fourth, agents working on teams fail to verify that their team leader holds a broker's license when the team leader is acting in a supervisory capacity. Vermont does not have a separate "team leader" license category. If a salesperson is directing the activities of other salespersons without proper broker oversight, the entire team's transactions may be in violation.

Fifth, agents neglect to document cooperating broker compensation in the purchase and sale agreement or a separate written addendum when the MLS no longer conveys that information. This gap creates a situation where the commission source is ambiguous at closing, leading to last-minute disputes that delay or kill deals.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry the heaviest compliance burden under Vermont law. 26 V.S.A. Section 2296 makes the broker responsible for the actions of affiliated licensees. When an agent violates the rules, the Commission looks at the broker first.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of every affiliated agent's licensing status, CE completion, and form compliance.** A spreadsheet is the bare minimum — and it is not enough when you have more than a handful of agents. Checking that every transaction file contains a signed Disclosure of Brokerage Relationships form, a properly executed listing or buyer agency agreement, and documented commission terms should be a non-negotiable part of file review.

Escrow account management is another audit priority. Vermont requires that earnest money deposits be placed in the broker's escrow account within a timeline specified by the purchase agreement. Commingling escrow funds with operating funds is grounds for immediate license suspension. The Commission has revoked broker licenses for escrow violations even when the underlying transactions closed without consumer harm.

Brokers should also verify that no affiliated agent is advertising independently without the brokerage name prominently displayed. Vermont advertising rules under Commission Rule 15 require that all advertising — digital, print, signage — include the brokerage firm name. Solo agent branding that omits the firm name is a violation, and the broker will be held jointly accountable.
