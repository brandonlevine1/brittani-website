## Understanding New Hampshire 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026

Most agents assume a 1031 exchange is purely a federal tax play. In New Hampshire, the state-level picture looks different from nearly every other state, and understanding New Hampshire 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is non-negotiable if you want to advise investment clients accurately. The Granite State's unusual tax structure creates both advantages and traps that licensed agents must account for at every stage of a deferred exchange.

New Hampshire has no broad-based personal income tax. The state repealed its Interest and Dividends Tax (RSA 77) effective January 1, 2025, meaning individual capital gains—including those from real estate—do not trigger a state income tax liability. But that does not mean all 1031 exchange transactions pass through New Hampshire tax-free.

**Business entities holding investment property face the Business Profits Tax (BPT) under RSA 77-A, which applies at a rate of 7.5% for taxable periods ending in 2026.** The Business Enterprise Tax (BET) under RSA 77-E also applies at 0.55% on the enterprise value tax base. These two taxes are the primary state-level concerns when a 1031 exchange involves an LLC, partnership, S-corp, or C-corp holding title.

## How the BPT and BET Interact with a Deferred Exchange

The BPT tracks federal taxable income as its starting point. Because IRC Section 1031 defers gain recognition at the federal level, a properly structured exchange also defers BPT liability. The gain simply never appears on the federal return, so it never flows into the New Hampshire BPT calculation—provided the exchange is executed correctly.

If the exchange fails or is partially completed, the recognized gain at the federal level flows directly into the BPT return. For partnerships, this appears on Form NH-1065. For corporations and LLCs taxed as corporations, it shows up on Form NH-1120. Both forms require an attachment of the federal return schedules showing the disposition, which means any discrepancy between federal and state reporting will surface during audit.

The BET is less directly affected because it taxes compensation, interest, and dividends paid—not capital gains. However, if exchange proceeds are distributed as dividends or if the entity structure creates a compensation event, the BET calculation could shift. Agents do not need to compute these taxes, but they must flag the issue for clients holding property in entity structures.

## New Hampshire Forms and Filing Obligations Agents Should Know

Real estate agents are not tax preparers, but knowing which forms exist prevents misinformation at the listing table. Here is a breakdown of the relevant New Hampshire Department of Revenue Administration (DRA) filings that touch 1031 exchange transactions:

| Form | Entity Type | Purpose | Due Date |
|------|------------|---------|----------|
| NH-1065 | Partnerships/LLCs | Report BPT for partnership income including deferred exchange gains | 15th of 4th month after tax year end |
| NH-1120 | Corporations | Report BPT for corporate income including exchange transactions | 15th of 4th month after tax year end |
| BT-Summary | All business entities | Reconcile BPT and BET obligations | Filed with NH-1065 or NH-1120 |
| DP-10 | All entities | Interest and Dividends (legacy—no longer applicable post-2025) | N/A for 2026 |

**Agents should direct clients to their CPA or tax attorney before the exchange begins, not after the closing.** The DRA has enforcement authority under RSA 21-J to assess penalties for late or incorrect filings, including a penalty of up to 25% of the tax due for failure to file.

One critical distinction: New Hampshire does not require real estate transfer tax withholding on out-of-state sellers the way states like California (with Form 593) or Maine (with Form REW-1) do. This simplifies the closing process but also means there is no automatic state-level safety net reminding parties of potential tax obligations.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Disclose or Comply

New Hampshire's [Real Estate Commission](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) (NHREC) regulates licensee conduct under RSA 331-A. While agents are not liable for providing tax advice, they are responsible for material misrepresentation. Telling a client "there's no state tax on this exchange" without qualifying that statement based on the client's entity structure could constitute a violation of RSA 331-A:26, II—misrepresentation or omission of a material fact.

The consequences range from a formal reprimand to license suspension. NHREC disciplinary actions are public record and can affect an agent's ability to maintain E&O coverage. Beyond regulatory risk, a client who loses an exchange—and owes unexpected BPT—due to agent misinformation has grounds for a civil claim.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a buyer's 1031 exchange fails because the agent on either side miscommunicated timeline requirements or failed to coordinate with the Qualified Intermediary (QI), the transaction can collapse. The 45-day identification window and 180-day closing deadline under IRC 1031(a)(1)(B) are absolute—no extensions, no exceptions. An agent who [misses a deadline](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in this context has created a taxable event for their client.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with New Hampshire 1031 Exchanges

**First, agents frequently tell individual sellers there are "no state tax implications" without asking whether the property is held in an entity.** A single-member LLC that elected corporate taxation faces BPT exposure. The entity structure matters more than the individual's residency status.

Second, agents confuse the elimination of the Interest and Dividends Tax with a blanket exemption from all state taxes. The I&D Tax repeal (effective 2025) only removed tax on interest and dividend income. It never applied to capital gains in the first place—the BPT always covered business-entity gains. Agents who conflate these two regimes mislead clients.

Third, agents fail to coordinate closing timelines with the QI's requirements. New Hampshire title companies handle closings without mandatory attorney involvement (unlike neighboring Massachusetts), which means agents sometimes rush to close without confirming the QI has received and approved the exchange agreement. If the deed records before the QI controls the proceeds, the exchange is disqualified.

Fourth, agents neglect to document the exchange in the transaction file. Even though New Hampshire does not require a specific exchange disclosure form, the [transaction coordination checklist](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) should include QI contact information, identification deadline dates, and written confirmation that the client has independent tax counsel. Without this documentation, the broker has no defense if a complaint is filed.

Fifth, agents working cross-border deals—common in southern New Hampshire near the Massachusetts line—forget that the relinquished or replacement property in another state may trigger that state's withholding or reporting requirements. A New Hampshire agent helping a client sell a Massachusetts investment property must account for Massachusetts Form 63D-ELT or the state's 5% income tax on the gain if the exchange fails.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under RSA 331-A:16. When a transaction involves a 1031 exchange, the broker's file review must confirm several elements.

| Audit Item | What Broker Should Verify | Risk if Missing |
|-----------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| QI engagement letter | Signed agreement between client and QI is in file | Exchange could be challenged; no proof of proper structure |
| Tax counsel confirmation | Written acknowledgment that client consulted CPA/attorney | Liability exposure for agent and brokerage |
| Timeline documentation | 45-day and 180-day deadlines noted with calendar dates | Missed deadlines create taxable event |
| Entity verification | Confirmation of how title is held (individual vs. entity) | Incorrect tax guidance risk |
| Cross-border flag | Notation if replacement/relinquished property is out of state | Other state's tax obligations missed |

Brokers who skip this audit expose their firm to vicarious liability. NHREC can sanction the supervising broker independently of the agent under RSA 331-A:26, IX for failure to supervise. E&O policies typically require that the brokerage demonstrate reasonable compliance procedures were in place—a bare transaction file with no exchange documentation undermines that defense.

**Quarterly file audits should include a specific 1031 exchange checkbox, even if the brokerage only handles a handful of exchanges per year.** The cost of adding this single line item to an audit protocol is trivial compared to defending a DRA assessment or NHREC complaint.

## The Federal-State Interplay for 2026

The federal 1031 exchange rules remain intact for 2026 under IRC Section 1031 as amended by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. Real property exchanges still qualify; personal property does not. The like-kind requirement applies only to the nature of the property (real for real), not to property class or quality.

At the state level, New Hampshire's conformity to federal tax treatment means that a valid federal deferral flows through cleanly for BPT purposes. But conformity is not automatic—it depends on the DRA's administrative rules, which reference the Internal Revenue Code as of a specific date. For 2026, New Hampshire conforms to the IRC as amended through the most recent legislative update under RSA 77-A:1, which defines "gross business profits" by reference to federal gross income.

If Congress modifies or eliminates Section 1031 (a perennial legislative proposal), New Hampshire's BPT treatment would follow suit unless the state legislature decouples. Agents should monitor federal tax reform discussions and advise clients to lock in exchanges before any potential sunset date—though no active legislation threatens 1031 as of mid-2026.
