## Understanding Oregon Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Oregon's closing process differs from many states because it is an escrow-closing state—no attorneys are required at the table, and independent escrow officers employed by title companies handle the final exchange of funds and documents. For agents operating under the Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) and bound by ORS Chapter 696, understanding **Oregon title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional; it is the framework that keeps your license intact and your clients protected.

The Oregon Real Estate Commissioner enforces the administrative rules found in OAR Chapter 863, which define how brokers must supervise transactional compliance. Failing to meet these standards exposes agents and their principal brokers to disciplinary action ranging from civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation (ORS 696.990) to license revocation. This post covers the statutes, forms, pitfalls, and audit points every licensee should know heading into the second half of 2026.

## How the Title Commitment Process Works in Oregon

**A preliminary title report—often called a "title commitment" once the insurer agrees to issue a policy—is the backbone of every Oregon closing.** Title companies issue these reports under the authority of the Oregon Division of Financial Regulation, which licenses and regulates title insurers under ORS Chapter 731 and ORS 735.100 through 735.145.

The commitment contains three schedules. Schedule A identifies the proposed insured, the property legal description, and the estate or interest in land. Schedule B-I lists requirements that must be satisfied before the policy will issue—think outstanding liens, unreleased deeds of trust, or unpaid property taxes. Schedule B-II lists permanent exceptions, such as easements and CC&Rs, that the insurer will not cover.

Agents should review the commitment immediately upon receipt because Schedule B-I items drive the curative timeline. If a seller's old HELOC from 2019 shows as unreleased, the listing agent needs to coordinate payoff demands well before the closing date. Waiting until the week of signing is how deals blow up.

## Key Oregon Forms Agents Must Know

Oregon residential transactions rely on standardized forms published by Oregon Real Estate Forms LLC (OREF). The forms most relevant to title and closing include the following pairs of documents, and agents should confirm they are using the 2026 editions.

| Form Number | Form Name | Primary Use |
|---|---|---|
| OREF 001 | Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement | Establishes closing date, title insurance provisions, escrow holder selection |
| OREF 037 | Seller's Property Disclosure Statement | Required under ORS 105.465; may trigger title-related disclosures |
| OREF 041 | Addendum—Title and Escrow | Modifies title/closing terms after mutual acceptance |
| OREF 047 | Buyer's Closing Instructions | Delivered to escrow; details buyer's conditions for disbursement |
| OREF 049 | Seller's Closing Instructions | Delivered to escrow; authorizes proceeds disbursement and deed recording |

Section 8 of OREF 001 specifically addresses title insurance, defining which party selects the title company and who bears the cost. Agents who leave this section blank create ambiguity that can delay closing by days. Under OAR 863-015-0125, a broker's failure to present complete documentation to all parties can be treated as a violation of the duty to conduct business honestly and with reasonable skill.

## Escrow and Fund Disbursement Under Oregon Law

Oregon follows a deed-and-money escrow model. The escrow officer—licensed under ORS 696.505 through 696.590—holds all documents and funds in trust until every condition in the escrow instructions is satisfied. Only then does the officer record the deed and disburse proceeds.

**Recording happens at the county recorder's office, and Oregon's recording statutes under ORS Chapter 93 require that deeds be in specific form to be accepted.** A warranty deed (ORS 93.850) is standard in most residential sales, though bargain and sale deeds appear in foreclosure resales and estate transfers. If the deed does not meet the county's formatting requirements—margins, font size, return address block—it will be rejected, and the closing cannot finalize until a corrected deed is re-executed.

Funds must be "good funds" before disbursement. Under ORS 696.581, escrow agents may not disburse until deposited funds are collected or guaranteed by the depositing institution. Wire transfers satisfy this rule immediately; cashier's checks typically require one business day. Personal checks can take up to five business days to clear, which is why most escrow companies refuse them for amounts over a few hundred dollars.

## Who Pays What: Buyer vs. Seller Cost Allocation

Oregon has no statute that dictates which party pays for title insurance, escrow fees, or recording costs. Everything is negotiable under the sale agreement. That said, regional customs have calcified into near-defaults that agents should understand so they can advise clients accurately.

| Cost Item | Typical Payor (Portland Metro) | Typical Payor (Southern/Central OR) |
|---|---|---|
| Owner's title insurance premium | Seller | Seller |
| Lender's title insurance premium | Buyer | Buyer |
| Escrow fee | Split 50/50 | Split 50/50 |
| Recording fee (deed) | Buyer | Buyer |
| Recording fee (reconveyance) | Seller | Seller |
| Transfer tax (Washington County) | Seller | N/A |
| City of Portland transfer tax (if applicable) | Negotiable—often seller | N/A |

Note that the City of Portland's Real Estate Transfer Tax, enacted under Portland City Code 7.02, applies to sales of $100,000 or more within city limits. The rate structure is tiered: 1% on sales priced $100,000 to $500,000, escalating to 1.25% above $500,000 and up to certain thresholds established by ordinance. Agents listing in Portland must account for this cost in net sheets or risk a seller receiving a materially lower net than expected.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Oregon Closings

**Errors during the closing phase of an Oregon transaction are not just inconvenient—they create liability exposure that follows an agent's license record.** The Oregon Real Estate Agency maintains a public disciplinary database, and closed complaints remain visible for years. Here are five specific mistakes that surface repeatedly.

First, agents fail to deliver the signed sale agreement to escrow within the contractual time frame. OREF 001 Section 19 requires delivery within two business days of mutual acceptance unless otherwise agreed. Missing this window can give the other party grounds to question whether a binding agreement exists, especially if market conditions shift.

Second, listing agents neglect to order a preliminary title report early enough to cure defects. A title search in Multnomah or Clackamas County can surface boundary disputes, federal tax liens, or mechanic's liens from unpaid contractors. If an agent waits until week three of a 30-day escrow to discover a $40,000 IRS lien, the transaction almost certainly misses its closing date.

Third, agents conflate the property disclosure requirement with title disclosure. ORS 105.465 mandates the seller's property disclosure, but title-related defects—like an unrecorded easement the seller knows about—must also be disclosed. An agent who coaches a seller to omit known title issues faces personal liability under ORS 696.301 for misrepresentation.

Fourth, buyer's agents sometimes instruct clients to wire earnest money before confirming escrow account details through a verified callback number. This is the most common entry point for wire fraud, and it exposes the agent to claims of negligence. The [reasons deals collapse](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often trace back to exactly this kind of preventable process failure.

Fifth, agents forget to verify that all contingencies have been formally released in writing before the closing date. Under OREF 001, contingency removal requires a signed release delivered to the other party. An oral "we're good" does not satisfy the written-instrument requirement, and a buyer who never signs a contingency release retains the right to terminate.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Oregon carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.020 and OAR 863-015-0215. If an affiliated agent commits a procedural violation during closing, the broker faces potential discipline from the Commissioner even if the broker had no direct involvement in the transaction.

**Brokers should conduct periodic file audits that verify four specific closing-related items.** One: confirm that the sale agreement was delivered to escrow within two business days, documented by an email timestamp or escrow receipt. Two: verify that the preliminary title report was reviewed by the agent within five days of receipt and that any Schedule B-I deficiencies were communicated to the client in writing. Three: confirm that contingency releases exist as signed documents in the file, not just verbal confirmations logged in a CRM note. Four: ensure that closing disclosure documents (the TRID Closing Disclosure for financed transactions) were received by the buyer at least three business days before signing, as required by federal TILA-RESPA rules.

Brokers who rely on [memory rather than documented checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) inevitably miss gaps in supervision. The administrative rules do not care whether a broker intended to review the file—they care whether the review happened and can be evidenced.

Under OAR 863-015-0270, transaction records must be retained for six years. This includes escrow instructions, title commitments, closing statements, and all correspondence related to title curative work. Brokers who allow agents to maintain files solely on personal devices or in non-backed-up folders are courting disaster in the event of a complaint investigation.

## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for non-compliance fall into three categories: regulatory, civil, and transactional. Regulatory consequences come from the Oregon Real Estate Agency, which can impose civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation under [ORS 696.990](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors696.html), suspend or revoke a license, or require additional education. A pattern of violations—such as repeated failure to deliver documents to escrow—can trigger an elevated review that examines the broker's entire office.

Civil liability arises when a client suffers financial harm due to an agent's negligence. If a buyer loses earnest money because the agent failed to ensure a contingency was properly documented, the agent and their brokerage face a potential professional liability claim. Oregon's statute of limitations for negligence is two years (ORS 12.110), but claims related to real property fraud extend to six years (ORS 12.080).

Transactional consequences are the most immediate. A missed closing date caused by an agent's failure to coordinate title curative work can trigger per-diem penalties written into the sale agreement, give the non-breaching party a right to terminate, or force a price renegotiation. None of these outcomes serve the client, and all of them erode the agent's reputation in a referral-driven business.
