## Wisconsin FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Federal-State Double Layer

Most Wisconsin agents close dozens of transactions before encountering a foreign seller—and when they finally do, the compliance gap can be staggering. Understanding Wisconsin firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 is not optional; it is a binary pass-fail obligation that exposes buyers, agents, and closing companies to direct financial liability if mishandled. The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (FIRPTA), codified at 26 U.S.C. Sections 1445-1446, mandates that buyers withhold a percentage of the gross sale price when a seller is a "foreign person" as defined by federal tax law.

Wisconsin compounds this federal requirement with its own nonresident withholding rules under Chapter 71 of the Wisconsin Statutes. That means agents working with foreign sellers face two separate withholding calculations, two different taxing authorities, and two sets of forms. Missing either one can unravel a deal or create post-closing liability that follows the buyer for years.

## How Federal FIRPTA Withholding Works in a Wisconsin Transaction

**The standard federal withholding rate under FIRPTA is 15% of the gross sale price—not 15% of the gain, but 15% of the total amount realized.** This catches many first-timers off guard. On a $400,000 residential sale, that means $60,000 must be remitted to the IRS within 20 days of closing unless an exemption applies or a withholding certificate reduces the amount.

The transferee (buyer) is the party legally obligated to withhold. In practice, the closing agent—whether that is a Wisconsin real estate attorney or a title company—handles the mechanics of collection and remittance using IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons). Agents should confirm with the closer that both forms will be filed within the 20-day window.

Sellers may apply for a reduced withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B if the tax owed on the gain will be less than 15% of the sale price. Processing typically takes 90 days, which means the application must be filed well before closing or the funds will sit in escrow. Wisconsin agents who wait until the week of closing to address FIRPTA are setting up a delay or cancellation scenario.

## Wisconsin's Additional State-Level Withholding Under Statute 71.775

Wisconsin does not simply piggyback on federal FIRPTA. Under [Wisconsin Statute 71.775](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/71/xii/775), a withholding obligation exists whenever a nonresident sells Wisconsin real property. The statute requires the buyer or closing agent to withhold an amount equal to the "amount realized" multiplied by the highest individual income tax rate—currently 7.65% for 2026.

**The state withholding is reported and remitted to the Wisconsin Department of Revenue (DOR) using Form PW-1 (Wisconsin Nonresident Income or Franchise Tax Withholding on Pass-Through Entity Income) or the applicable nonresident withholding form specified by the DOR for direct real estate sales.** Sellers who believe their actual Wisconsin tax liability will be lower can request a reduced withholding by filing Form PW-2 with the DOR before closing.

The interplay between these two layers creates a scenario where a foreign seller could see more than 22% of gross proceeds withheld at closing. Agents need to set seller expectations early—ideally at listing—and refer the seller to a CPA or tax attorney who handles international transactions.

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | Wisconsin State |
|---|---|---|
| Governing Law | 26 U.S.C. § 1445 | Wis. Stat. § 71.775 |
| Standard Rate | 15% of gross sale price | 7.65% of amount realized |
| Forms Used | IRS 8288, 8288-A, 8288-B | DOR Form PW-1 / PW-2 |
| Remittance Deadline | 20 days after closing | 20 days after closing |
| Reduction Request | Form 8288-B to IRS | Form PW-2 to WI DOR |
| Liable Party | Buyer (transferee) | Buyer or closing agent |

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance are not theoretical. If the buyer fails to withhold, the IRS can assess the full 15% plus interest and penalties against the buyer personally. The IRS may also place a tax lien on the property itself under 26 U.S.C. § 1445(e). At the state level, the Wisconsin DOR can assess the withholding amount plus a 25% penalty against the person who failed to withhold, per Wis. Stat. § 71.775(4).

Listing agents and buyer's agents are not the "withholding agent" under the statute—that role falls to the buyer or closing company. However, agents who affirmatively misrepresent a seller's residency status, fail to disclose known foreign-person status, or instruct a closer to skip withholding can face disciplinary action from the Wisconsin Department of Safety and Professional Services (DSPS), which oversees real estate licenses. DSPS has authority under Wis. Admin. Code RL 24.04 to sanction agents for incompetence or untrustworthiness.

Deal cancellation is the other practical risk. Title companies will refuse to close without proper FIRPTA documentation. If the issue surfaces at the last minute, the transaction stalls, the buyer walks, and the agent's reputation takes a hit. This is why proper [title and closing procedures](/blog/wisconsin-title-and-closing-procedures-2026) must account for foreign seller status from day one.

## Common Mistakes Wisconsin Agents Make With FIRPTA

**Error number one: assuming "foreign" means "non-English speaker" or "lives overseas."** Under FIRPTA, a foreign person is any individual who is not a U.S. citizen or resident alien for tax purposes. A Canadian citizen who has lived in Milwaukee for five years but never obtained a green card may still qualify as a foreign person depending on their tax filing status. The test is tax residency, not physical presence.

Error number two: relying on the seller's verbal representation without collecting a written certification. The Non-Foreign Affidavit (also called a FIRPTA Affidavit or Certification of Non-Foreign Status) must be signed by the seller and include their name, U.S. taxpayer identification number, and home address. Without this document, the buyer is legally obligated to withhold—period.

Error number three: confusing the personal residence exception as a blanket exemption. The buyer's intended use as a residence only eliminates withholding if the purchase price is $300,000 or less. Between $300,001 and $1,000,000, the rate drops to 10% but does not disappear. Above $1,000,000, the full 15% applies regardless of intended use.

Error number four: ignoring Wisconsin's separate withholding. Agents who handle the federal FIRPTA forms but forget the state PW-1 filing leave the buyer exposed to a DOR assessment. Both withholdings must be addressed at closing.

Error number five: failing to involve tax counsel early enough for a withholding certificate application. The IRS can take 90 days or longer to process Form 8288-B. Filing it two weeks before closing guarantees either a delay or full withholding at the table. Agents listing a property with a foreign seller should advise the seller to engage a tax attorney within the first week of the listing period.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Wisconsin brokers operating under Wis. Stat. § 452.14 have supervisory duties that extend to FIRPTA compliance awareness. A broker cannot ignore systemic knowledge gaps among their agents when those gaps expose clients to five- and six-figure tax liabilities. Brokers should build three specific audit checkpoints into their transaction management workflow.

First, verify that every listing file includes either a signed Non-Foreign Affidavit or a documented foreign-seller flag that triggers the FIRPTA workflow. The [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) process is the natural point to collect this certification. Adding a question about citizenship or tax residency to the intake checklist costs nothing and prevents surprises.

| Broker Audit Checkpoint | Timing | Action Required |
|---|---|---|
| Seller citizenship/residency status | Listing intake | Collect Non-Foreign Affidavit or flag file |
| FIRPTA withholding calculation | Pre-closing review | Confirm 15% federal + 7.65% state withheld or exemption documented |
| Form 8288-B status (if applicable) | 90+ days before closing | Verify IRS receipt and tracking number |
| State PW-1 / PW-2 filing | Closing day | Confirm title company or attorney will remit to WI DOR |
| Post-closing confirmation | 20 days after closing | Verify IRS and DOR remittances completed |

Second, brokers should require their agents to confirm in writing—via email or transaction management notes—that the closing agent is aware of the foreign seller's status and will handle withholding. This creates an audit trail that protects the brokerage if a closer drops the ball. Documentation habits like these are [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in high-stakes compliance situations.

Third, annual training on FIRPTA should be part of every Wisconsin brokerage's continuing education planning. The Wisconsin DSPS requires 18 hours of CE per two-year cycle, and at least some of that time should address federal tax withholding for international transactions. Brokers who treat FIRPTA as "the closer's problem" are one missed affidavit away from a regulatory inquiry.

## Practical Steps for Agents Representing Foreign Sellers in 2026

Start at the listing appointment. Ask directly whether the seller is a U.S. citizen or resident alien for federal income tax purposes. If the answer is no—or if there is any ambiguity—document it immediately and recommend the seller retain a tax attorney who handles cross-border transactions.

Next, confirm with the [closing attorney or title company](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026) that they have experience handling FIRPTA transactions. Not every Wisconsin closer processes foreign seller deals regularly, and inexperience at the closing table introduces risk. A closer who has handled fewer than five FIRPTA transactions may not catch edge cases like LLC ownership structures, treaty exemptions, or dual-status filers.

Build the withholding amount into the seller's net sheet from day one. A seller who discovers at closing that $90,000 of a $600,000 sale will be withheld is a seller who may refuse to close. Transparency eliminates eleventh-hour blowups.
