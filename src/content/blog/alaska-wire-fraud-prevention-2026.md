# Alaska Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Every Agent Must Do Before Closing

Real estate wire fraud losses in the United States exceeded $446 million in 2023, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Alaska is not immune, and the state has responded with regulatory expectations that demand action from every licensee. Understanding Alaska wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional — it is a baseline competency that the Alaska Real Estate Commission (AREC) expects agents and brokers to demonstrate in every single transaction.

The threat is specific and well-documented. A bad actor intercepts email communications between a buyer, agent, and title company, then sends spoofed wiring instructions that redirect closing funds to a fraudulent account. By the time anyone realizes what happened, the money is gone. Alaska's relatively small market and close-knit professional community might feel like a buffer, but cybercriminals do not care about population density.

## How Alaska Regulates Wire Fraud Prevention

Alaska does not have a standalone wire fraud prevention statute the way some states do. Instead, AREC enforces wire fraud prevention obligations through existing licensee conduct standards found in [Alaska Statute 08.88.401](https://www.akleg.gov/basis/statutes.asp#08.88.401), which establishes the grounds for disciplinary action against licensees who fail to act with reasonable skill and care. The Commission has issued guidance letters making clear that failure to warn clients about wire fraud schemes falls within the scope of conduct that may violate AS 08.88.391 (duties of licensees) and the associated administrative code at 12 AAC 64.

**AREC treats wire fraud advisory failures the same way it treats other material disclosure failures.** If you would not skip a lead paint disclosure or a property condition report, you should not skip a wire fraud advisory. The Commission's position, reinforced in its 2025 licensee bulletin, is that agents have an affirmative duty to educate clients about known transaction risks, and wire fraud is now among the most prominent risks in any closing.

The Alaska Association of Realtors (AAR) supports this position through its standard forms library, which includes Form 7030 — the Wire Fraud Advisory and Acknowledgment. This form is designed to be signed by all parties and retained in the transaction file.

## The Forms You Need to Know

Form 7030 is the primary document Alaska agents should be using. It warns clients that criminals may attempt to intercept wiring instructions, advises them never to trust wire instructions received solely by email, and instructs them to verify all wiring details by calling a phone number they have independently confirmed. The form also includes a hold-harmless acknowledgment.

Some brokerages require a supplemental wire fraud disclosure that goes beyond Form 7030. Coldwell Banker, Keller Williams, and RE/MAX offices operating in Alaska often have proprietary addenda that address the brokerage's specific liability concerns. If your brokerage has one, you must use both it and the AAR form unless your broker instructs otherwise.

**The timing of form delivery matters as much as the form itself.** AREC guidance and best practice standards call for the wire fraud advisory to be delivered at the earliest practical point in the transaction — ideally at the time of the purchase agreement or during the initial buyer consultation, not three days before closing when funds are already in motion.

| Form / Document | Purpose | When to Deliver | Who Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| AAR Form 7030 | Wire fraud warning and acknowledgment | At or before purchase agreement execution | Buyer, seller, and agents |
| Brokerage supplemental wire fraud addendum | Additional brokerage-specific liability coverage | Same time as Form 7030 | Varies by brokerage |
| Title company wire instructions letter | Official wiring details from escrow/title | 3-7 days before closing | Title company issues; buyer acknowledges |
| Verbal verification log | Agent's record of confirming wire details by phone | Day of or day before wire transfer | Agent notes in file |

This documentation chain is what protects you. If a client wires funds to a fraudulent account and you cannot produce a signed Form 7030 and evidence that you advised verbal verification, your exposure is significant.

## What Happens If You Do Not Comply

The penalties for failing to meet wire fraud prevention obligations in Alaska fall into three categories: regulatory, civil, and reputational.

On the regulatory side, AREC can impose fines of up to $25,000 per violation under AS 08.88.401. The Commission can also suspend or revoke your license. These penalties apply even if no actual fraud occurs — the failure to provide the advisory itself constitutes the violation. AREC has broad discretion, and in cases where a client actually lost money, the Commission has historically pursued the maximum available sanctions.

Civil liability is the larger financial risk. An Alaska court can hold an agent personally liable for a client's wire fraud losses if the agent failed to warn the client or failed to follow reasonable industry standards for fraud prevention. Under Alaska's tort law framework, the duty of care owed by a real estate licensee to a client is well established, and wire fraud advisories are now squarely within the scope of that duty. A single residential closing in Anchorage or Juneau can involve wire transfers of $400,000 or more, meaning a single failure could result in a judgment that exceeds your E&O policy limits.

**Reputational damage in Alaska's tight-knit real estate community can end a career faster than a fine.** Word travels quickly among title companies, lenders, and agents. A single wire fraud incident tied to an agent's negligence will follow that licensee for years. ## Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make

Five specific errors come up repeatedly in AREC complaint files and brokerage audits related to wire fraud prevention.

First, agents deliver Form 7030 too late in the transaction. Handing a wire fraud advisory to a buyer at the closing table when the wire has already been sent is performative, not protective. The advisory must be delivered early enough for the client to act on it.

Second, agents rely on email to transmit wire instructions without adding a fraud warning to every message. AREC expects that any email containing or referencing financial details includes a clear, conspicuous wire fraud warning in the body of the message — not buried in a signature block disclaimer that no one reads. A footer disclaimer alone does not satisfy the duty of care.

Third, agents fail to document verbal verification calls. You told your buyer to call the title company and confirm the routing number by phone. Great. But if you did not log that conversation — the date, the phone number used, the person contacted — your file has a gap that a plaintiff's attorney will exploit. Documentation is your only proof.

Fourth, agents assume the title company or lender handles all wire fraud prevention. Title companies in Alaska do issue their own wire instructions and warnings, but AREC's position is that the agent's duty to the client is independent. You cannot delegate your fiduciary obligation to a third party and claim you had no responsibility.

Fifth, agents use the same email address and password across multiple platforms without two-factor authentication. Compromised agent email accounts are the most common entry point for wire fraud schemes. This is not just an IT issue — it is a licensing issue, because AREC can argue that failing to secure your communications constitutes a failure to protect client interests under AS 08.88.391.

## Alaska Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Brokers Must Audit

Brokers have a supervisory obligation under AS 08.88.395 that extends to wire fraud compliance. A managing broker who fails to establish and enforce wire fraud prevention policies within the brokerage risks personal disciplinary action, not just firm-level consequences.

At minimum, brokers should audit every closed transaction file for the presence of a signed Form 7030, delivered at an appropriate point in the timeline. The audit should also confirm that any brokerage-specific supplemental forms are present. Spot-checking email communications for proper wire fraud warnings is a best practice that several Anchorage-area brokerages have adopted as mandatory quarterly procedure.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signed AAR Form 7030 in file | Every transaction | Signatures from all parties, dated at or before purchase agreement | AREC fine, civil liability exposure |
| Brokerage supplemental form | Every transaction (if applicable) | Proper execution, consistent with brokerage policy manual | Internal compliance violation |
| Email wire fraud disclaimers | Quarterly spot-check | Body-of-email warnings on messages referencing financial details | Regulatory and civil exposure |
| Agent cybersecurity compliance | Annually | Two-factor authentication, unique passwords, encrypted email | Potential AREC action under AS 08.88.391 |
| Verbal verification documentation | Every transaction involving a wire | Log entry with date, phone number, contact name | Gaps in defense if fraud occurs |

**Brokers who do not have a written wire fraud prevention policy in their office policy manual are operating with unnecessary risk.** AREC auditors and plaintiff's attorneys will both look for this document. The policy should specify which forms are required, when they must be delivered, how wire instructions are communicated, and what cybersecurity standards agents must meet. Training is also a broker obligation. Annual wire fraud prevention training should be documented with sign-in sheets and stored in the brokerage's compliance records. AREC has not yet mandated specific continuing education hours dedicated to wire fraud, but the Commission's 2025 bulletin strongly recommends it, and the trend across other states suggests a formal requirement may be coming.

## Building a Verification Protocol That Actually Works

A written protocol eliminates ambiguity. Every Alaska brokerage should have a step-by-step wire verification process that agents follow without exception.

The protocol starts with a rule: wire instructions are never sent by email alone. They should be delivered through a secure portal or encrypted transmission, with a follow-up phone call to a number the client obtained independently — not from the same email containing the instructions. The agent should remind the client of this process at least twice: once when the Form 7030 is signed and again three to five days before the expected wire date.

Agents should also coordinate directly with the title company to confirm the title company's own fraud prevention measures. In Alaska, title companies regulated under AS 21.66 are required to maintain security protocols, but the specifics vary. Knowing your title company's process — and documenting that you confirmed it — adds another layer of protection to your file.
