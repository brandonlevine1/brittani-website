## Understanding Hawaii Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Hawaii's regulatory framework for earnest money deposits and escrow handling differs meaningfully from mainland states, and agents who treat it as a formality put their licenses at risk. The rules governing Hawaii earnest money & escrow laws 2026 stem primarily from Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 467 (Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons), HRS Chapter 449 (Escrow Depositories), and the Hawaii Administrative Rules Title 16, Chapter 99. Every licensed agent and principal broker operating in the state needs current fluency in these provisions because enforcement actions by the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC) have increased in recent audit cycles.

The deposit itself functions as a good-faith signal from buyer to seller, but in Hawaii the procedural requirements around its handling carry independent legal weight. Mishandle the timeline, the account, or the disbursement paperwork and you face fines, license discipline, or deal-killing litigation—regardless of whether the underlying transaction closes.

## Statutory Framework: Where the Rules Live

**HRS Section 467-14 enumerates the grounds for license revocation and suspension, and several subsections apply directly to earnest money mishandling.** Subsection (8) covers commingling of funds, subsection (13) addresses failure to deposit client funds promptly, and subsection (15) catches any conduct demonstrating incompetency or untrustworthiness. A single earnest money violation can trigger multiple charges under this statute.

Hawaii Administrative Rules Section 16-99-3 details trust fund handling requirements for brokerages that hold deposits directly. The rule mandates that all monies received on behalf of others be deposited into a federally insured trust account within five business days of contract acceptance. The account must be clearly labeled as a trust account and maintained at a Hawaii financial institution.

For transactions where a third-party escrow company holds the deposit—the more common arrangement on Oahu and the Neighbor Islands—HRS Chapter 449 governs the escrow depository's obligations. The [Hawaii Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA)](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) oversees both HIREC and escrow depository licensing, creating a single regulatory umbrella for both sides of the deposit chain.

## Key Forms Agents Must Know

Hawaii Association of Realtors (HAR) standard forms drive most residential transactions. The form that sets earnest money terms is the **Deposit, Receipt, Offer and Acceptance (DROA)**, officially designated as HAR Form RR (Revised). Paragraph 3 of the DROA specifies the deposit amount, the identity of the escrow holder, and the timeline for deposit.

Additional forms tied to earnest money and escrow handling include:

| Form | Purpose | When Used |
|------|---------|-----------|
| HAR Form RR (DROA) | Sets deposit amount, escrow holder, and delivery deadline | At offer submission |
| HAR Form CR (Counter Offer) | May modify deposit terms or escrow designation | During negotiation |
| HAR Form CA (Cancellation and Mutual Release) | Authorizes release of deposit after deal fails | At cancellation |
| HAR Form EI (Escrow Instructions) | Provides escrow company with transaction terms | After acceptance |
| HIREC Trust Fund Record | Broker's internal ledger for deposits held in trust | Ongoing compliance |

Agents must confirm that the DROA's deposit clause matches the actual escrow instructions delivered to the escrow company. Discrepancies between these documents are a top source of disputes when deals unwind, and they create audit exposure for the brokerage.

## Deposit Timelines and Delivery Obligations

**The five-business-day deposit window under HAR Section 16-99-3(f) starts running on the date of mutual acceptance, not the date the buyer hands over the check.** If the DROA specifies a different timeline—say, three calendar days—the contractual provision controls, provided it doesn't violate the statutory minimum protections for consumers.

Here's how the timeline typically unfolds in a standard Hawaii residential transaction:

| Event | Typical Timeline |
|-------|-----------------|
| Buyer delivers deposit check to agent | Within 2 business days of offer |
| Agent delivers check to escrow company | Same day or next business day |
| Escrow deposits into trust account | Within 5 business days of acceptance |
| Buyer's contingency period begins | Per DROA terms (often 15-20 days) |
| Deposit becomes "hard" (non-refundable) | Upon contingency waiver or expiration |
| Disbursement at closing or cancellation | Per escrow instructions or Form CA |

Agents who hold checks in their desk drawers or car consoles while waiting for the seller to counter are playing with fire. Even if no harm results, the act of delaying delivery is itself a violation that HIREC can cite during a routine audit.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

HIREC has broad enforcement authority under HRS 467-14. Penalties for earnest money violations range from formal reprimands to license revocation, with monetary fines up to $2,500 per violation. The Commission can also require restitution to harmed parties and order continuing education as a condition of license reinstatement.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose deposit is lost due to agent negligence or commingling can sue for the full deposit amount plus consequential damages. Hawaii courts have also recognized claims for breach of fiduciary duty in these scenarios, which can open the door to punitive damages in egregious cases.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical consequence. If a seller or buyer discovers that the deposit was never properly delivered to escrow, either party may treat the failure as a material breach. In a hot market, that means the seller relists and the buyer loses the property—and the agent loses both the commission and the client relationship.

## Common Mistakes Hawaii Agents Make

Years of HIREC disciplinary decisions reveal patterns. The following five errors account for a disproportionate share of enforcement actions and civil claims related to earnest money.

**First, agents accept personal checks made out to themselves rather than the escrow company or brokerage trust account.** This creates an immediate commingling risk the moment the check touches a personal deposit slot, even briefly. The DROA's escrow designation exists precisely to prevent this—agents must ensure the payee line matches the designated holder.

Second, agents fail to track the calendar when a buyer requests a deposit extension. The DROA allows modifications by mutual written agreement, but if the extension isn't documented on a signed addendum or Form CR before the original deadline passes, the deposit is technically delinquent. Some agents rely on verbal agreements with the other side, which have no enforceability and no audit trail.

Third, agents on the listing side release deposits without mutual written cancellation instructions. Hawaii law requires both parties to sign off before funds move. When a deal falls apart and the listing agent verbally tells the escrow company to "send it back to the buyer," the agent has exceeded their authority—and exposed their broker to liability if the seller later claims entitlement to the deposit.

Fourth, agents ignore the distinction between "initial deposit" and "additional deposit" provisions in the DROA. Many Hawaii purchase contracts call for a second deposit upon contingency removal. Missing that second deadline doesn't just breach the contract—it can trigger a default notice and forfeiture of the initial deposit under specific DROA language.

Fifth, agents working with out-of-state buyers fail to account for wire transfer timing. A wire initiated on day four of a five-day window may not arrive until day six or seven due to intermediary bank processing. The deposit obligation isn't satisfied until funds actually land in the escrow trust account, not when the wire is initiated.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry personal liability for trust fund violations committed by their sponsored agents under HRS 467-1.6. HIREC expects brokers to maintain active supervision, and "I didn't know" is not a defense that has historically worked in disciplinary proceedings.

**Every brokerage should conduct monthly trust account reconciliations and maintain a ledger that ties each deposit to a specific transaction file.** Hawaii Administrative Rules 16-99-3 requires that these records be available for HIREC inspection upon request, with no advance notice required. A broker who cannot produce a current reconciliation during a random audit faces an immediate citation.

Brokers must also verify that agents are using current HAR form versions. HIREC periodically updates approved forms, and using outdated versions—particularly outdated DROA or cancellation forms—can create ambiguity about deposit terms that exposes the brokerage to claims from both sides of a failed transaction.

Transaction file audits should confirm three things for every deal: (1) the deposit was delivered to the designated holder within the contractual or statutory timeline, (2) the escrow receipt or trust account entry matches the DROA amount, and (3) any cancellation or disbursement is supported by a signed Form CA or equivalent written instructions. Brokers who implement this three-point check as a [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) catch problems before they become regulatory complaints.

Training is the other half of the equation. Quarterly compliance meetings that walk agents through recent HIREC disciplinary decisions—which are published on the DCCA website—give real-world context to abstract rules. Agents who understand that a colleague lost their license over a seven-day deposit delay tend to take the five-day rule seriously.

## Escrow Company Selection and Agent Responsibilities

Hawaii agents don't have to hold deposits themselves, and most shouldn't. Directing deposits to a licensed escrow company under HRS Chapter 449 shifts much of the custodial risk off the brokerage, but it doesn't eliminate the agent's responsibility to confirm delivery and proper handling.

The agent's obligation doesn't end at the escrow company's front door. If the escrow company fails to deposit funds timely—or worse, commingles or misappropriates them—the agent may still face discipline if HIREC determines the agent selected a company they knew or should have known was operating improperly. Due diligence on escrow company licensing status through the DCCA's online portal takes sixty seconds and creates a defensible record.

Agents should also understand that CFPB rules under RESPA still apply in Hawaii. Receiving a referral fee or kickback from an escrow company in exchange for directing deposits to them is a federal violation, regardless of Hawaii state law. The penalties stack: a RESPA violation plus a HIREC ethics violation plus potential MLS disciplinary action.
