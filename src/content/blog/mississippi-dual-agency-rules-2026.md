## Understanding Mississippi Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Mississippi permits dual agency, but the state draws a hard line on informed consent and timing. If you hold a license under the Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC), understanding the Mississippi dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 is not optional—it is a condition of keeping your license active and your transactions enforceable. The rules sit primarily in Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-35-5 and the MREC administrative regulations that implement it.

The distinction between dual agency and designated agency matters more than many agents realize. Dual agency means a single licensee represents both buyer and seller in the same transaction, with both parties' written consent. Designated agency means the broker assigns one licensee within the firm to represent the buyer and another to represent the seller, preserving fiduciary duties on each side.

Mississippi does not treat these arrangements identically, and the disclosure forms differ. Getting them confused—or worse, skipping them—creates the kind of liability that ends careers.

## The Statutory Framework: Mississippi Code 73-35-5

**Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-35-5 establishes the legal foundation for all agency relationships in residential and commercial real estate transactions.** The statute defines the duties owed under single agency, dual agency, and designated agency. It also specifies that a licensee owes every customer (non-client) duties of honesty, good faith, and fair dealing, even when no agency relationship exists.

Under this statute, a dual agent cannot advocate for either party's negotiating position. The agent's role shifts from advocate to facilitator—a distinction that many agents fail to communicate clearly to their clients. The agent must still maintain confidentiality of each party's motivations and must not disclose the highest price the buyer will pay or the lowest price the seller will accept unless specifically authorized in writing.

The [Mississippi Real Estate Commission](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/) publishes guidance documents and disciplinary decisions that further clarify how these statutes are enforced. Agents who assume they can rely on general principles from other states are making a dangerous bet.

## Disclosure Forms and Timing Requirements

Mississippi mandates two primary forms for agency disclosure, and the timing of their delivery is non-negotiable.

**MREC Form 1 (Consumer Agency Disclosure)** must be presented at first substantial contact with a prospective buyer or seller. "First substantial contact" means the point at which you begin discussing specific property information, financial qualifications, or motivation to buy or sell. Handing this form over at the offer stage is too late.

**MREC Form 2 (Consent to Dual Agency)** must be executed before the licensee begins acting as a dual agent. If you are already representing a seller and an unrepresented buyer walks into your open house wanting to write an offer on that property, you must present Form 2 and obtain signatures from both parties before drafting that offer.

| Form | Purpose | Timing | Required Signatures |
|------|---------|--------|---------------------|
| MREC Form 1 | Disclose agency relationship options | First substantial contact | Prospective client |
| MREC Form 2 | Consent to dual agency | Before dual agency begins | Both buyer and seller |
| Designated Agency Addendum (brokerage-specific) | Assign separate agents within firm | Before representation begins | Both parties and designated agents |
| Broker Acknowledgment | Confirm broker oversight of designated agency | At assignment | Qualifying broker |

For designated agency, MREC does not prescribe a single mandatory form number the way it does for dual agency, but brokerages are expected to maintain written documentation of the designation. Most firms use a brokerage-specific designated agency addendum that identifies the two agents and confirms the information barriers in place.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: Side-by-Side Comparison

Agents frequently conflate these two arrangements, and that confusion has real consequences for fiduciary duties and liability exposure.

| Factor | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| Number of agents involved | One licensee represents both parties | Two licensees from the same brokerage represent separate parties |
| Fiduciary duties | Limited to facilitation; no advocacy | Full fiduciary duties preserved for each designated agent |
| Confidentiality | Agent cannot share either party's confidential information | Each agent advocates for their client; broker maintains neutrality |
| Consent form | MREC Form 2 required | Brokerage-specific written disclosure required |
| Broker's role | Supervises the single dual agent | Acts as neutral supervisor between two designated agents |
| Risk level | Higher—potential for perceived bias | Lower—duties remain clear and separated |

**Designated agency exists because it gives brokerages a way to handle in-house transactions without stripping clients of advocacy.** The qualifying broker becomes the dual agent in a designated agency arrangement, while the two designated licensees maintain their individual fiduciary roles. This means the broker must not share confidential information received from one side with the other designated agent.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalties for dual agency disclosure violations in Mississippi range from administrative discipline to transaction rescission and civil liability.

MREC has the authority to impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation, suspend a license for a defined period, or revoke a license entirely for egregious or repeated violations. Disciplinary proceedings are published and become part of the licensee's permanent record. Beyond the Commission, an aggrieved party can pursue civil remedies including rescission of the purchase contract and monetary damages.

**A buyer or seller who was never informed of dual agency can petition to unwind the transaction.** Courts in Mississippi have held that undisclosed dual agency constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty, which can trigger liability for the agent, the broker, and potentially the brokerage's errors and omissions carrier. If the E&O insurer determines the agent willfully failed to disclose, coverage may be denied entirely.

Even where no financial harm occurred, the mere failure to disclose is itself a violation. MREC does not require a complainant to prove they were damaged—only that the agent did not comply with the statutory disclosure requirements.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Mississippi Dual Agency Transactions

Three to five specific errors recur in MREC disciplinary files and civil litigation year after year.

The first mistake is delivering MREC Form 1 at the wrong time. Agents wait until the offer is being written, which defeats the purpose of informed consent. The form exists so consumers can make a knowing choice about representation before they are emotionally or financially committed to a specific transaction.

The second mistake is treating verbal acknowledgment as sufficient. Mississippi requires written consent. An agent who testifies that "the buyer said it was fine" at a disciplinary hearing will not prevail. No signature, no consent—period.

The third mistake involves failing to maintain information barriers in designated agency. Two agents from the same office share a bullpen, attend the same meetings, and naturally discuss active transactions. Without documented protocols for information separation, the designation becomes a legal fiction that will not survive scrutiny.

The fourth mistake is allowing an unlicensed assistant or transaction coordinator to present the agency disclosure forms. [Understanding what a transaction coordinator does and does not do](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) matters here—form presentation and explanation must be handled by a licensed individual, while a TC can ensure the signed forms are properly filed and tracked.

The fifth mistake is the qualifying broker failing to document the designated agency assignment. If the broker cannot produce a written record showing when and how the designation was made, the regulatory presumption defaults to undisclosed dual agency.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Qualifying brokers in Mississippi carry personal liability for their agents' disclosure failures. MREC holds brokers responsible for supervisory compliance, and a pattern of violations within a single office can result in the broker's license being sanctioned even if the broker personally disclosed properly in their own transactions.

**Brokers should audit every transaction file for three specific items: a signed MREC Form 1 dated before or at first substantial contact, a signed MREC Form 2 in any dual agency file, and written designated agency documentation in any in-house deal.** Spot-checking is not enough—every file needs to be reviewed before closing.

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy that defines the firm's approach to dual and designated agency. This policy should specify whether the firm permits dual agency at all (some Mississippi brokerages have opted out entirely), the procedure for initiating designated agency, and the physical or digital information barriers that prevent cross-contamination of client data.

A quarterly compliance review that [examines pipeline files for missing disclosures](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) is one of the most effective ways to catch errors before they metastasize into complaints. Document the review itself—dates, files examined, findings, and corrections made.

## Practical Steps for Agents Entering Dual or Designated Agency

When a dual agency situation arises mid-transaction, stop and reset before proceeding. Pull out MREC Form 2, explain the shift in your role from advocate to facilitator, and give both parties the opportunity to seek independent representation. Document the conversation and the client's decision in your file notes.

For designated agency, the trigger point is earlier. The moment your brokerage receives an inquiry on a listing held by a fellow agent, the broker must decide whether to designate. That decision and the resulting assignment should be documented before the buyer's agent provides any property-specific guidance.
