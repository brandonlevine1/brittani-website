## Understanding Utah Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

Wire fraud losses in real estate exceeded $446 million nationally in 2024, and Utah has responded with enforcement actions that put agents squarely in the crosshairs. The Utah wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 framework builds on existing Division of Real Estate rules, adding new expectations for licensee conduct during the closing process. If you hold a Utah real estate license, these are not optional suggestions.

The Utah Division of Real Estate operates under Title 61, Chapter 2f of the Utah Code, which governs real estate licensing and practice standards. Rule R162-2f-401a, administered by the Division, establishes the duty of licensees to disclose material risks to clients. Wire fraud is now treated as a foreseeable risk that agents must affirmatively address in every transaction involving electronic fund transfers.

Failing to warn clients is no longer a gray area. The Division has signaled through its 2025 enforcement bulletins that wire fraud advisory omissions will be treated as violations of the licensee's fiduciary duty under Utah Code 61-2f-401.

## The Specific Forms and Documents You Must Use

**Utah agents are required to use Form 60 (Wire Fraud Advisory and Electronic Communication Warning), published by the Utah Association of Realtors.** This form must be presented to clients early in the transaction, ideally at the time of contract execution. It cannot be buried in a stack of closing documents and signed retroactively at the settlement table.

Form 60 covers three distinct warnings: the risk of email compromise, the procedure for independently verifying wire instructions, and the limitation of liability when parties fail to follow verification protocols. Agents should also be familiar with Form 57 (Buyer Due Diligence Advisory), which contains a brief reference to wire fraud risk but does not replace the standalone Form 60.

The [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) goes beyond simply handing a form to your client. You must document that you explained the risks verbally and that the client acknowledged understanding. A signature alone, without evidence of actual advisement, has proven insufficient in Division disciplinary proceedings.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance fall into three categories: regulatory penalties, civil liability, and transaction disruption. The Division of Real Estate can impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation under Utah Code 61-2f-503. Repeated violations trigger license suspension or revocation proceedings.

Civil liability presents the larger financial exposure. When a buyer loses $300,000 to a business email compromise scheme and the agent never provided a wire fraud advisory, that agent becomes a target in the resulting lawsuit. Utah courts have recognized that the failure to warn creates a breach of the duty of care owed to clients, particularly when industry standards clearly require such warnings.

| Consequence | First Offense | Repeat Offense |
|---|---|---|
| Division Fine | Up to $2,500 | Up to $5,000 |
| License Action | Written reprimand | Suspension (30-180 days) |
| Civil Liability | Potential full loss recovery | Full loss + punitive damages |
| E&O Insurance | Claim filed, premium increase | Possible coverage denial |
| Transaction Impact | Delayed closing | Deal cancellation |

Deal cancellation is a real outcome. Title companies in Utah are increasingly refusing to close transactions where the wire fraud advisory form is missing from the file. They view the absence as a red flag that proper procedures were not followed, which increases their own insurer's exposure.

## Common Mistakes Utah Agents Make

The first mistake is timing. Agents frequently present Form 60 at the closing table alongside dozens of other documents. By that point, the client has already received and acted on wire instructions. The advisory must come early enough to actually influence client behavior, which means at contract signing or during the due diligence period.

**The second mistake is using outdated forms.** The Utah Association of Realtors updates Form 60 periodically, and the 2025 revision added language specifically addressing AI-generated phishing emails and deepfake voice calls. Agents using forms from 2023 or earlier are technically providing incomplete disclosures, which the Division has flagged as insufficient compliance.

The third mistake is delegation without verification. Agents assume their transaction coordinator or title company handled the wire fraud advisory. The Division holds the licensee responsible regardless of who was supposed to complete the task. Your name is on the file, and [understanding why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) like advisory delivery is the first step toward fixing the problem.

The fourth mistake involves verbal-only warnings. Some agents tell clients over the phone to be careful with wire instructions but never document the conversation. Without a signed Form 60 in the transaction file, there is no defensible record that the warning was given. Utah's regulatory framework requires written documentation.

The fifth mistake is failing to address both sides of the transaction. Listing agents sometimes assume wire fraud only affects buyers wiring earnest money or closing funds. Sellers receiving proceeds via wire are equally vulnerable to instruction manipulation. Both parties need advisories, and both agents bear responsibility for their respective clients.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Utah carry supervisory liability under [Utah Code 61-2f-401(4)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title61/Chapter2F/61-2f-S401.html), which requires them to establish policies that ensure licensee compliance with Division rules. A broker who fails to implement wire fraud prevention protocols faces the same penalties as the agent who skips the form.

**Every brokerage operating in Utah should conduct quarterly file audits specifically checking for signed Form 60 documents.** The audit should verify both the presence of the form and its timing relative to the contract execution date. A Form 60 signed on the same day as settlement documents is a compliance failure, not a success.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Form 60 present in file | Every transaction | Signed copy with date |
| Form 60 timing verification | Every transaction | Date comparison to contract |
| Agent training completion | Annually | CE certificate or internal training log |
| Updated form version check | Quarterly | Version date on form matches current year |
| Client communication log | Every transaction | Notes showing verbal advisement |

Brokers should also maintain written office policies that specify when Form 60 must be delivered, who is responsible for delivery in team structures, and what happens internally when an agent fails to comply. The Division's disciplinary board looks favorably on brokerages that demonstrate proactive enforcement, even when individual agents fall short.

Training documentation matters. Brokers should require all agents to complete wire fraud prevention training annually and maintain records of completion. The Utah Division of Real Estate's continuing education catalog includes approved wire fraud courses, and several count toward the 18-hour CE requirement under Rule R162-2f-201.

## How Technology Fits Into Compliance

Transaction management platforms can automate the delivery and tracking of Form 60, but automation does not eliminate agent responsibility. The agent must still confirm the client received, read, and understood the advisory. Automated delivery with a digital signature satisfies the documentation requirement only when paired with evidence of client engagement.

Many brokerages now use [checklist-based systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to ensure wire fraud advisories are delivered at the correct point in the transaction timeline. These systems flag missing forms before the file reaches the title company, preventing last-minute scrambles that result in backdated signatures or missing documentation.

The rise of AI-powered phishing has made static form delivery insufficient on its own. Agents should be prepared to have a live conversation with clients about the specific tactics criminals use, including spoofed email addresses, compromised title company accounts, and last-minute changes to wiring instructions. Form 60 is the minimum standard, not the ceiling of your duty.
