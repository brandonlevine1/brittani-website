## Understanding Montana Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Montana earnest money & escrow laws 2026 remain one of the most frequent sources of disciplinary actions against licensed agents in the state. Every year, the Montana Board of Realty Regulation opens investigations tied to trust account violations, late deposits, and unauthorized disbursements. Knowing the statutes and rules that govern these funds is not optional—it is the baseline of professional practice.

The governing framework sits in Title 37, Chapter 51 of the Montana Code Annotated (MCA) and the accompanying Montana Administrative Rules (ARM), specifically ARM 24.210.601 through 24.210.660. These provisions define how brokers must handle money belonging to others, the timelines for deposit, the conditions for release, and the penalties for noncompliance. The [Montana Board of Realty Regulation](https://boards.bsd.dli.mt.gov/realty-regulation) enforces these rules through complaint investigations, audits, and formal disciplinary proceedings.

Agents who treat earnest money handling as a clerical afterthought expose themselves—and their brokers—to license suspension, civil suits, and deal collapse. This post breaks down exactly what Montana law requires, the forms that document compliance, and the mistakes that land agents in front of the Board.

## Statutory Framework: MCA 37-51-321 and ARM 24.210.641

**MCA 37-51-321 establishes that all money received by a licensee on behalf of others must be placed in a trust account maintained by the broker.** The statute does not distinguish between earnest money deposits, rental security deposits, or other fiduciary funds. All of it goes into a single regulated bucket: the broker's trust account.

ARM 24.210.641 adds the teeth. It mandates that funds be deposited within two banking days of receipt unless the purchase agreement specifies an alternative timeline. "Banking days" excludes weekends and state-recognized holidays, but the clock starts the moment the licensee or anyone in the brokerage receives the check, wire, or electronic transfer.

The rule also prohibits commingling. A broker may not place personal funds in the trust account beyond a nominal amount—generally $500—needed to keep the account open and cover bank fees. Commingling, even temporarily, constitutes a per-se violation that the Board treats as grounds for disciplinary action regardless of intent.

## Montana Association of Realtors Forms: Numbers That Matter

Montana agents working within the MAR (Montana Association of Realtors) system rely on specific form numbers that document earnest money terms. The MAR Residential Real Estate Purchase Agreement—Form No. 20-6—contains the section where agents specify the deposit amount, the holder of funds, and the deposit deadline. Agents must fill in whether the broker's trust account or a designated title/escrow company will hold the funds.

Form No. 20-6 also includes language addressing what happens to earnest money upon default or termination. The inspection contingency removal, financing contingency removal, and other addenda reference the earnest money's fate at each decision point. Getting the box-checking wrong on this form is the origin of most disputes that escalate to the Board.

When a transaction terminates, Form No. 26-1 (Release of Earnest Money) requires signatures from both buyer and seller authorizing disbursement. Without both signatures or a court order, the broker must hold the funds. Agents who pressure one party to sign or release funds based on a single party's instruction are violating ARM 24.210.641(5).

| Form Number | Purpose | Key Earnest Money Provision |
|---|---|---|
| MAR 20-6 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Specifies deposit amount, holder, and deadline |
| MAR 26-1 | Release of Earnest Money | Requires mutual written authorization for disbursement |
| MAR 20-7 | Counter Proposal | May modify original earnest money terms |
| MAR 24-1 | Inspection Response | Can trigger contingency-based release |
| MAR 28-1 | Notice to Buyer to Perform | Starts cure period that may forfeit deposit |

Agents who skip the Release of Earnest Money form and instead handle disbursement informally—via email confirmation or phone call—create a documentation gap that the Board will not overlook during an audit.

## Deposit Timelines and Trust Account Requirements

**The two-banking-day deposit rule is the single most violated provision in Montana real estate practice.** Agents receive a check on a Friday afternoon, forget it in a desk drawer over the weekend, and fail to deposit it until Wednesday. That is a violation. The administrative rules do not provide a grace period, and "I didn't get to the bank" is not a recognized defense before the Board.

Montana law requires the broker—not the individual agent—to maintain the trust account at a Montana financial institution. The account must be clearly labeled as a trust account, and the broker's name and license number should be associated with it. Individual salespersons may not maintain their own trust accounts. All funds flow through the broker's designated account.

Wire transfers and electronic payments add a layer of complexity. When a buyer wires earnest money directly to a title company, the listing broker's two-day obligation may not apply—because the broker never "received" the funds. However, the purchase agreement must clearly designate the title company as the holder. If it designates the broker's trust account but the funds went elsewhere, the documentation mismatch creates liability. ## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make

**Mistake one: depositing earnest money before all parties have signed the purchase agreement.** Some agents rush to deposit a buyer's check the moment it arrives, even though the contract is not yet fully executed. In Montana, a contract is not formed until mutual acceptance. Depositing before that point means the broker holds uncontracted funds with no legal framework for disbursement. If the seller rejects the offer, returning the money should be straightforward—but it creates an accounting headache and potential trust account reconciliation errors.

Mistake two: failing to specify the deposit holder in the purchase agreement. Form 20-6 has a blank for this information. When agents leave it empty or assume "the title company will handle it," they create ambiguity. If a dispute arises, neither the broker nor the title company may feel authorized to act, and the funds sit in limbo. The Board considers vague documentation a supervision failure on the broker's part.

Mistake three: releasing earnest money based on one party's verbal instruction. ARM 24.210.641(5) is explicit—both parties must provide written authorization. An agent who releases funds because the buyer "said to cancel" without a signed Form 26-1 is personally liable and exposes the broker to disciplinary proceedings.

Mistake four: failing to document the date and time of receipt. The two-day clock starts at receipt, not when the agent gets around to logging it. Agents who do not note the exact date they received the deposit have no way to prove compliance if the Board asks. A simple date-stamped entry in the transaction file solves this.

Mistake five: holding a post-dated check without disclosure. If a buyer provides a post-dated check and asks the agent to hold it, the agent must disclose this to the seller and note it in the agreement. Holding a post-dated check without disclosure misleads the seller about the buyer's financial readiness.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry vicarious liability for their agents' trust account violations under MCA 37-51-321. The Board does not accept "I didn't know my agent did that" as a defense. Montana regulations require designated brokers to perform monthly trust account reconciliations and maintain records for at least three years after the transaction closes.

| Broker Responsibility | Statutory Basis | Audit Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly trust account reconciliation | ARM 24.210.641 | Monthly, no exceptions |
| Verify deposit within two banking days | ARM 24.210.641(1) | Per-transaction |
| Confirm Form 26-1 before any disbursement | ARM 24.210.641(5) | Per-disbursement event |
| Maintain records for three years post-close | ARM 24.210.660 | Ongoing |
| Ensure trust account labeling and bank designation | MCA 37-51-321 | Annual review minimum |

Brokers should audit their agents' transaction files quarterly at minimum—not just at year-end. The Board's auditors look for patterns: repeated late deposits, missing release forms, and reconciliation discrepancies. A single isolated error might result in a warning, but a pattern triggers formal charges. Understanding [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is directly relevant here; brokers who implement systematic file reviews catch problems before the Board does.

Supervision extends to independent contractors. Montana law does not exempt a broker from oversight responsibility just because agents are 1099 contractors rather than employees. The brokerage agreement should include explicit language requiring agents to deliver all trust funds to the office within 24 hours of receipt—one day tighter than the regulatory deadline—to give the broker a buffer for deposit.

## Penalties and Liability for Noncompliance

The Board of Realty Regulation has broad authority under MCA 37-51-321 and MCA 37-1-136 to impose sanctions. Possible outcomes include license suspension, license revocation, civil fines up to $1,000 per violation, mandatory education, and required restitution to harmed parties. The Board may also refer criminal conduct—such as conversion of trust funds—to the county attorney's office for prosecution under Montana's theft statutes (MCA 45-6-301).

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue the agent and broker for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and negligence. Montana courts have awarded actual damages plus attorney fees in such cases. The broker's errors and omissions insurance may cover some exposure, but many E&O policies exclude trust account violations from coverage.

Deal cancellation is another consequence. If a buyer learns that their earnest money was not deposited on time—or was commingled with brokerage operating funds—they may have grounds to void the contract under the implied covenant of good faith. Sellers, meanwhile, may lose confidence in the transaction's integrity and exercise termination rights. The financial harm extends well beyond the deposit amount when a deal collapses because of agent negligence.
