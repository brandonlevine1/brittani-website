## Understanding Maine Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

If you hold a Maine real estate license, the rules governing earnest money and escrow accounts are not optional knowledge—they are enforceable obligations backed by statute and regulatory authority. Maine earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry specific deposit timelines, accounting requirements, and dispute resolution protocols that can cost you your license if ignored. This article breaks down the statutes, forms, common pitfalls, and broker-level compliance obligations that matter right now.

The Maine Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under the Department of Professional and Financial Regulation, is the primary regulatory body overseeing escrow and trust account conduct. Its authority comes from Title 32, Chapter 114 of the Maine Revised Statutes, specifically sections 13271 through 13281. Agents and brokers who fail to comply face disciplinary action ranging from fines to license revocation.

## The Statutory Framework: Title 32 and Commission Rules

**Title 32 M.R.S. Section 13273 establishes the foundational requirement that all brokers maintain escrow accounts for funds received on behalf of others.** The statute mandates that these accounts be held in a federally insured banking institution located in Maine. Personal or operating funds may not be deposited into escrow accounts except for the minimum balance required to keep the account open, which must not exceed $500.

Section 13273 further specifies that brokers must maintain accurate records of all escrow transactions for a minimum of three years. These records must be available for inspection by the Commission at any time. The Commission's Chapter 420, Rule 4.11 elaborates on the timing requirement: funds must be deposited by the next banking day following execution of the purchase agreement by all parties.

The distinction between "receipt" and "deposit" matters in Maine. An agent who physically receives a check on Friday afternoon and deposits it the following Monday is technically compliant, provided Monday is the next banking day. But holding that check in a desk drawer through Tuesday without depositing it creates a violation.

## Forms and Documentation Agents Must Use

Maine's standard residential purchase and sale agreement—MAR Form 101, published by the Maine Association of Realtors—contains specific earnest money provisions in Section 3. This section identifies the amount, the holder of the deposit, and the conditions under which it may be released or forfeited. Agents must fill this section out completely; leaving blanks creates ambiguity that invites disputes.

| Form/Document | Purpose | Required By |
|---|---|---|
| MAR Form 101 (Section 3) | Identifies earnest money amount and holder | All residential transactions using MAR forms |
| Escrow Ledger (broker-maintained) | Tracks deposits, disbursements per transaction | MREC Chapter 420, Rule 4.11 |
| Receipt of Deposit (broker copy) | Written acknowledgment of funds received | Best practice per MREC guidance |
| Interpleader Filing | Court petition when funds are disputed | Triggered by unresolved party dispute |
| Trust Account Reconciliation | Monthly reconciliation of escrow account | MREC Chapter 420, Rule 4.13 |

Beyond MAR Form 101, brokers must maintain a written ledger or electronic accounting system that ties each deposit to a specific transaction. The Commission does not prescribe a single software or format, but it does require that records clearly show the date received, date deposited, source of funds, and disbursement date with payee information.

When agents work with [transaction coordinators who manage deadlines and documentation](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the escrow deposit timeline is one of the first items flagged—because missing it is one of the fastest ways to generate a complaint.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

**Violating Maine's escrow laws is not a slap on the wrist—it carries real consequences.** Under Title 32 M.R.S. Section 13281, the Commission may impose civil penalties up to $1,500 per violation against licensees. Repeated or egregious violations—particularly commingling funds or misappropriating escrow money—can result in license suspension or permanent revocation.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents and brokers face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is lost due to broker negligence can sue for the full amount plus damages. Maine courts have also awarded attorney's fees in cases where brokers acted in bad faith regarding escrow disbursement.

The Commission publishes disciplinary actions on the [Maine.gov Professional and Financial Regulation website](https://www.maine.gov/pfr/professionallicensing/professions/real-estate/), and recent cases show that escrow violations remain one of the top reasons agents face discipline. In 2024 and 2025, multiple licensees received consent agreements involving escrow account mismanagement—ranging from late deposits to failure to maintain proper records.

Deal cancellation is another consequence. If an agent's escrow violation causes a transaction to fall apart—say, the seller discovers the deposit was never actually deposited—the non-breaching party may terminate the agreement and pursue damages against the licensee personally. This is distinct from the buyer-seller dispute; it puts the agent's own finances and E&O coverage at risk.

## Common Mistakes Maine Agents Make With Earnest Money

After reviewing Commission disciplinary records and speaking with practicing brokers across the state, five recurring errors stand out.

**First, agents accept earnest money checks made payable to themselves rather than the brokerage.** Maine law requires that escrow funds be deposited into the broker's trust account. A check made out to "Jane Smith, Realtor" cannot be deposited into the brokerage escrow account without endorsement issues. Instruct buyers to make checks payable to the brokerage name as it appears on the escrow account.

Second, agents fail to note post-dated checks or hold-until instructions. If a buyer hands over a check and says "don't deposit this until next Friday," the agent faces a conflict: the Commission requires deposit by the next banking day, but depositing a post-dated check early can bounce. The correct answer is to not accept post-dated checks. If the buyer needs more time, restructure the contract terms rather than create an off-the-books side arrangement.

Third, agents neglect to confirm that the deposit actually cleared. Depositing a check satisfies the timing requirement, but a bounced check creates a material breach of the purchase agreement. Agents should follow up with their brokerage within three to five business days to confirm clearance and document it.

Fourth, agents handle disbursement disputes informally. When a deal collapses and both parties claim the earnest money, some agents try to mediate a split or simply release funds to whichever party pressures them harder. This violates Maine law. The broker must hold disputed funds in escrow until both parties sign a release or a court orders disbursement.

Fifth, some agents operating on [teams with insufficient broker supervision](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) assume someone else is handling the deposit. Delegation without verification is not a defense before the Commission.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Maine carry personal liability for escrow account compliance across their entire brokerage. The Commission holds the broker responsible even if an individual agent caused the violation. That reality demands proactive auditing.

| Broker Obligation | Frequency | Source Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Reconcile escrow account with ledger | Monthly | MREC Chapter 420, Rule 4.13 |
| Verify deposit timing for each transaction | Per transaction | Title 32 M.R.S. § 13273 |
| Confirm no commingling of personal funds | Monthly | Title 32 M.R.S. § 13273 |
| Maintain records for Commission inspection | Ongoing (3-year minimum) | MREC Chapter 420, Rule 4.11 |
| Supervise agent handling of deposits | Ongoing | Title 32 M.R.S. § 13271 |

Brokers should implement a written escrow policy that every agent signs upon affiliation. This policy should specify who may physically accept earnest money, how quickly it must be delivered to the office, and what documentation the agent must provide. A verbal "just get it deposited" instruction is not sufficient.

Monthly reconciliation is not optional under Rule 4.13. The broker or a designated bookkeeper must reconcile the escrow account balance against the sum of all individual transaction ledger balances. Discrepancies must be identified and resolved immediately—not at the end of the quarter.

Brokers should also audit for zero-balance transactions that remain on the ledger. If a deal closed six months ago and the ledger still shows a balance, something went wrong with disbursement. These ghost entries are red flags during a Commission audit. ## Dispute Resolution: Interpleader and Release Agreements

When buyer and seller disagree on who deserves the earnest money after a failed transaction, Maine does not permit the broker to play judge. The broker's obligation is straightforward: hold the funds and seek resolution.

The first option is a mutual release. Both parties sign a written agreement specifying how the funds will be distributed. MAR provides a release form that agents should keep on hand. Once signed by both parties, the broker disburses accordingly and closes out the ledger entry.

If the parties refuse to agree, the broker may file an interpleader action in Maine District Court or Superior Court, depending on the amount. Interpleader deposits the funds with the court and asks the judge to determine rightful ownership. The broker is released from liability once the court accepts the deposit. Filing fees and potential attorney costs make this the less attractive option, but it protects the broker from claims by either party.

**Brokers who unilaterally release disputed funds—even with good intentions—expose themselves to personal liability and Commission discipline.** There is no "30-day rule" in Maine that automatically releases funds to one party if the other does not respond. That myth persists among agents, but it has no basis in Maine statute.
