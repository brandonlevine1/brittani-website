## Understanding Kentucky Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on transfer taxes or recording fees does not just embarrass you at the closing table—it can delay title transfer, trigger contract breach claims, and expose your brokerage to regulatory scrutiny. The Kentucky transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by the same statutory framework that has been in place for decades, but fee increases at the county clerk level and revised forms catch agents off guard every cycle. This guide breaks down the exact dollar amounts, form numbers, statutory citations, and compliance traps you need to know as a licensed agent or broker operating in the Commonwealth.

Kentucky imposes its real estate transfer tax under KRS Chapter 142, specifically KRS 142.050. The tax applies to every deed, instrument, or writing that transfers real property interest for consideration, and it is collected at the county clerk's office at the time of recording. Unlike states with tiered or locality-specific transfer taxes, Kentucky keeps a single statewide rate—making calculation straightforward but leaving zero room for excuses when errors occur.

## The Current Rate Structure and How It Is Calculated

**The Kentucky deed transfer tax rate is $0.50 for each $500 of value, or fractional part thereof.** That translates to an effective rate of $1.00 per $1,000 of gross consideration. On a $350,000 sale, the transfer tax is $350. On a $512,750 sale, you round up to the next $500 increment ($513,000) and calculate $1,026.

Recording fees are separate from the transfer tax and are set by each county clerk under authority granted by KRS 64.012. As of 2026, the standard recording fee for a deed in most Kentucky counties is $33 for the first five pages and $3 for each additional page. Mortgage recordings follow the same page-based structure. Some counties also charge a $6 legal process tax per instrument under KRS 142.010.

| Fee Type | Rate / Amount | Authority | Paid By (Custom) |
|----------|--------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Deed transfer tax | $0.50 per $500 of consideration | KRS 142.050 | Seller |
| Deed recording fee | $33 (first 5 pages) + $3/page | KRS 64.012 | Buyer |
| Mortgage recording fee | $33 (first 5 pages) + $3/page | KRS 64.012 | Buyer |
| Legal process tax | $6 per instrument | KRS 142.010 | Varies |
| Lien release recording | $33 (first 5 pages) | KRS 64.012 | Seller |

These figures should appear on every preliminary settlement statement your transaction coordinator or closing attorney prepares. If you are estimating net sheets for listing presentations, baking in the correct transfer tax from day one prevents awkward renegotiations later. ## Required Forms and Filing Procedures

Every deed submitted for recording in Kentucky must be accompanied by Revenue Form 73A802, titled "Real Estate Transfer Tax Return." This form is administered by the Kentucky Department of Revenue and must include the full consideration amount, the property address, the names of grantor and grantee, and the transfer tax amount due. The county clerk will not accept a deed without it—or without the correct remittance attached.

When a transaction qualifies for an exemption under KRS 142.050(6)—such as transfers between spouses incident to divorce, transfers to or from governmental bodies, or transfers where no consideration is exchanged—the agent or closing attorney must still submit Form 73A802. The difference is that the exemption code is marked on the form and zero tax is remitted. Skipping the form entirely because the tax is zero is a recording rejection waiting to happen.

The county clerk stamps the deed with the recording information (book, page, date, and time) and forwards the transfer tax revenue to the Kentucky Department of Revenue on a monthly basis. Agents do not interact directly with the Department of Revenue, but brokers should understand this chain of custody when auditing closed files for compliance documentation.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Kentucky Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

**Mistake one: calculating the transfer tax on the net proceeds rather than the gross sale price.** The tax is based on the full consideration stated in the deed, not the amount after seller concessions, commission, or mortgage payoffs. A $400,000 sale with $10,000 in seller-paid closing costs still generates a transfer tax of $400, not $390.

Mistake two: assuming the buyer always pays all recording fees. While Kentucky custom places recording of the new deed and mortgage on the buyer's side of the settlement statement, lien release recordings and satisfaction filings are the seller's responsibility. Agents who lump all recording fees onto one party create HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure discrepancies that delay funding.

Mistake three: omitting the legal process tax from net sheet estimates. That $6 per instrument adds up when a transaction involves a deed, a mortgage, a subordination agreement, and a power of attorney—each one triggering its own legal process tax. Sellers who expected $350 in transfer taxes suddenly see $374 and question the agent's competence.

Mistake four: failing to verify page counts before closing. A deed with legal descriptions running beyond five pages incurs additional per-page fees. Agents who pull preliminary title and notice lengthy legal descriptions should alert the closing agent early so the correct amount is collected at settlement.

Mistake five: not confirming whether a county has adopted optional fees. Under KRS 67A.070, certain consolidated local governments (Louisville Metro, for example) have the authority to impose additional fees on recordings. Agents working across county lines must verify local schedules rather than assuming uniformity.

## What Happens When Compliance Fails

If the transfer tax is short-paid, the county clerk rejects the deed. A rejected deed means the buyer does not receive recorded title, which can trigger a breach of the purchase contract's closing deadline provisions. The Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) does not directly penalize transfer tax errors, but KREC regulation 201 KAR 11:121 requires licensees to act with competence and provide accurate information to clients.

A pattern of errors could support a KREC complaint under KRS 324.160, which lists grounds for disciplinary action including "incompetency" and "conduct that is dishonest or unethical." Disciplinary outcomes range from mandatory continuing education to license suspension. Beyond KREC, an agent who consistently miscalculates closing costs may face civil liability claims from clients who relied on inaccurate net sheets when making purchase or pricing decisions.

> "Any licensee who fails to disclose material facts or who provides inaccurate financial representations to a client may be subject to disciplinary action under KRS 324.160(4)."

Recording delays also create title insurance complications. A title company that has issued a commitment expects the deed to be recorded promptly. Extended gaps between closing and recording can void coverage windows, leaving the buyer uninsured during the interim. Agents who understand the full chain of consequences treat fee accuracy as non-negotiable rather than administrative trivia. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Brokers carry supervisory liability under KRS 324.160 and 201 KAR 11:121 for the acts of their affiliated licensees.** A brokerage that never audits closed transaction files for correct transfer tax documentation is building a compliance time bomb. Here is what a quarterly audit should examine.

First, verify that every closed file contains a copy of the completed Form 73A802. If your agents are not retaining this form, you have no proof that the correct amount was remitted—and no defense if a client later claims they were overcharged or the tax was never paid. Second, compare the consideration stated on the deed against the purchase price in the contract. Discrepancies between these two numbers suggest either a drafting error or a misunderstanding of what constitutes "consideration" under KRS 142.050.

Third, confirm that the settlement statement allocates recording fees and transfer taxes to the correct parties as specified in the purchase agreement. The [Kentucky Association of Realtors](https://www.kar.com) standard form contracts include specific line items for this allocation, and any deviation should be documented with a written amendment signed by both parties. Fourth, check that agents who claim an exemption under KRS 142.050(6) have noted the correct statutory subsection on Form 73A802 rather than simply writing "exempt."

| Audit Item | Where to Find It | Red Flag |
|------------|-----------------|----------|
| Form 73A802 | Closing package | Missing or incomplete |
| Consideration match | Deed vs. contract | Dollar amounts differ |
| Fee allocation | Settlement statement | Doesn't match contract terms |
| Exemption documentation | Form 73A802 + deed | No statutory citation listed |
| Page-count fees | Recording receipt | Fewer pages charged than deed contains |

Brokers who identify recurring errors should mandate additional training. KREC's continuing education catalog includes courses on closing procedures, and brokerage-specific training sessions on transfer tax compliance take less than an hour to deliver.

## Exemptions That Agents Frequently Misapply

KRS 142.050(6) lists specific exemptions from the deed transfer tax, but agents routinely misread the scope. Transfers between spouses are exempt only when incident to a divorce decree—not when spouses simply retitle property during the marriage for estate planning purposes without a court order. Transfers to revocable living trusts where the grantor retains beneficial interest are exempt, but transfers to irrevocable trusts with different beneficiaries may not qualify.

Foreclosure deeds and deeds in lieu of foreclosure are exempt from transfer tax under KRS 142.050(6)(j), but short sale transactions where a third party purchases the property are not exempt—even though a lender approves the sale at a loss. Agents representing buyers in short sales should never assume the transfer tax will be waived simply because the transaction involves a distressed seller.

Government entity transfers are exempt, but agent-assisted sales of government-owned surplus property to private buyers are taxable. The exemption attaches to the governmental body's role as grantor or grantee, not to the nature of the property. Misapplying exemptions does not merely delay recording; it can constitute tax evasion, exposing the closing agent and potentially the listing agent to liability under KRS 142.990, which authorizes penalties for failure to pay required transfer taxes.

When you prepare a seller net sheet, run the transfer tax calculation yourself: sale price divided by 500, rounded up to the nearest whole number, then multiplied by $0.50. Cross-check recording fee estimates against the specific county clerk's published schedule—not a generic statewide assumption.
