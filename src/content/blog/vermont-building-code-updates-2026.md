## Why Vermont Building Code Updates 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The phrase Vermont building code updates 2026 should be on every agent's radar this fall, not because code changes are glamorous, but because they directly affect how you list properties, advise clients, and manage your own liability exposure. Vermont's adoption of amended versions of the 2021 International Codes, administered through the Department of Public Safety's Division of Fire Safety, has introduced requirements that touch everything from energy efficiency mandates to egress window specifications in older housing stock. Agents who treat building codes as "the inspector's problem" are courting complaints, deal collapses, and potential disciplinary action.

Vermont's housing inventory skews old. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, more than 40% of the state's housing units were built before 1970. That means a significant share of the properties you list or show will have features that predate modern code standards, and some will have been modified without permits. The 2026 updates sharpen the stakes for agents caught between aging inventory and tightening standards.

## What Changed: The 2026 Code Amendments in Detail

**Vermont does not write its own building code from scratch.** Instead, the state adopts the International Code Council's model codes and layers on Vermont-specific amendments through the rulemaking authority of the Division of Fire Safety under 20 V.S.A. Chapter 173. The current cycle brought the state into alignment with the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) and the 2021 International Building Code (IBC), with amendments that took effect in stages through late 2025 and into 2026.

The changes most relevant to residential real estate transactions fall into four categories: energy performance, fire safety, accessibility, and structural resilience. Vermont's Residential Building Energy Standards (RBES), which are administered separately by the Department of Public Service under 30 V.S.A. § 51, were also updated to align with the 2021 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC). New construction and major renovations now face stricter insulation, air sealing, and mechanical ventilation requirements.

Here is how the key residential changes stack up against the prior code cycle:

| Requirement Area | Prior Standard (2015 IRC Basis) | 2026 Standard (2021 IRC Basis) |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling insulation (new construction) | R-49 | R-60 |
| Wall insulation (new construction) | R-20 or R-13 + R-5 ci | R-20 + R-5 ci or R-13 + R-10 ci |
| Carbon monoxide detection | Required near sleeping areas | Required on every level, interconnected |
| Emergency escape openings | 5.7 sq ft minimum | 5.0 sq ft minimum (aligns with 2021 IRC) |
| EV-ready parking (new multifamily) | Not required | At least one EV-ready space per unit |

That last line — EV-ready parking — has caught many agents off guard when working with new condominium projects. If you are listing units in a post-2025 multifamily build, buyers will expect it, and lenders may verify it as part of new construction compliance documentation.

## How Code Updates Affect the Transaction Itself

Agents do not enforce building codes. That distinction belongs to local building officials and the Division of Fire Safety. But agents absolutely bear responsibility for disclosure obligations that intersect with code compliance. Under 26 V.S.A. § 2296, the Vermont Real Estate Commission can impose sanctions on licensees who fail to disclose material facts, and a known or reasonably discoverable code violation qualifies.

**Vermont's Property Disclosure Report — the form sellers complete and agents facilitate — is where most code-related liability originates.** The form asks sellers directly about known defects, alterations made without permits, and compliance with building or zoning requirements. If a seller indicates that a basement bedroom was added without permits, and the listing agent markets that room as a conforming bedroom anyway, the agent has created independent liability beyond the seller's nondisclosure.

The Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) publishes standardized transaction forms that most agents use. The Purchase and Sale Contract (VAR Form 200 series) includes inspection contingency provisions that reference the buyer's right to investigate code compliance. The Property Disclosure Form (VAR Form 600 series) contains the specific questions about permits and known violations. Agents should know these forms well enough to explain each section to their clients, not just collect signatures.

Similar to how [agents in other states face liability when deadlines slip](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), Vermont agents who fail to flag code-related disclosures on time risk losing deals and facing commission complaints.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Around Building Codes

Five recurring errors show up again and again in disciplinary files and E&O claims tied to code issues.

**First, agents market unpermitted spaces as conforming living areas.** A finished attic with no egress window or a basement "bedroom" with a ceiling height of 6 feet 6 inches does not meet the IRC minimum of 7 feet. Listing these rooms in the MLS bedroom count invites misrepresentation claims. Vermont's MLS guidelines, administered by PrimeMLS, require that advertised room counts reflect habitable space standards, and code compliance is the baseline.

Second, agents assume that if a property passed a prior sale's inspection, it must still be code-compliant. Code standards change. The 2026 updates to carbon monoxide detector placement alone could make a property noncompliant that was fine five years ago. Prior inspections are snapshots, not guarantees.

Third, agents fail to recommend that buyers verify permit histories with the local zoning administrator or town clerk. Vermont's Act 250 (10 V.S.A. Chapter 151) adds another layer for properties in certain environmental or development zones. Permit research is not the agent's job to perform, but recommending it is part of competent representation.

Fourth, agents confuse the Residential Building Energy Standards certificate with a full code compliance certificate. The RBES certificate, required for new construction under 30 V.S.A. § 51, confirms energy performance only. It says nothing about structural, fire safety, or plumbing code compliance. Agents who tell buyers "the house has a certificate of compliance" based solely on the RBES document are misrepresenting the scope of that certification.

Fifth, agents neglect to update their own knowledge when codes change. Vermont requires 24 hours of continuing education per two-year licensing cycle under the rules of the [Vermont Office of Professional Regulation](https://sos.vermont.gov/real-estate/). None of those hours are specifically mandated for building code topics, which means agents must proactively seek out code-related CE rather than waiting for it to appear in a required curriculum.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under 26 V.S.A. § 2299 for the acts and omissions of their affiliated licensees. When a code-related disclosure failure leads to a complaint, the Vermont Real Estate Commission does not just look at the agent — it examines whether the broker had systems in place to prevent the error.

| Broker Audit Area | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| MLS listing accuracy | Room counts, square footage sourced from permit records | Misrepresentation claims |
| Disclosure form completion | VAR Form 600 series reviewed before listing goes active | Failure-to-disclose complaints |
| Permit verification recommendations | Written buyer advisory recommending permit research | Negligence exposure |
| CE tracking for agents | Code-related training logged within past 24 months | Supervisory liability |
| Post-inspection follow-up | Confirmed that code deficiencies flagged in inspections are addressed in negotiations | Deal collapse, litigation |

Brokers should institute a policy requiring that every listing file include confirmation that the seller completed the Property Disclosure Form and that the agent reviewed it for internal consistency. If a seller discloses a renovation but answers "no" to the permit question, the agent needs to follow up before the listing goes live, not after a buyer's inspector finds the discrepancy.

The brokerage's transaction management system should flag these checkpoints automatically. ## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

Vermont's Real Estate Commission, operating under the Office of Professional Regulation, has authority to investigate complaints and impose discipline ranging from written warnings to license revocation. Under 26 V.S.A. § 2296, fines can reach $1,000 per violation. Repeated violations or patterns of nondisclosure can result in license suspension.

Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face civil liability. Vermont courts have recognized that real estate agents owe a duty of honest dealing to all parties in a transaction, not just their own clients. If a buyer can show that the agent knew or should have known about a code deficiency and failed to disclose it, the agent may be liable for damages including repair costs, diminished value, and in some cases, rescission of the sale.

**E&O insurance does not cover intentional misrepresentation.** If a carrier determines that an agent deliberately marketed an unpermitted space as code-compliant, the claim may be denied. Agents who think "my insurance will cover it" are operating under a dangerous assumption.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate consequence. When a buyer's inspector identifies code violations that the seller failed to disclose and the agent failed to flag, the buyer will invoke the inspection contingency in the VAR purchase contract. The deal dies, the listing sits, and the agent's reputation takes a hit with both the seller and the buyer's agent.

## Preparing for the Rest of 2026 and Beyond

Vermont's Division of Fire Safety has signaled that the next code cycle will likely adopt elements of the 2024 International Codes, with rulemaking expected to begin in late 2026 or early 2027. Agents who build a habit of tracking code updates now will be better positioned when the next round of amendments arrives.

One practical step: subscribe to the Division of Fire Safety's rulemaking notices and attend at least one code-focused CE session per renewal cycle. The Vermont Association of Realtors periodically offers training that covers disclosure obligations related to building code compliance, though availability varies by year.
