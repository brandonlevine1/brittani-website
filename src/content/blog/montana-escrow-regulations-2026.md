## Understanding Montana Escrow Regulations 2026 and Why They Matter

Montana escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents who mishandle trust funds or miss statutory deadlines. The Montana Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Department of Labor and Industry, enforces rules that can end careers when violated. Every licensed agent in the state needs to understand not just what the rules say, but where liability attaches when something goes wrong.

**The governing statutes are primarily found in Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Title 37, Chapter 51, and the Montana Administrative Rules (ARM) Title 24, Chapter 210.** These aren't suggestions. They are enforceable mandates that the Board audits, investigates, and prosecutes with regularity.

Unlike some western states that allow informal earnest money handling, Montana's framework demands precise documentation, strict timelines, and broker-level oversight. If you've been treating escrow compliance as a background task, 2026 is the year to change that approach—because enforcement actions have accelerated since the Board increased its audit staff in late 2025.

## Statutory Framework: MCA 37-51-321 and ARM 24.210.641

The foundational statute for escrow handling in Montana is [MCA 37-51-321](https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0370/chapter_0510/part_0030/section_0210/0370-0510-0030-0210.html), which requires every broker to maintain a trust account at a federally insured financial institution within Montana. This statute mandates that all money received by a licensee on behalf of others—earnest money, security deposits, rental proceeds—goes directly into that trust account.

ARM 24.210.641 adds the operational detail. It specifies the two-banking-day deposit rule, record-keeping requirements, and the conditions under which funds may be disbursed. The rule also defines what constitutes commingling and conversion, two violations that the Board treats as presumptive grounds for license revocation.

A broker may maintain personal funds in a trust account only to cover bank service charges, and that amount cannot exceed $500. Anything beyond that threshold is commingling, full stop. The distinction between an honest bookkeeping delay and a violation often comes down to documentation—did the broker record why excess funds temporarily appeared in the account?

## Key Deadlines and Deposit Requirements

Timing is where most agents create exposure for themselves and their brokers. ARM 24.210.641 establishes the two-banking-day rule: once a purchase agreement is signed by all parties, the earnest money must be deposited into the designated trust account within two banking days.

| Action | Deadline | Governing Rule |
|--------|----------|----------------|
| Deposit earnest money after acceptance | 2 banking days | ARM 24.210.641 |
| Broker trust account reconciliation | Monthly | ARM 24.210.641(5) |
| Disbursement after closing | Per closing instructions | MCA 37-51-321 |
| Return of earnest money (mutual release) | Reasonable time, typically 3-5 days | Contract terms + MCA 37-51-321 |
| Notification to Board of trust account change | 10 days | ARM 24.210.625 |

The phrase "two banking days" does not mean two calendar days. Weekends and federal holidays do not count. But agents who receive an executed contract on a Friday afternoon and wait until the following Wednesday to deposit are skating close to a violation if Monday was a banking day.

**Montana does not allow agents to hold earnest money checks in a desk drawer "pending inspection."** That informal practice, common in rural markets, is a direct violation unless the purchase agreement explicitly states the check will be held uncashed until a specific contingency is satisfied. Even then, the check must be in the broker's physical possession, not the agent's.

## Forms and Documentation Montana Agents Must Use

Montana real estate transactions rely on forms published by the Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) and approved for statewide use. The relevant forms for escrow handling include the MAR Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 100), the Earnest Money Receipt, and the Mutual Release of Earnest Money (Form 133).

Form 100 contains the earnest money clause in Section 3, specifying the amount, the holder, and the conditions for release. Agents must fill this section completely—leaving the "held by" field blank or writing "TBD" creates ambiguity that the Board has cited in disciplinary actions. The form explicitly asks whether funds will be held by the listing broker, a title company, or another designated party.

Form 133—the Mutual Release—is required when both parties agree to release earnest money outside of closing. An agent who returns earnest money based on a verbal agreement or a text message, without a signed Form 133 or equivalent written release, exposes the broker to claims from the other party. The Board's position is clear: no written release, no disbursement.

Agents working with [transaction coordination systems](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) will find that proper form tracking prevents most compliance gaps. The key is making sure every escrow action—deposit, hold, release—has a corresponding document in the file.

## Common Mistakes That Trigger Board Investigations

Five errors account for the majority of escrow-related complaints filed with the Montana Board of Realty Regulation. Each one is avoidable with basic process discipline.

**First, late deposits remain the single most common violation.** An agent receives an earnest money check at signing, puts it in a folder, and doesn't deliver it to the broker or title company within two banking days. Sometimes the delay is only 24 hours past the deadline. The Board does not care about the margin. Late is late.

Second, agents disburse earnest money without proper authorization. This happens most often when a deal falls apart and the buyer's agent hands back the check without obtaining signatures on Form 133. The listing agent or seller later disputes the return, and the broker is caught in the middle with no documentation. The Board views unauthorized disbursement as a trust violation regardless of the agent's good intentions.

Third, agents deposit earnest money into their personal accounts rather than the broker's trust account. This is conversion under Montana law, even if the agent immediately writes a check to the broker. The funds must go directly into the trust account. There is no intermediate step allowed.

Fourth, some agents accept earnest money made payable to them personally rather than to the brokerage or title company. ARM 24.210.641 requires that checks be made payable to the trust account holder. An agent who accepts a check in their own name and then endorses it over to the broker creates a documentation problem that looks like commingling on audit.

Fifth, failing to note the specific trust account holder on the purchase agreement. When Section 3 of Form 100 is incomplete, the Board treats it as a supervision failure by the broker and a competency issue for the agent.

## Liability, Fines, and What Happens When You Violate the Rules

The Montana Board of Realty Regulation has authority under MCA 37-51-321 and MCA 37-51-501 to impose a range of penalties. For escrow violations, consequences follow a predictable escalation path depending on severity and intent.

| Violation | Typical Board Action | Statutory Authority |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Late deposit (first offense) | Letter of warning or $500 fine | MCA 37-51-501 |
| Commingling (no harm to parties) | Fine up to $2,500 + mandatory education | MCA 37-51-321, ARM 24.210.641 |
| Conversion of trust funds | License revocation + referral for criminal prosecution | MCA 37-51-321, MCA 45-6-301 |
| Unauthorized disbursement | Suspension (30-180 days) + fine | MCA 37-51-501 |
| Failure to maintain trust records | Fine + probation | ARM 24.210.641(5) |

Beyond Board penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money was mishandled can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, and Montana courts have awarded damages including the earnest money amount, consequential damages from a lost deal, and attorney fees. Brokers are vicariously liable for their agents' escrow errors under MCA 37-51-313, which means the broker's E&O insurance—and personal assets—are on the line.

Criminal exposure exists too. Conversion of trust funds over $1,500 qualifies as felony theft under MCA 45-6-301. The Board routinely refers conversion cases to county attorneys, and prosecution rates in Montana are higher than many agents realize.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Broker responsibility for escrow compliance is not optional in Montana—it is statutory. MCA 37-51-313 places direct supervisory liability on the designated broker for every trust account transaction conducted by affiliated agents. A broker who fails to audit is not merely negligent; they are in violation of their own licensing obligations.

**Monthly reconciliation of the trust account is required under ARM 24.210.641(5).** This means the broker must compare bank statements against the transaction ledger, identify discrepancies, and resolve them before the next month's reconciliation. The Board expects reconciliation records to be available upon request during any audit.

Brokers should implement a deposit verification process where every earnest money receipt is logged with the date received, date deposited, property address, and parties involved. The log must match the bank records. Discrepancies—even minor ones caused by bank processing delays—need written explanations in the file.

Training is another enforcement obligation. ARM 24.210.301 requires brokers to ensure their agents understand trust account procedures. A broker who never trains agents on escrow handling and later faces a Board complaint cannot claim ignorance as a defense. The Board has historically treated a lack of written office policies as evidence of inadequate supervision.

Firms that handle high transaction volumes often benefit from [deadline tracking systems](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that flag when earnest money deposits are approaching the two-day window. Automated tracking doesn't replace broker oversight, but it reduces the chance that a deposit slips through the cracks during a busy week.

## Disputed Escrow Funds: The Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller disagree about who gets the earnest money, Montana brokers face a specific legal problem. They cannot simply choose a side. MCA 37-51-321 prohibits disbursement without either mutual written consent (Form 133) or a court order.

The practical remedy is interpleader—a legal action where the broker deposits the disputed funds with the district court and asks the court to determine entitlement. Montana Rule of Civil Procedure 22 governs this process. Once the broker files interpleader and deposits the funds, they are released from liability to both parties.

Brokers should not wait indefinitely before filing interpleader. The Board considers a broker who holds disputed funds for months without seeking resolution to be in potential violation of trust account rules. A reasonable timeline is 30-60 days after the dispute arises, assuming good-faith attempts at resolution have failed.

The cost of interpleader—typically $500-$1,500 in attorney fees—can be deducted from the escrowed funds if the purchase agreement allows it, or the broker may absorb it as a cost of doing business. Either way, it is far cheaper than defending a Board complaint or civil lawsuit.

## Staying Compliant Without Losing Your Mind

Escrow compliance does not have to consume hours of administrative time each week, but it does require a system. Agents who rely on memory alone to track deposit deadlines and form completion are the ones who end up in front of the Board.

The most effective approach combines a [checklist-driven process](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) with regular broker audits. When every transaction file has a standardized escrow tracking sheet—recording the date the check was received, the date it was deposited, the account it went into, and the form confirming the deposit—compliance becomes routine rather than reactive.
