## Understanding Alabama HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Alabama handles HOA and condominium disclosures differently than most states, and that distinction trips up agents every single year. The **Alabama hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** framework splits obligations between two separate statutory regimes—one for condominiums under the Alabama Uniform Condominium Act and one for non-condo planned communities that, frankly, has far less statutory guidance. If you represent buyers or sellers in association-governed properties, you need to know exactly where your duties begin and where the statute goes silent.

The Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) expects licensees to understand these disclosure mechanics even though AREC itself does not publish a standalone HOA disclosure form. That gap between statutory mandate and practical execution is where agents face the most risk. This post breaks down the current rules, the forms you should be using, and the liability exposure that comes from getting it wrong.

## The Statutory Framework: Condominiums vs. Planned Communities

**Alabama draws a hard legal line between condominiums and other types of HOA-governed communities.** The Alabama Uniform Condominium Act, codified at [Ala. Code Title 35, Chapter 8A](https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-35/chapter-8a/), governs condominium resales and imposes specific disclosure requirements on sellers. Section 35-8A-401 through 35-8A-418 outlines what must be disclosed, who must provide it, and what a buyer can do if disclosure fails.

For non-condominium planned communities—think subdivisions with an HOA but no stacked units—Alabama has no equivalent comprehensive statute as of 2026. The Alabama Homeowners Association Act (Ala. Code 35-20-1 et seq.) provides some governance rules but does not mandate a resale disclosure packet the way the Condominium Act does. This means agents working non-condo HOA transactions must rely on contract language, association bylaws, and due diligence addenda rather than a statutory safety net.

## What the Condo Resale Certificate Must Contain

Section 35-8A-408 specifies the content of the resale certificate that a condominium unit seller must furnish to the buyer. The certificate must include the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments currently due or approved, the association's current operating budget, and any pending lawsuits involving the association. It must also disclose the amount of reserves, any capital expenditures anticipated within the current or next fiscal year, and any unsatisfied judgments against the association.

The seller—not the buyer's agent, not the listing agent—bears the statutory duty to request and deliver this certificate. However, as the listing agent, your practical responsibility is to ensure your client initiates the request early enough to meet contractual deadlines. Most management companies in Alabama take 7 to 14 business days to produce the document, so waiting until the week before closing is a recipe for contract breach.

**If the resale certificate reveals material changes from what the buyer expected, the buyer has the right to cancel.** Section 35-8A-408(d) grants the purchaser a cancellation window that runs from receipt of the certificate. The specific number of days depends on what the parties negotiated in the contract, but industry standard in Alabama is 5 business days after delivery.

## Forms Alabama Agents Should Be Using

AREC does not publish a state-mandated HOA disclosure form, but the Alabama Association of Realtors (AAR) provides standardized forms that most brokerages adopt. For 2026 transactions, agents should be working with AAR Form 30 (the standard residential purchase agreement) which contains association-related provisions in its property disclosure sections. When the property is in an HOA or condo association, agents typically attach AAR Form HOA-1, the Homeowners Association Addendum, which requires sellers to identify the association, provide contact information, state the assessment amounts, and disclose transfer fees.

For condominium resales specifically, agents should also reference AAR Form CONDO-1, which addresses the resale certificate delivery timeline and buyer's right to review. This form works in tandem with the statutory requirements of Section 35-8A-408 and establishes the contractual deadline for delivery that triggers the buyer's cancellation right. Without this addendum attached, your contract may lack a clear enforcement mechanism even though the statute independently requires the disclosure.

You should also be aware of the AAR Property Disclosure Statement (Form PDS-1), which covers general property condition but does not substitute for association-specific disclosures. Agents sometimes mistakenly believe the PDS-1 covers HOA obligations—it does not. These are separate duties running on parallel tracks, and missing one because you completed the other is a compliance failure, not a technicality.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with HOA Disclosures

**The five errors below account for the majority of HOA-related complaints filed with AREC and association-related closing delays in Alabama.**

The first mistake is assuming the buyer's lender will catch everything. Lenders do request an HOA questionnaire for loan underwriting, but the lender's form serves underwriting purposes—not buyer disclosure purposes. The lender questionnaire does not satisfy the seller's statutory obligation under the Condominium Act, and lenders often request different information than what Section 35-8A-408 requires. Agents who conflate these two documents leave their sellers exposed.

The second mistake is failing to order the resale certificate early enough. Management companies in Birmingham, Huntsville, and the Gulf Coast routinely quote 10 to 14 business day turnaround times. If your contract has a 30-day close and you wait until day 15 to request the certificate, you have already created a delivery problem that could give the buyer a cancellation argument or push the closing date.

The third mistake is neglecting to disclose known HOA litigation. Even in non-condo communities where no resale certificate is statutorily required, Alabama agency law (Ala. Code 34-27-84) imposes a duty on agents to disclose material facts they know or should know. If you are aware of pending HOA litigation, special assessments, or financial distress in the association, your silence is not protected by the absence of a statutory HOA disclosure form.

The fourth mistake is omitting transfer fee disclosures. Many Alabama associations charge transfer or capital contribution fees ranging from $200 to $1,500 at closing. If the contract does not specify who pays these fees and the agent never raised the issue, the dispute surfaces at the closing table—exactly when it causes maximum damage. Using the HOA-1 addendum with the fee section completed eliminates this ambush.

The fifth mistake involves [missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) for the buyer's review period. Once the resale certificate is delivered, the clock starts on the buyer's right to cancel. Agents who cannot prove delivery date—because they handed it off informally or never documented receipt—lose the ability to enforce the expiration of that cancellation window. Always get a signed acknowledgment of receipt with a date stamp.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under AREC Rules 790-X-3-.03 and 790-X-3-.09 for their agents' transaction conduct. When it comes to HOA and condo disclosures, broker audits should focus on five specific checkpoints in every association-governed transaction file.

First, confirm that the correct addendum is attached. Every transaction involving an HOA or condo should have the HOA-1 or CONDO-1 addendum in the file. If neither is present, the transaction has a gap that could generate post-closing liability. Second, verify that the resale certificate request was documented with a date. This is your proof of diligence if a complaint is filed.

Third, check that the resale certificate or HOA disclosure was delivered to the buyer with a signed receipt. Fourth, confirm the buyer's review period expired without cancellation—or that cancellation was properly handled. Fifth, ensure transfer fees were disclosed and allocated in writing before the closing disclosure was prepared. Brokers who build these five checkpoints into their [weekly pipeline reviews](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) catch problems before they become complaints.

| Audit Checkpoint | Document to Verify | Red Flag if Missing |
|------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| Addendum attached | HOA-1 or CONDO-1 | No contractual framework for disclosure |
| Certificate requested | Request confirmation with date | Potential delivery delay |
| Certificate delivered | Signed buyer receipt | Unenforceable review deadline |
| Review period expired | Calendar verification | Buyer may still have cancellation right |
| Transfer fees addressed | Contract or addendum line item | Closing table dispute likely |

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When You Get It Wrong

**Failure to comply with Alabama's condo disclosure statute can unwind a closed transaction.** Under Section 35-8A-408, if the resale certificate was never delivered, a buyer may have grounds to rescind even after closing—though case law on post-closing rescission is fact-specific and courts consider whether the buyer had actual knowledge of the information that should have been disclosed.

For agents personally, AREC has authority under Ala. Code 34-27-36 to impose disciplinary action including fines up to $2,500 per violation, license suspension, or revocation for violations of the Alabama Real Estate License Law. Failure to disclose material facts about an association—whether in a condo or non-condo context—falls within the scope of conduct that AREC considers grounds for discipline. Beyond regulatory risk, agents face civil liability for negligent misrepresentation or breach of fiduciary duty if a buyer suffers financial harm from undisclosed assessments, liens, or litigation.

Brokers inherit this exposure through respondeat superior and AREC's supervisory responsibility rules. If an agent in your office consistently fails to attach HOA addenda or document resale certificate delivery, the broker's file review process becomes evidence in any subsequent complaint. The defense of "I didn't know my agent skipped that step" does not insulate a broker who failed to maintain reasonable oversight systems.
