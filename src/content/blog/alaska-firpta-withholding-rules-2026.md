## Alaska FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Agent's Liability Playbook

The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act has been on the books since 1980, yet it still trips up experienced agents in every state. Understanding Alaska firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 is not optional if you represent buyers or sellers in transactions involving non-U.S. persons. A single missed form can shift tens of thousands of dollars in tax liability from the foreign seller to your buyer, and potentially drag you into an IRS enforcement action.

Alaska does not impose a separate state-level income tax withholding on real estate transactions, which means FIRPTA's federal framework is the primary compliance mechanism agents must track. That absence of a state backstop makes the federal rules even more important here. Failing to identify a foreign seller early in the deal creates a chain reaction of missed deadlines, blown closings, and personal liability that no errors-and-omissions policy is designed to cover.

## How FIRPTA Works in Alaska Real Estate Transactions

FIRPTA requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold a percentage of the gross sale price and remit it to the IRS. The standard withholding rate is 15 percent of the amount realized on the sale. For properties sold at $300,001 to $1,000,000 where the buyer intends to use the property as a personal residence, the rate drops to 10 percent.

**Alaska has no state income tax, which means there is no parallel state withholding requirement layered on top of FIRPTA.** This distinguishes Alaska from states like California, Hawaii, and Maryland, which impose their own withholding on foreign or nonresident sellers. Agents who have practiced in those states before moving to Alaska sometimes assume there is a state form to file — there is not.

The buyer is technically the "transferee" under [IRC Section 1445](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1445) and bears primary liability for the withholding. In practice, the closing agent — usually the title company — handles the mechanics. But if the title company fails to withhold and the seller walks away with the full proceeds, the IRS comes after the buyer first, not the title company.

## Key Forms and Filing Deadlines Agents Must Know

The IRS form ecosystem around FIRPTA is small but unforgiving on deadlines. Missing the 20-day window after closing to remit withholding triggers automatic penalties. Here is the form lineup every Alaska agent should have memorized or pinned to their transaction checklist.

| Form | Purpose | Who Files | Deadline |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|
| IRS Form 8288 | Reports and remits withheld tax to the IRS | Buyer (or closing agent on behalf of buyer) | 20 days after closing |
| IRS Form 8288-A | Statement of withholding sent to foreign seller | Filed with Form 8288; copy to seller | 20 days after closing |
| IRS Form 8288-B | Application for reduced withholding or exemption | Foreign seller (before closing) | Must be filed before closing; 90-day processing |
| Transferor's Certification of Non-Foreign Status | Seller certifies they are not a foreign person | Seller provides to buyer | At or before closing |

**The Transferor's Certification of Non-Foreign Status is the single most important document in your FIRPTA compliance workflow.** If the seller provides a valid certification — including their U.S. taxpayer identification number, address, and a statement under penalty of perjury that they are not a foreign person — the buyer is relieved of the obligation to withhold. Agents should confirm this certification appears in the closing file for every transaction, not just those involving obviously foreign sellers.

When a seller files Form 8288-B to request reduced withholding, the closing agent can hold the withheld funds in escrow pending the IRS determination rather than remitting the full 15 percent immediately. This is often the path foreign sellers prefer, but it requires filing well ahead of the closing date — and the IRS processing timeline of approximately 90 days means last-minute applications almost never work.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences of FIRPTA noncompliance split into two categories: financial liability and transactional damage. Both can be severe.

On the financial side, if withholding is not collected and remitted, the buyer becomes liable for the full amount that should have been withheld, plus interest and penalties. Under IRC Section 1445(e), the IRS can assess a penalty equal to the tax that was required to be withheld. For a $500,000 sale, that means the buyer could owe $75,000 to the IRS — money they may have no practical way to recover from a foreign seller who has already left the country.

For agents and brokers, the Alaska Real Estate Commission (operating under the Alaska Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development, Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing) can take disciplinary action if an agent's failure to disclose known FIRPTA obligations constitutes a material misrepresentation or breach of fiduciary duty. Alaska Statute 08.88.401 outlines the grounds for disciplinary action against licensees, including incompetence and negligence.

Deal cancellations are the more immediate risk. Buyers who learn mid-transaction that their agent failed to flag a foreign seller may walk away entirely. Title companies may refuse to close if FIRPTA compliance is unresolved. The result is a dead deal, wasted time, and potential claims from both sides.

## Five Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make With FIRPTA

**First, agents assume that because Alaska has no state income tax, there is no withholding requirement at all.** Federal FIRPTA withholding applies regardless of state tax status. The absence of a state-level form does not eliminate the federal obligation.

Second, listing agents fail to ask the right questions during the listing appointment. The question is not "Are you a U.S. citizen?" — it is broader than that. A seller can be a non-citizen but still qualify as a U.S. resident for tax purposes. Conversely, a U.S. citizen living abroad with a foreign-owned LLC holding title to Alaska property may trigger FIRPTA in unexpected ways.

Third, agents confuse the buyer's withholding obligation with the seller's tax liability. FIRPTA withholding is not a tax on the sale — it is a prepayment mechanism to ensure the IRS collects tax on gains. The actual tax owed may be more or less than the withheld amount, and the seller files a U.S. tax return to reconcile the difference. Agents who tell their foreign sellers "you owe 15 percent in taxes" are giving inaccurate and potentially harmful advice.

Fourth, closing agents and buyer's agents fail to coordinate on the 20-day filing deadline for Form 8288. When both sides assume the other is handling it, neither does. The result is a missed deadline and automatic penalties.

Fifth, agents do not account for FIRPTA when structuring offers. If a foreign seller needs to net a specific amount, the 15 percent withholding must be factored into the purchase price or the seller's net sheet from day one. Discovering at the closing table that the seller's proceeds will be $75,000 less than expected kills deals. For agents looking to tighten their [transaction checklists and avoid deadline-driven errors](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), building a FIRPTA status check into the listing intake process is the fix.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Alaska carry supervisory liability under Alaska Statute 08.88.401 and the regulations administered by the Alaska Real Estate Commission. If an agent under your brokerage mishandles FIRPTA, the commission can hold you accountable for inadequate supervision.

**Start with a mandatory FIRPTA screening question on every listing intake form your agents use.** The question should ask whether the seller is a foreign person, a foreign corporation, a foreign partnership, a foreign trust, or a foreign estate. If the answer is yes — or uncertain — the file should be flagged for additional compliance steps before it moves to the marketing phase.

Brokers should also audit closed files for the presence of either a Transferor's Certification of Non-Foreign Status or documentation of withholding and Form 8288 filing. A quarterly review of closed transactions is not excessive — it is the minimum defensible standard if the commission investigates a complaint. Consider building this review into your existing file audit process, similar to the way brokers in other states [verify wire instructions to prevent fraud](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process).

Training is the other half of enforcement. FIRPTA is not covered in depth during Alaska pre-licensing education, and many continuing education courses treat it as a footnote. Brokers who run annual compliance trainings covering FIRPTA, anti-money laundering red flags, and foreign ownership structures will catch problems before they reach the closing table. The Alaska Real Estate Commission does not currently mandate FIRPTA-specific CE hours, but that does not reduce the broker's exposure when an agent under their license mishandles a transaction.

## Reduced Withholding and Exemptions That Apply in Alaska

Not every transaction involving a foreign seller requires the full 15 percent withholding. The most commonly used exemption is the personal-use buyer exemption: if the property sells for $300,000 or less and the buyer intends to use it as a residence for at least 50 percent of the time it is in use during the first two 12-month periods after closing, the withholding rate is zero. Between $300,001 and $1,000,000, the rate is 10 percent under the same buyer-use conditions.

Foreign sellers can also apply for a withholding certificate using Form 8288-B, requesting a reduced amount based on the actual expected tax liability. This is common when the seller has a low or zero gain on the property, or when tax treaty provisions reduce the applicable rate. The catch is timing — agents should advise foreign sellers to file Form 8288-B as early as possible, ideally 90 to 120 days before the anticipated closing date.

Another path is the "nonrecognition" transaction exemption, which applies to certain like-kind exchanges under IRC Section 1031. If a foreign seller is exchanging into qualifying replacement property, the withholding may be reduced or eliminated. However, the mechanics of combining [1031 exchange rules](/blog/wisconsin-1031-exchange-rules-2026) with FIRPTA compliance are complex enough to require coordination between a qualified intermediary and a tax attorney.
