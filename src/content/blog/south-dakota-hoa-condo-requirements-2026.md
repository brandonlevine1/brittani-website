# South Dakota HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Roughly one in five residential transactions in the Sioux Falls metro involves a property governed by a homeowners association or condominium regime. If you represent buyers or sellers in those deals, the **South Dakota HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026** carry real teeth — and real consequences when agents cut corners. The statutes are not long, but they are specific, and the margin for error is thinner than many agents assume.

This post breaks down the governing law, the forms you should be using, the liability you face if you skip a step, and the broker-level audits that keep offices out of trouble.

## The Statutes That Control HOA and Condo Disclosures

South Dakota's primary authority for condominium transactions is the [South Dakota Condominium Act, codified at SDCL Chapter 43-15B](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-15B). Within that chapter, Sections 43-15B-7 through 43-15B-10 govern resale disclosure obligations. These sections require the seller of a condominium unit to furnish a resale certificate to the buyer before or at the time of closing.

For non-condominium HOA properties — think single-family subdivisions with deed-restricted associations — South Dakota does not have a standalone HOA disclosure statute comparable to states like Colorado or Virginia. Instead, agents must rely on the general property condition disclosure framework in SDCL 43-4-44 and the South Dakota Real Estate Commission's approved Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement. The practical gap between condo and HOA disclosure requirements is wider than most agents realize.

The South Dakota Real Estate Commission, housed under the Department of Labor and Regulation, oversees licensee compliance. The commission publishes approved forms, issues disciplinary actions, and maintains the administrative rules found in ARSD 20:69.

## Required Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

**The single most important document in a South Dakota condo resale is the resale certificate described in SDCL 43-15B-8.** The statute does not prescribe a state-published template for the certificate itself; instead, the association prepares the document, and it must include specific items: the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments approved but not yet billed, the association's current budget, its most recent audited financial statement, the amount of reserves, and any pending litigation involving the association.

For the broader property disclosure, agents use the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement (commonly referenced as the SDREC Disclosure Form). This form includes a section where the seller must identify whether the property is subject to a homeowners association, the amount of dues, and any known pending assessments. Agents working with the South Dakota Association of REALTORS purchase agreement forms will also encounter the addendum fields for HOA and condominium details baked into the standard residential purchase agreement.

| Document | Who Prepares It | Governing Statute | When It Must Be Delivered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resale Certificate (Condo) | Association or its management company | SDCL 43-15B-8 | Before or at closing |
| Seller's Property Condition Disclosure | Seller (agent assists) | SDCL 43-4-44 | Before acceptance of offer or as specified in contract |
| Association Governing Documents (Bylaws, CC&Rs, Rules) | Association | SDCL 43-15B-7 | Upon buyer request; recommended before closing |
| Financial Statements / Budget | Association | SDCL 43-15B-8 | Included in or attached to resale certificate |

Agents sometimes confuse the resale certificate with the governing documents. They are not the same. The resale certificate is a financial and operational snapshot; the governing documents are the association's charter and rules. Buyers are entitled to both, and the failure to deliver either can expose you to liability, as discussed next.

## Liability, Deal Cancellation, and Disciplinary Risk

**Under SDCL 43-15B-8, a buyer who has not received the resale certificate may cancel the purchase agreement.** The cancellation right exists until five days after the certificate is delivered. If you close without delivering the certificate at all, the buyer's rescission window arguably never starts running — a fact that plaintiff's attorneys know well.

From the agent's side, liability branches in three directions. First, the South Dakota Real Estate Commission can impose disciplinary sanctions — including license suspension or revocation — under SDCL 36-21A-71 for failure to disclose material facts or for negligent misrepresentation. Second, a buyer can pursue civil damages against the seller and, depending on the facts, the listing agent for losses caused by undisclosed assessments, litigation, or restrictive covenants. Third, the brokerage's errors-and-omissions insurer may deny coverage if the agent's omission was a knowing violation of statutory duty rather than an innocent mistake.

Real-world exposure looks like this: a buyer closes on a unit in a Rapid City condominium complex, discovers three months later that a $15,000 special assessment was approved before closing but never disclosed, and sues the seller and listing agent. If the resale certificate was never ordered or was incomplete, the listing agent has almost no defense. The statute placed the burden squarely on the seller — and the agent who failed to advise the seller to obtain the certificate shares in the fallout.

## South Dakota HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Five errors surface repeatedly in commission complaints and E&O claims tied to association-governed properties in South Dakota.

**First, agents assume that single-family HOA transactions carry the same statutory disclosure obligations as condo deals.** They do not. The SDCL 43-15B resale certificate requirement applies only to condominiums. For non-condo HOA properties, the agent must affirmatively build disclosure into the transaction through the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure and contract addenda. Relying on the statute alone for HOA homes leaves a gap that the buyer's agent will exploit.

Second, listing agents fail to order the resale certificate early enough. Many South Dakota associations — especially those managed by smaller local firms — take 10 to 15 business days to produce the certificate. If you wait until the week before closing, you are gambling with the buyer's cancellation rights and the closing date.

Third, agents neglect to review the resale certificate themselves before passing it to the buyer. The document may reveal a pending special assessment, a lawsuit, or a reserve fund that is critically underfunded. If the buyer discovers these facts post-closing and claims the agent should have flagged them, the commission will look at whether the agent exercised reasonable care. Simply forwarding the document without reading it is not a defense.

Fourth, buyer's agents fail to calendar the five-day cancellation window under SDCL 43-15B-8. Once the buyer receives the certificate, the clock starts. If you miss the window and your client later wants to cancel based on certificate information, the right may have lapsed. [Understanding why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) applies directly here — the five-day window is exactly the kind of short-fuse obligation that slips through without a system.

Fifth, agents omit HOA transfer fees from the estimated closing costs. Many South Dakota associations charge a transfer or move-in fee that ranges from $100 to $500. Leaving it off the buyer's or seller's cost estimate creates a last-minute surprise and erodes trust.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-69. If an agent in your office repeatedly closes condo transactions without ordering resale certificates, the commission can look past the agent and come after the broker's license. A reactive approach — waiting for a complaint — is not a compliance strategy.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate in file | Every condo transaction | Certificate present, dated, and delivered before or at closing |
| Seller's Property Condition Disclosure — HOA section | Every HOA or condo transaction | HOA box checked, dues amount listed, special assessments disclosed |
| Five-day cancellation window documented | Every condo transaction | Date buyer received certificate; expiration date noted in file |
| Transfer fee accounted for | Every HOA or condo transaction | Fee listed on settlement statement or closing disclosure |
| Governing documents offered to buyer | Every condo transaction | Evidence that bylaws, CC&Rs, and rules were made available |

**Brokers should implement a quarterly file audit that specifically flags association-governed transactions.** Pull a random sample of 10 to 15 closed files per quarter and verify that the resale certificate, disclosure form, and cancellation timeline documentation are all present. If your office uses transaction coordinators, the TC should have a checklist item for each of these documents — and the broker should verify the checklist is actually being followed.

## Practical Steps to Stay Compliant in 2026

Start every listing appointment for an association-governed property by identifying whether it is a condominium under SDCL 43-15B or a non-condo HOA. That single determination changes your disclosure checklist. For condos, order the resale certificate within three business days of executing the listing agreement. For non-condo HOA properties, request a dues statement, a copy of the current budget, and confirmation of any pending or approved special assessments directly from the association.

Build the five-day cancellation window into your transaction timeline as a hard deadline. Note the date the buyer receives the certificate, calculate the expiration, and communicate it in writing to the buyer's agent. If the buyer does not cancel within five days, document that the window has closed.

On the contract side, specify in the purchase agreement addendum which party is responsible for the cost of the resale certificate and any transfer or move-in fees. South Dakota statute is silent on cost allocation, so the contract controls. Leaving it ambiguous invites a closing-table argument that can delay funding.
