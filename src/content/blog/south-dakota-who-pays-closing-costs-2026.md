## Understanding South Dakota Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of **South Dakota who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026** trips up even experienced agents because the answer is rarely black and white. South Dakota law does not mandate a single party to shoulder all costs; instead, statutes assign liability for certain fees while leaving most others to negotiation between the parties. The purchase agreement is the document that ultimately controls, which means sloppy contract drafting creates real financial exposure for both the client and the licensee.

South Dakota's closing process is governed by a mix of codified law under South Dakota Codified Laws (SDCL) Title 43 and administrative rules enforced by the [South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC)](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/). Unlike attorney-state closings in the Northeast, South Dakota transactions typically close through title companies, making the agent's role in setting accurate cost expectations even more significant.

## The Default Cost Split: What Custom and Statute Dictate

**South Dakota has no single statute that assigns every closing cost to one party.** What exists instead is a combination of statutory requirements and long-standing county customs that most title companies follow unless the purchase agreement says otherwise. Agents who assume the "standard split" applies in every deal are gambling with their clients' money.

The transfer tax under SDCL 43-4-20 is the most commonly referenced statutory cost. It applies at a rate of $0.50 per $500 of the sale price, collected through documentary transfer stamps affixed to the deed. While sellers traditionally pay this tax, the statute technically imposes joint and several liability on both grantor and grantee, meaning either party can be held responsible if the stamps go unpurchased.

Property taxes are prorated at closing based on the calendar year, with the seller covering the period up to the closing date. South Dakota's property tax cycle runs on a calendar year with first-half taxes due April 30 and second-half taxes due October 31, governed by SDCL 10-21. Getting the proration wrong — especially on summer closings — is one of the fastest ways to generate a post-closing dispute.

Agents should note that recording fees in South Dakota are set by statute at the county register of deeds office and vary by document type and page count under SDCL 7-9-6. These are not percentage-based fees but flat charges, and they have increased in several counties over the past two years.

The distinction between owner's and lender's title insurance matters enormously to buyers. Many first-time buyers assume their lender's policy protects them personally, and agents who fail to clarify this risk a SDREC complaint for inadequate disclosure. The South Dakota Association of REALTORS (SDAR) Purchase Agreement form addresses title insurance allocation in its standard provisions, but agents must still confirm the selection matches the parties' intent.

## South Dakota Forms Agents Must Get Right

**The SDAR Purchase Agreement is the foundational document where closing cost allocation lives or dies.** Agents operating in South Dakota primarily work with the SDAR Form 101 (Residential Purchase Agreement), which contains specific blanks and checkboxes for assigning costs between buyer and seller. Leaving fields blank does not default to local custom — it creates ambiguity that title companies cannot resolve without an addendum or amendment.

SDAR Form 102 (Addendum to Purchase Agreement) is used when the parties negotiate cost allocations that deviate from what the base agreement provides. Seller concessions — particularly common when the buyer requests help covering lender fees — must be documented here with specific dollar amounts or percentage caps. A vague notation like "seller to help with closing costs" without a defined figure has caused deal delays across the state.

For transactions involving property condition concerns that might shift costs (such as repair credits applied at closing), agents use SDAR Form 107 (Inspection Response). The repair-versus-credit decision directly affects the closing statement, and agents who promise credits verbally without memorializing them on Form 107 or a separate addendum expose themselves to liability. The SDREC has made clear under SDCL 36-21A-71 that agents must reduce material terms to writing.

## Common Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make on Closing Costs

Three to five specific errors surface repeatedly in SDREC complaint files and title company frustration lists. Each one is preventable with better contract hygiene.

**First, agents routinely forget to specify which party pays for the title search versus the title insurance premium.** These are separate line items. In western South Dakota counties like Pennington and Meade, custom places the title search on the seller, but in eastern counties near Sioux Falls, some title companies bundle the search into the insurance premium. Assuming one county's custom applies statewide is a mistake that surprises clients at the closing table.

Second, agents miscalculate or misquote the transfer tax amount to sellers during listing presentations. The $0.50 per $500 rate sounds simple, but agents sometimes apply it to the net proceeds rather than the full consideration. On a $350,000 sale, the transfer tax is $350, not a negligible amount, and quoting it wrong erodes trust at a moment when the seller is already scrutinizing every dollar.

Third, failing to confirm lender-specific closing cost requirements early enough in the transaction creates last-minute scrambles. VA, FHA, and USDA loans each carry restrictions on what the buyer can pay, and some costs must shift to the seller by regulation regardless of what the purchase agreement says. An agent who does not verify the buyer's loan program within the first 48 hours of an accepted offer is setting up a renegotiation.

Fourth, agents neglect to address proration methodology in the contract. South Dakota closings can prorate taxes based on the prior year's assessment or the current year's estimated assessment, and the difference on ag-adjacent properties in counties like Brown or Brookings can be thousands of dollars. The SDAR Purchase Agreement allows agents to specify the method, but the field is often overlooked.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-69, which means an agent's closing cost error can land on the broker's desk as a formal complaint. Reviewing completed transaction files for closing cost documentation is not optional — it is a license-protection measure.

**Every file should contain a signed purchase agreement with all cost-allocation fields completed, not blank.** Brokers should flag any SDAR Form 101 that leaves the title insurance, transfer tax, or home warranty payment fields empty. If an addendum (Form 102) modifies the cost split, the broker must confirm that both parties initialed the change and that the title company received a copy before preparing the closing disclosure.

Brokers should also audit seller concession amounts against lender limits. A file showing a $15,000 seller concession on a conventional loan with 5% down will trigger a lender rejection because Fannie Mae caps concessions at 3% for that down payment tier. The agent should have caught this, but the broker is the backstop, and SDREC expects that backstop to function. Quarterly file reviews should specifically look for verbal credit promises that never made it onto paper. If a buyer's closing disclosure shows a $2,000 repair credit but the transaction file has no signed Form 107 or addendum authorizing it, the broker has a documentation gap that could become an E&O claim.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

SDREC enforces licensee conduct under SDCL 36-21A-71, which lists misrepresentation, negligence, and failure to account for funds as grounds for disciplinary action. An agent who tells a buyer "the seller always pays closing costs in South Dakota" and that turns out to be inaccurate has potentially committed a misrepresentation that triggers a formal investigation.

Penalties range from a letter of reprimand to license revocation, depending on the severity and whether client harm resulted. Fines imposed by SDREC are separate from any civil liability the agent or brokerage faces if a client sues for damages. A botched closing cost allocation that forces a buyer to bring an unexpected $5,000 to the table is exactly the scenario that generates both a commission complaint and a small claims filing.

Deal cancellation is also on the table. If the closing disclosure reveals costs that materially differ from what the purchase agreement specified, the injured party may have grounds to terminate under the contract's default provisions. Title companies in South Dakota will not close a transaction when the settlement statement conflicts with the signed agreement, so the deal simply stalls until someone agrees to cover the gap — or walks away.
