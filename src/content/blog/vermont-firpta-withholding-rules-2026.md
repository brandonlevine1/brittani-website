## Vermont FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Full Picture

Every year, foreign nationals buy and sell real estate in Vermont—ski chalets in Stowe, lakefront homes on Champlain, and farmland in the Northeast Kingdom. When those sellers are not U.S. persons, the Vermont firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 dictate a specific set of federal and state obligations that can derail a closing if agents are unprepared. Getting this wrong does not just embarrass you at the closing table; it exposes your buyer client to direct IRS liability and can trigger professional discipline from the Vermont Real Estate Commission.

**FIRPTA—the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, codified at IRC Section 1445—requires a buyer to withhold 15% of the gross sale price when acquiring property from a foreign seller.** Vermont layers its own income tax withholding on top of that federal requirement. The state mandate, found in 32 V.S.A. § 5847, generally requires withholding of 2.5% of the gain (not the gross price) attributable to a nonresident seller, though in practice the closing agent often withholds a percentage of the sale price to simplify compliance. Agents who treat FIRPTA as "the attorney's problem" are gambling with their license.

## How the Federal Withholding Works Under FIRPTA

The core FIRPTA mechanic is straightforward in theory and messy in execution. When a foreign person disposes of a U.S. real property interest, the buyer—referred to as the "transferee"—must withhold 15% of the amount realized and remit it to the IRS using Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both forms must be filed within 20 days after the date of transfer.

There are exceptions. If the property will be used as the buyer's primary residence and the sale price is $300,000 or less, the withholding rate drops to zero. If the sale price is between $300,001 and $1,000,000 and the buyer intends to use the property as a residence, the rate drops to 10%. Sellers can also apply for a withholding certificate on IRS Form 8288-B to reduce the withholding to the seller's actual expected tax liability, but this application must be filed before closing and the IRS processing time can stretch to 90 days.

| Scenario | Withholding Rate | Key Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer's residence, sale price at or below $300,000 | 0% | Buyer must sign affidavit of residence use |
| Buyer's residence, sale price $300,001–$1,000,000 | 10% | Buyer must sign affidavit of residence use |
| All other dispositions by foreign persons | 15% | Default rate under IRC § 1445 |
| Seller obtains IRS withholding certificate (Form 8288-B) | Reduced to actual liability | Must be filed and approved before closing |

Note that the residence exception applies only when the buyer is a natural person who will reside in the property. If your buyer is an LLC, a trust, or any entity, the full 15% applies regardless of intended use.

## Vermont's Own Withholding Requirement Under 32 V.S.A. § 5847

Federal FIRPTA withholding is only half the equation. Vermont imposes a separate nonresident withholding tax on real estate transactions under [32 V.S.A. § 5847](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/32/151/05847). This statute applies to any seller who is not a Vermont resident—foreign or domestic—and requires the buyer or withholding agent to withhold 2.5% of the gain from the sale. The Vermont Department of Taxes administers this requirement and provides Form RW-171 (Vermont Withholding Tax Return for Transfer of Real Property) for reporting and remittance.

**The distinction between "gain" and "gross sale price" trips up agents constantly.** At the federal level, FIRPTA withholding is based on the gross amount realized. At the Vermont level, withholding is based on the estimated gain. If the seller cannot establish basis—or if the closing agent cannot verify it—many attorneys default to withholding a higher amount to avoid underreporting. The seller can later recover any overpayment by filing a Vermont income tax return.

Vermont does offer exemptions from the state withholding. If the seller provides a Certificate of Residence (Form RW-171-C) confirming Vermont domicile, no withholding is required. A foreign seller, by definition, cannot claim Vermont residency, so the state withholding applies in virtually every FIRPTA-triggering transaction.

## Agent Liability: What Happens When You Get It Wrong

The legal exposure here is not hypothetical. Under IRC § 1445(e), if the required federal withholding is not collected and remitted, the IRS can assess the full 15% against the buyer—plus interest and penalties. Your buyer client does not get a pass because nobody at the closing table raised the issue. On the Vermont side, 32 V.S.A. § 5847 authorizes the Department of Taxes to pursue the withholding amount from the buyer or the withholding agent who failed to collect it.

For agents, the professional risk is separate from the tax liability. The Vermont Real Estate Commission, operating under 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41, has broad authority to discipline licensees who facilitate transactions that violate state law. An agent who knew or should have known that the seller was a foreign person and failed to raise FIRPTA at any point during the transaction is exposed to complaints, fines, and license suspension. Deal cancellation is also a real outcome—buyers who discover post-closing that they owe a six-figure IRS withholding bill have sued agents and brokerages for negligence.

**The penalties compound quickly.** The IRS charges a failure-to-file penalty on Form 8288 of 5% per month (up to 25%) plus a failure-to-pay penalty of 0.5% per month on the withholding amount due. Vermont imposes its own late-filing penalties under 32 V.S.A. § 3202. An agent who lets a $2 million ski lodge close without proper withholding could expose the buyer to $300,000 in federal withholding plus state obligations, penalties, and interest—all because nobody asked the seller a simple question about citizenship status.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With FIRPTA in Vermont

Three to five errors recur with alarming frequency in Vermont transactions involving foreign sellers. Recognizing these patterns is more useful than memorizing form numbers.

First, agents assume the closing attorney will catch everything. Vermont is an attorney-closing state, and most title work runs through law offices rather than title companies. That creates a false sense of security. Attorneys handle the mechanics of withholding, but they rely on information the agent provides—including whether the seller is a foreign person. If the agent never flags the issue, the attorney may not discover it until wire instructions are being prepared, or worse, not at all.

Second, agents confuse residency with citizenship. A seller who has lived in Vermont for 15 years but holds Canadian citizenship is still a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. Residency status for Vermont state tax purposes (Form RW-171-C) is a separate analysis from FIRPTA's definition of "foreign person" under IRC § 7701(b). Agents who conflate the two risk missing the federal withholding entirely while correctly handling the state piece, as [similar issues arise in other states](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026).

Third, agents wait until closing week to raise FIRPTA. The withholding certificate process (Form 8288-B) takes months. If the seller wants to reduce the 15% to their actual tax liability, that application needs to go in well before closing. An agent who surfaces the issue 72 hours before the scheduled close forces the seller into full withholding or a delayed closing—neither of which builds client trust.

Fourth, agents fail to document the seller's status in writing. Every listing agent should obtain a written representation from the seller regarding their U.S. person status early in the transaction. The IRS Form W-9 (for U.S. persons) or Form W-8BEN (for foreign persons) should be part of the listing file. Without written documentation, agents have no defense if a dispute arises later.

Fifth, agents overlook entity ownership. A Vermont LLC owned by a foreign national selling a condo in Burlington still triggers FIRPTA. The analysis looks through to the beneficial ownership, and agents who see a U.S. entity name on the deed often stop asking questions too early.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Vermont carry supervisory liability under 26 V.S.A. § 2296, which makes them responsible for the conduct of their affiliated agents. A transaction file that closes without any documentation of the seller's FIRPTA status is a compliance gap the broker owns. This is not about micromanaging every deal—it is about having systems that flag foreign-seller transactions before they become liabilities.

| Broker Audit Point | What to Look For | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Seller identification | W-9 or W-8BEN in the listing file | No citizenship/residency documentation |
| Federal withholding | Copy of Form 8288 and 8288-A | Missing or filed after 20-day deadline |
| State withholding | Copy of Vermont Form RW-171 | No state withholding form in file |
| Withholding certificate | Form 8288-B application if reduced rate claimed | Application filed too late or never approved |
| Closing disclosure | Withholding amounts reflected on settlement statement | Withholding not itemized or absent |

**Every brokerage should have a written policy requiring agents to determine seller FIRPTA status within the first week of a listing agreement.** The policy should specify which forms go into the file, who reviews them, and what happens if the agent does not complete the checklist. Brokers who rely on verbal assurances from agents that "the seller is American" are setting themselves up for a regulatory problem. The same discipline around [deadline tracking that prevents missed contingencies](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) applies here—structure beats intention.

Quarterly file audits should include a FIRPTA-specific check for any transaction involving a seller with a foreign address, a foreign-sounding entity name, or a property previously held in a foreign trust. These are imperfect heuristics, but they catch the cases that slip through when agents are juggling 20 transactions at once.

## Working With Closing Attorneys and the Vermont Department of Taxes

Vermont's attorney-closing model means the settlement attorney is typically the withholding agent responsible for collecting and remitting both the federal and state amounts. The Vermont Department of Taxes expects Form RW-171 to be filed and the withholding remitted by the due date specified on the form. Agents should confirm, in writing, that the closing attorney is handling both the IRS and Vermont filings, and request copies for the transaction file.

When a foreign seller requests a reduced withholding rate, the process involves filing IRS Form 8288-B with the IRS before closing. The IRS issues a withholding certificate specifying the reduced amount. Until that certificate arrives, the full 15% must be escrowed. Agents should advise foreign sellers to engage a U.S. tax advisor as early as possible—ideally before the property is listed. The same principle of [early process management that prevents deals from falling apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down) applies to FIRPTA compliance.

Communication with the closing attorney should happen at contract execution, not at the pre-closing walkthrough. The agent's job is to surface the foreign-seller status early, provide the W-8BEN or equivalent documentation, and confirm that all withholding amounts are reflected on the draft settlement statement before the parties sit down to sign.
