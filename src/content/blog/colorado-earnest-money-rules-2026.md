## Understanding Colorado Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Colorado earnest money & escrow laws 2026 impose specific obligations on agents and their employing brokers that go well beyond what many licensees learned in pre-licensing courses. The Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC) enforces these rules under Title 12, Article 10 of the Colorado Revised Statutes (C.R.S. 12-10-217 and 12-10-218), and violations can result in license discipline, civil lawsuits, or collapsed deals. Every transaction you touch this year demands precision on deposit timelines, trust account handling, and release procedures.

The stakes are higher than a slap on the wrist. CREC's enforcement arm has ramped up audits of brokerage trust accounts, and the 2025 rule clarifications carry forward into 2026 with zero grace period for ignorance. If you are listing a property, representing a buyer, or supervising agents who do either, this post covers what you are legally required to do—and the specific missteps that get agents into trouble.

## The Statutory Framework: C.R.S. 12-10-217 and CREC Rules

**Colorado's earnest money obligations are primarily governed by C.R.S. 12-10-217, which addresses the handling of money belonging to others.** The statute requires that all funds received by a licensee on behalf of another person be deposited into a trust account maintained by the employing broker or delivered to the escrow holder named in the contract. There is no discretion here—this is mandatory, not best practice.

CREC Rule E-17 through E-20 (found in Chapter 1 of the Commission's Rules, 4 CCR 725-1) provide the operational details. Rule E-17 governs disbursement restrictions: a broker cannot release disputed funds unless both parties consent in writing, a court orders it, or the broker files an interpleader action. Rule E-20 addresses deposit timing and record-keeping requirements for trust accounts.

The designated broker at every Colorado brokerage is personally responsible for trust account compliance, even when an employed agent physically handles the check. This means a single agent's error can expose the entire brokerage to CREC disciplinary action and civil claims from aggrieved parties.

## Key Forms and Contract Provisions Agents Must Know

Colorado's approved Contract to Buy and Sell Real Estate (Residential) is form CBS1-7-24, published by the Colorado Real Estate Commission. Section 4 of this form addresses earnest money—amount, form of payment, who holds it, and the deposit deadline. Agents who leave any of these fields blank or ambiguous create an immediate compliance risk.

| Form or Document | Purpose | Key Section for Earnest Money |
|---|---|---|
| CBS1-7-24 | Contract to Buy and Sell (Residential) | Section 4: Dates, Deadlines, and Price |
| CBS2-7-24 | Contract to Buy and Sell (Commercial) | Section 4 |
| CP40-7-24 | Counterproposal | Modifies Section 4 terms if applicable |
| Form ENM44-7-24 | Earnest Money Release | Authorizes disbursement |
| Form DIS35-7-24 | Dispute Resolution (Mediation/Arbitration) | Used when parties cannot agree on release |

**Form ENM44-7-24 is the specific document agents need when releasing earnest money upon termination or dispute resolution.** Both buyer and seller must sign it before the holder disburses funds, unless a court order intervenes. Agents who verbally authorize a title company to release funds without executing this form are violating CREC rules.

## Deposit Timelines and Trust Account Requirements

The deposit deadline is whatever the parties agree to in Section 4 of the CBS form. Most contracts specify that the earnest money must be delivered to the holder by a date certain—often within one to three business days of mutual execution. The critical point: if no specific deadline is written, CREC interprets this as requiring prompt deposit, which Commission guidance has historically defined as the next business day.

**Once funds are in the broker's possession, they must be deposited into the brokerage trust account before the contractual deadline expires.** Holding a check in a desk drawer past the deposit date is a violation of C.R.S. 12-10-217(1)(n), which lists failure to properly account for money belonging to others as grounds for license discipline.

Trust accounts in Colorado must be non-interest-bearing unless the broker has obtained written authorization from all parties to place funds in an interest-bearing account and has designated the recipient of the interest. The brokerage must maintain the account at a Colorado financial institution, and it must be labeled in a way that clearly identifies it as a trust or escrow account. Monthly reconciliation is required under CREC Rule E-20.

## Common Mistakes That Get Colorado Agents in Trouble

Mistake number one is missing the deposit deadline written in the contract. Agents sometimes receive the earnest money check from a buyer on a Friday, leave it on their desk over the weekend, and fail to deliver it to the title company or brokerage trust account before the Monday deadline. CREC treats this as a material violation regardless of intent.

Mistake number two involves releasing earnest money without both parties' written consent. When a deal falls apart and the buyer invokes an inspection objection under CBS Section 10, agents sometimes instruct the title company to release funds back to the buyer without having the seller sign Form ENM44-7-24. Even when the release seems obvious, the procedural requirement is non-negotiable. One-sided releases expose brokers to disciplinary complaints from the other party.

Mistake number three is commingling funds. Some agents—especially those operating as independent contractors with their own LLCs—mistakenly deposit earnest money into a personal or operating account rather than the brokerage trust account. This is a [per se violation under C.R.S. 12-10-217](https://www.sos.state.co.us/CCR/GenerateRulePdf.do?ruleVersionId=11324) and often triggers immediate license suspension during investigation.

Mistake number four is failing to disclose who holds the earnest money. The CBS form requires identification of the holder, but agents occasionally leave this ambiguous when multiple title companies are involved or when the listing brokerage expects to hold funds. Ambiguity here creates a chain-of-custody gap that both CREC and civil courts view unfavorably.

Mistake number five is accepting earnest money in cash without issuing a receipt and immediately depositing it. Cash deposits require especially careful documentation, and failure to receipt and deposit same-day can look like conversion or theft to an investigator reviewing a complaint.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers in Colorado bear statutory responsibility for trust account compliance under C.R.S. 12-10-218. This means the designated broker must have systems in place to verify that every earnest money deposit matches what the contract specifies—amount, form, timeline, and holder designation. Delegation to a transaction coordinator or office manager does not eliminate the broker's personal liability.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Verify |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly (minimum) | Bank balance matches ledger; no outstanding uncleared deposits older than 10 days |
| Deposit timeliness | Per transaction | Check was deposited by the date in CBS Section 4 |
| Release authorization | Per disbursement | Form ENM44-7-24 is signed by all parties before funds leave the account |
| Agent receipt documentation | Per transaction | Agent issued a written receipt for any earnest money received in person |
| Account labeling and institution | Annually | Account title includes "trust" or "escrow"; held at a Colorado bank |

Brokers should also audit for stale funds—earnest money that remains in the trust account long after closing or contract termination. CREC considers retained funds without authorization to be a potential conversion issue. If neither party responds to requests for disbursement instructions, the broker must follow the interpleader process rather than unilaterally deciding where the money goes.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, Discipline, and Deal Collapse

CREC has broad disciplinary authority under C.R.S. 12-10-217. Sanctions for earnest money violations range from letters of admonition (the lightest) to outright license revocation. Fines can reach $2,500 per violation per day in administrative proceedings. In egregious cases involving misappropriation, the Commission refers the matter to the Colorado Attorney General for criminal prosecution.

Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face civil exposure. A buyer whose earnest money is not returned promptly after proper termination can sue the holding broker for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, or both. Colorado courts have awarded damages plus attorney fees in cases where brokers unreasonably delayed disbursement or failed to follow the interpleader process for disputed funds.

**Deal collapse is the most immediate practical consequence.** If a listing agent's brokerage cannot verify that earnest money has been properly deposited, the seller's confidence erodes. Buyers who learn their deposit was mishandled often terminate under the contract's objection provisions and move on to another property—taking their financing approval and motivation with them.

## Staying Compliant as Rules Evolve

CREC updates its rules and approved forms annually, with each revision cycle typically taking effect on July 1. Agents must confirm they are using the current version of every form—an outdated CBS version with incorrect section numbering can create ambiguity about deadlines and holder designations. The Commission publishes updates on the [Colorado Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) website](https://dre.colorado.gov/), and licensees are expected to review them as part of their continuing education obligations.
