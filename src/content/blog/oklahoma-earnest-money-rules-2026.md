## Understanding Oklahoma Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Oklahoma earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who handle trust funds carelessly. The Oklahoma Real Estate Commission (OREC) enforces these rules under Title 59, Oklahoma Statutes, Sections 858-301 through 858-312, and the consequences for violations range from license suspension to criminal prosecution. Every dollar that passes through your brokerage trust account is subject to these statutes—no exceptions, no grace periods for ignorance.

The foundational statute agents must know is [Title 59 O.S. Section 858-312](https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=95052), which governs the handling, depositing, and disbursement of trust funds. This statute requires that all earnest money, security deposits, and other entrusted funds be deposited into a designated trust account maintained by the broker. Agents who operate under a broker's license cannot hold these funds in a personal account under any circumstance.

OREC also publishes specific regulations in OAC 605:10-9, which details trust account requirements including record-keeping, reconciliation schedules, and permissible uses. These regulations work in tandem with the statutory framework to create a compliance obligation that rests squarely on the broker of record—even when an associate licensee handles the physical deposit.

## Deposit Deadlines and Trust Account Requirements

**Oklahoma law requires earnest money to be deposited by the end of the next banking day after contract acceptance.** This is among the tightest deposit windows in the region and trips up agents who treat weekends or holidays as free passes. If a contract is fully executed on Thursday evening, that check must hit the trust account by Friday's close of business.

The trust account itself must be an interest-bearing or non-interest-bearing account at an FDIC-insured institution located in Oklahoma. Brokers must register the account with OREC and ensure the account title clearly identifies it as a trust or escrow account. Any interest earned on pooled trust accounts belongs to the parties as specified in the contract—or, absent such a clause, remains in the account until closing.

Monthly reconciliation is mandatory. OREC requires brokers to perform a three-way reconciliation comparing the bank statement balance, the individual client ledgers, and the checkbook or journal balance. Discrepancies must be documented and resolved before the next reconciliation period. Failure to reconcile is treated as a standalone violation even if no funds are actually missing.

## Oklahoma Forms Agents Must Use Correctly

The Oklahoma Association of REALTORS (OAR) publishes standardized forms that most brokerages adopt. For residential transactions, the primary contract form is the **OAR Residential Sales Contract (Form R-3)**, which includes earnest money terms in its opening sections. The form specifies the deposit amount, the holder of earnest money, and the deadline for delivery to the holder.

When disputes arise over earnest money, agents should be familiar with the **OAR Earnest Money Release Form** and the **Mutual Release and Cancellation Agreement**. These forms require signatures from both buyer and seller before a broker can disburse contested funds. Without both signatures, the broker must hold the money—period.

| Form | Purpose | Key Earnest Money Provision |
|------|---------|----------------------------|
| OAR Form R-3 | Residential Sales Contract | Specifies deposit amount, holder, and deposit deadline |
| OAR Earnest Money Release | Disputed fund release | Requires dual-party consent for disbursement |
| Mutual Release and Cancellation | Contract termination | Dictates who receives earnest money upon cancellation |
| OREC Trust Account Record | Broker compliance | Tracks individual deposits against ledger |
| OAR Amendment Form | Contract modifications | Can alter earnest money terms mid-transaction |

Agents should also note that OREC's own complaint forms—available on the Commission's website—allow consumers to file trust account grievances directly. A single consumer complaint triggers an audit of the specific transaction and potentially the entire trust account.

## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

**The consequences for earnest money violations in Oklahoma are not hypothetical—they are enforced regularly.** OREC publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and trust account violations consistently rank among the top reasons for license revocation. The Commission can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, and refer cases to the Oklahoma Attorney General for criminal prosecution under embezzlement or fraud statutes.

Under Title 59 O.S. Section 858-312, a broker who commingles trust funds with operating or personal accounts faces immediate suspension pending a hearing. Commingling includes depositing earnest money into a general business account, writing personal checks from the trust account, or failing to segregate funds belonging to different parties. Even a temporary deposit into the wrong account—corrected the same day—constitutes a violation.

Civil liability compounds the regulatory risk. If earnest money is lost due to broker negligence, the broker and potentially the associate agent face personal liability in civil court. Oklahoma courts have held that the fiduciary duty owed to parties in a transaction extends specifically to the safekeeping of entrusted funds, meaning standard E&O insurance exclusions for intentional acts or trust account mishandling may leave agents exposed. ## Common Mistakes Oklahoma Agents Make

The first mistake is treating the deposit deadline as flexible. Agents routinely hold checks over a weekend or "wait for Monday" when Friday acceptance should trigger a same-day or Monday deposit. OREC interprets "next banking day" literally—if the bank is open, the deposit must happen.

The second mistake involves accepting alternative forms of earnest money without contract language supporting them. While Oklahoma law does not prohibit wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or promissory notes as earnest money, the contract must specifically identify the form of deposit. Agents who accept a wire transfer when the contract says "check" create an ambiguity that can unravel the deal or expose the brokerage to liability.

Third, agents frequently fail to notify all parties when earnest money is not received by the stated deadline. Under the OAR Form R-3, the seller has the right to terminate if earnest money is not deposited as promised. An agent who knows the buyer missed the deadline but stays silent is arguably facilitating a material misrepresentation.

Fourth, releasing earnest money without proper authorization remains alarmingly common. Some agents, under pressure from a frustrated buyer or seller, release funds based on a verbal agreement or a single party's written instruction. Oklahoma law is unambiguous: absent mutual written consent, a court order, or an interpleader action, the broker must hold the funds. Knowing [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) helps explain how these errors cascade through a transaction.

Fifth, agents neglect to document the chain of custody for physical checks. From the moment a buyer hands over a check, the agent should record the date received, date delivered to the brokerage, and date deposited. OREC auditors look for this paper trail, and its absence creates a presumption of noncompliance.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokers in Oklahoma bear ultimate responsibility for every dollar in their trust account, regardless of which agent accepted the deposit.** OREC holds the broker of record liable for all trust account violations occurring under their supervision—a standard that demands proactive audit systems rather than reactive damage control.

At minimum, brokers should conduct monthly three-way reconciliations and retain records for five years, as required by OAC 605:10-9. But smart brokers go further. They implement weekly spot-checks of pending deposits against executed contracts, verify that every deposit matches the contract terms in amount and timing, and maintain a centralized log accessible to compliance staff.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Three-way reconciliation | Monthly | OAC 605:10-9 |
| Deposit timing verification | Per transaction | Title 59 O.S. Section 858-312 |
| Trust account registration confirmation | Annually | OREC licensing requirements |
| Individual ledger accuracy | Monthly | OAC 605:10-9 |
| Dormant fund review | Quarterly | Best practice / OREC guidance |
| Agent custody documentation | Per transaction | Broker supervisory duty |

Brokers should also enforce a written office policy that prohibits agents from holding earnest money checks overnight. The policy should require same-day delivery to the office or direct deposit by the buyer into the trust account. Any deviation should trigger a documented exception with the broker's written approval.

The supervisory obligation extends to training. OREC can cite a broker for inadequate supervision if an agent's trust account violation stems from a lack of training or policy enforcement. Annual compliance training—documented and signed by each agent—provides a defense against this charge. ## Disputed Earnest Money: The Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller disagree about who gets the earnest money, Oklahoma brokers face a narrow set of legal options. They can hold the funds indefinitely while awaiting mutual written release, they can file an interpleader action in district court, or they can comply with a court order directing disbursement. There is no fourth option.

An interpleader action under 12 O.S. Section 2022 allows the broker to deposit the disputed funds with the court and withdraw from the dispute entirely. The court then determines entitlement. Filing an interpleader protects the broker from claims by either party, and Oklahoma courts have consistently held that a broker who interpleads in good faith is entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees from the deposited funds.

> "A stakeholder who is in doubt as to which of two or more claimants is entitled to funds held by the stakeholder may bring an action of interpleader against all of the claimants to compel them to litigate their claims among themselves." — 12 O.S. Section 2022

Brokers should not wait indefinitely before filing. While no statutory deadline exists for initiating interpleader, OREC guidance suggests that holding disputed funds beyond 90 days without resolution or legal action may itself become a supervisory concern. Prompt action protects the license and the brokerage's reputation.
