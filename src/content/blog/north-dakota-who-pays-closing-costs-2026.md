## Understanding North Dakota Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of North Dakota who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by statute alone. It is answered by what you write into the purchase agreement, what local custom dictates, and what your client actually negotiates. Agents who treat cost allocation as an afterthought expose themselves and their brokerages to liability, delayed closings, and regulatory scrutiny from the North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC).

North Dakota is somewhat unusual among states in that it imposes no state transfer tax on real estate transactions. That single fact shifts the closing cost equation in ways that catch agents moving in from other states off guard. Without a mandatory transfer tax line item, the remaining costs — title insurance, recording fees, attorney or closing agent fees, prorations, and lender charges — become the entire battlefield of negotiation.

This post breaks down exactly which costs typically land on each side, what forms govern the allocation, and where agents routinely get it wrong.

## The Standard Closing Cost Split in North Dakota

**No North Dakota statute mandates who pays which closing cost, so every allocation is negotiable.** That said, long-standing custom across Cass, Burleigh, Grand Forks, and most other counties has produced a default pattern that most title companies and closing agents follow when the purchase agreement is silent or ambiguous.

| Cost Item | Typical Payer | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | Customary statewide, but negotiable |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Required by lender; buyer's expense |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer | N.D.C.C. Chapter 11-18 sets county fee schedule |
| Recording fees (mortgage) | Buyer | Same statutory basis |
| Real estate tax prorations | Split at closing | Prorated to date of closing per agreement terms |
| Title search/abstract update | Seller | Custom varies by county |
| Closing/settlement fee | Split or negotiated | Some counties split 50/50; others assign to buyer |
| Transfer tax | N/A | North Dakota does not impose a transfer tax |

Recording fees in North Dakota are governed by [N.D.C.C. Chapter 11-18](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t11c18.pdf), which authorizes county recorders to charge per-page fees for instruments filed. As of 2026, the base recording fee remains modest compared to coastal states, but agents should verify exact amounts with the county recorder's office where the property sits. Fee schedules can differ between Cass County and, say, McKenzie County.

## Key Forms and Where Cost Allocation Lives

The North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR) publishes the standardized forms most agents use. The primary document governing closing cost allocation is **NDAR Form 51, the Purchase Agreement**. Within Form 51, specific fill-in blanks and checkboxes address who pays for title evidence, which party covers the closing fee, and whether the seller will contribute toward the buyer's costs.

Agents also encounter NDAR Form 55, the Counter Proposal form, which is where cost allocation frequently gets renegotiated after the initial offer. If a seller counters with a reduced concession amount, the revised closing cost split must be reflected clearly on Form 55 and initialed by both parties. A verbal agreement to "split the difference" means nothing without ink on the form.

For transactions involving [earnest money](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026) held by a title company or brokerage trust account, NDREC Administrative Rule 70-02-01 outlines the trust account requirements that govern how those funds are handled. Any earnest money applied toward closing costs at settlement must be accounted for on the final closing disclosure.

## What Happens When an Agent Gets This Wrong

**Failing to specify closing cost responsibilities on the purchase agreement is not just sloppy — it is a potential NDREC complaint waiting to happen.** The North Dakota Real Estate Commission has authority under N.D.C.C. Chapter 43-23 to investigate complaints, issue reprimands, impose fines, and suspend or revoke licenses. An agent whose ambiguous paperwork causes a deal to collapse or a client to absorb unexpected costs could face a formal complaint alleging incompetence or negligence under N.D.C.C. Section 43-23-11.1.

Beyond regulatory action, civil liability is a real risk. If a buyer shows up at closing expecting the seller to cover a $4,000 concession that was never documented on Form 51 or Form 55, the agent who made a verbal promise may be personally liable for the shortfall. North Dakota courts have recognized that agents owe fiduciary duties to their clients, and failure to memorialize material terms in writing breaches that duty.

Deal cancellation is the most common consequence. Title companies in North Dakota will not close a transaction when the settlement statement reflects costs that neither party has agreed to absorb. The closing gets postponed, the buyer's rate lock may expire, and everyone at the table blames the agent.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Costs in North Dakota

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in NDREC complaints and brokerage audit findings. Every one of them is preventable.

**First, agents leave cost allocation fields blank on NDAR Form 51.** They assume "custom" will fill the gap. Title companies do follow custom when the agreement is silent, but if the parties had a different expectation, the agent has created a dispute with no written resolution. Fill in every blank. Every single one.

Second, agents confuse seller concessions with price reductions. A seller agreeing to pay $5,000 toward the buyer's closing costs is not the same as reducing the purchase price by $5,000. The concession must appear as a separate line item on the purchase agreement, and the lender must approve it within their program limits. FHA, VA, and USDA loans each cap seller concessions at different percentages, and exceeding the cap can kill the loan.

Third, agents fail to update closing cost terms when an addendum or counter proposal changes the deal. If the original Form 51 says the seller pays the full closing fee and Form 55 shifts half to the buyer, both documents must be consistent and clearly referenced. Contradictory forms create the exact ambiguity that leads to disputes.

Fourth, agents operating near the Minnesota or Montana borders sometimes assume neighboring-state customs apply. North Dakota's lack of a transfer tax and its reliance on abstract title evidence in many rural counties make its closing cost profile distinct. An agent accustomed to Minnesota's deed tax may quote costs incorrectly to a North Dakota client.

Fifth, agents neglect to account for [special assessments](/blog/where-deals-break-down). In many North Dakota municipalities, particularly in Fargo and Bismarck, special assessments for infrastructure improvements can represent a significant cost at closing. Whether the seller pays off remaining assessments or the buyer assumes them must be explicitly addressed in the purchase agreement.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in North Dakota carry supervisory liability under N.D.C.C. Section 43-23-11.1 and NDREC Administrative Rules. If a supervised agent's paperwork is deficient, the broker's license is on the line too. Audit protocols should target specific, high-risk areas.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Form 51 cost allocation fields | Every blank filled, no contradictions | Client disputes, NDREC complaint |
| Seller concession caps | Concession within lender program limits | Loan denial, closing delay |
| Counter proposals (Form 55) | Consistent with original Form 51 terms | Ambiguity, deal collapse |
| Special assessment disclosure | Addressed in agreement, amounts verified | Post-closing claims against agent |
| Trust account compliance | Earnest money deposited per Rule 70-02-01 | NDREC fine, license suspension |

**Brokers should pull a random sample of closed files monthly and review Forms 51 and 55 specifically for blank or contradictory closing cost provisions.** This is not optional diligence — it is the minimum standard that NDREC auditors will apply during a brokerage inspection. A pattern of incomplete forms signals a systemic training failure that regulators treat more seriously than a single-agent error.

Brokers also need to confirm that agents are not making unauthorized representations about closing costs in MLS remarks or marketing materials. Advertising "seller pays all closing costs" when the purchase agreement has not yet been executed creates a binding obligation in some interpretations and an ethics complaint in others. Keep marketing language conditional until the terms are signed.

## How Prorations and Prepaid Items Affect the Final Number

Real estate taxes in North Dakota are paid in arrears, which means proration calculations at closing determine how much the seller owes for the portion of the tax year they occupied the property. The standard proration method in most North Dakota transactions uses a 365-day year calculated to the date of closing. The purchase agreement should specify whether proration is based on the most recent tax bill or an estimated current-year amount.

Prepaid items on the buyer's side — including homeowners insurance premiums, prepaid interest, and escrow reserves — can add thousands to the buyer's closing costs. Agents who quote buyers a rough closing cost estimate without factoring in prepaids underestimate the actual cash needed at closing. That miscalculation erodes trust and can [cause deals to fall apart](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) when the buyer sees the final closing disclosure.

Heating fuel prorations deserve special mention in North Dakota. For properties with propane or fuel oil tanks, the seller is typically credited for fuel remaining in the tank at closing. This line item catches agents off guard if they have never handled rural North Dakota transactions, but it is standard practice and should be addressed in writing.
