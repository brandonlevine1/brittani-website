## Understanding Wyoming Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Mishandling earnest money is one of the fastest ways for a licensed agent to face disciplinary action in Wyoming. The rules governing trust accounts, deposit timelines, and disbursement are specific, enforceable, and carry real consequences — yet they trip up agents and brokers every year. This guide breaks down **Wyoming earnest money & escrow laws 2026** as they apply to daily practice, with the specific statutes, forms, and enforcement mechanisms you need to know.

Wyoming's regulatory framework for earnest money sits primarily within the [Wyoming Real Estate License Act (W.S. 33-28-101 through 33-28-401)](https://www.wyorealtors.com) and the rules promulgated by the Wyoming Real Estate Commission (WREC). The Commission's Chapter 3 rules govern trust account management, while the standardized forms — developed by the Wyoming Association of REALTORS (WAR) — dictate how earnest money terms are documented in every residential transaction. If you are licensed in this state, these are not suggestions. They are binding requirements.

## Statutory Framework: Where the Rules Come From

**Wyoming Statute 33-28-303 establishes the foundation for how brokers must handle funds belonging to others.** It requires that all earnest money, security deposits, and other entrusted funds be deposited into a separate trust or escrow account maintained by the broker. Commingling personal or business operating funds with trust account money is a violation that can result in license suspension or revocation.

WREC Chapter 3 rules add operational detail. Section 3(a) requires that trust accounts be maintained in a federally insured financial institution located in Wyoming. Section 3(b) mandates that brokers keep detailed records of all deposits, disbursements, and balances, and that those records be available for audit by the Commission at any time.

The distinction between "broker" and "agent" matters here. Individual licensees acting as sales agents do not maintain trust accounts — their designated broker does. But the agent is not off the hook. Agents who fail to deliver earnest money to their broker promptly, or who misrepresent the status of a deposit, face their own disciplinary exposure under W.S. 33-28-303 and Commission rules.

## The Forms That Govern the Transaction

Wyoming residential transactions rely on standardized forms created by WAR and approved for use by WREC. The most relevant form for earnest money is the **Purchase and Sale Agreement, commonly referred to as the WR-1**. This form includes specific blanks for the earnest money amount, the form of payment (check, wire, etc.), the identity of the escrow holder, and the deadline for deposit.

The WR-1 also addresses what happens to earnest money if the deal falls apart. Paragraph language covers contingency failures, default by either party, and the process for releasing funds. Agents who modify this language without understanding its legal effect are creating liability — for themselves and for their brokers.

Beyond the WR-1, agents should be familiar with the **Earnest Money Release (WR-30)** form. This form is used when both parties agree to release earnest money to one side or the other after a contract termination. It requires signatures from both buyer and seller, a requirement that reflects Wyoming law and prevents unilateral disbursement by the escrow holder. Skipping this form, or improvising your own version, is a compliance failure.

| Form | Purpose | Key Earnest Money Function |
|------|---------|---------------------------|
| WR-1 (Purchase and Sale Agreement) | Defines all transaction terms | Sets deposit amount, deadline, holder, and default remedies |
| WR-30 (Earnest Money Release) | Authorizes disbursement after termination | Requires dual-party signatures for any release |
| WR-9 (Inspection Resolution) | Documents inspection contingency outcomes | May trigger earnest money refund if contingency not met |
| WR-15 (Amendment/Extension) | Modifies existing contract terms | Can alter earnest money amount or deposit deadline |

## Deposit Timelines and Trust Account Rules

**The deposit timeline for earnest money is dictated by the contract itself, not by a blanket state statute.** The WR-1 includes a fill-in-the-blank field for when earnest money must be deposited. Standard practice in Wyoming is within two to three business days of the binding agreement date, but the parties can negotiate a different window. Whatever deadline appears in the contract is enforceable.

Once the broker or escrow agent receives the funds, deposit into the trust account must happen promptly. WREC rules do not specify a separate statutory deposit window beyond what the contract requires, but the Commission interprets "promptly" strictly. Holding a check in a desk drawer for a week while waiting for the buyer to confirm something is not prompt. It is a violation.

Trust account reconciliation is another area where Wyoming enforces discipline. Brokers must reconcile their trust accounts at least monthly, comparing the bank statement balance to the ledger of individual transaction balances. Discrepancies must be identified and corrected immediately. Failure to reconcile is one of the most common audit findings, and it can result in fines or formal disciplinary proceedings. ## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences for mishandling earnest money in Wyoming range from inconvenient to career-ending. WREC has authority under W.S. 33-28-303 to suspend or revoke a license for trust account violations. The Commission can also impose fines, require additional education, or place a licensee on probation.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. A buyer who loses earnest money due to an agent's negligence or unauthorized disbursement can sue for damages. A seller who doesn't receive funds they're contractually entitled to has the same recourse. These lawsuits are expensive even when the agent wins, and errors and omissions insurance may not cover intentional or grossly negligent trust account mismanagement.

Deal cancellation is also on the table. If earnest money is not deposited by the contractual deadline, the non-breaching party may have the right to terminate the agreement and pursue the deposit as liquidated damages. This is not hypothetical — it happens in Wyoming transactions, and it generates complaints to WREC regularly.

## Common Mistakes Wyoming Agents Make With Earnest Money

**The first and most frequent error is treating the deposit deadline as flexible.** Agents sometimes hold earnest money checks while waiting for inspection results or financing updates, reasoning that the deal might fall through anyway. The contract deadline is the contract deadline. Missing it exposes the buyer to default and the agent to a Commission complaint.

The second common mistake is failing to use the WR-30 Earnest Money Release form when a transaction terminates. Agents will sometimes just tell the escrow holder to "send the money back" via email or a phone call. Without a properly executed WR-30 bearing both parties' signatures, the escrow holder should not — and in most cases will not — release funds. The agent who skips this step creates a dispute where none needed to exist.

Third, agents routinely forget to confirm that the earnest money was actually deposited. Handing a check to the front desk at the brokerage or dropping it in a title company's inbox is not the same as confirming it was deposited into the trust account. Agents should follow up and document the deposit date, because if a dispute arises months later, "I think I dropped it off" is not a defense.

Fourth, some agents accept earnest money directly and hold it personally rather than delivering it to their broker or the designated escrow holder. This is a clear violation of W.S. 33-28-303 and WREC rules. The agent is not the trust account holder. Period.

Fifth, agents occasionally draft custom earnest money clauses on the WR-1 without legal review. Adding language about forfeiture triggers, interest allocation, or alternative disbursement mechanisms without attorney input creates ambiguity that often leads to disputes and potential liability. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear the ultimate responsibility for trust account compliance in Wyoming. WREC's rules assign the designated broker as the custodian of all entrusted funds, and the Commission audits brokers — not individual agents — when complaints arise.

**Every brokerage should have a written trust account policy that addresses deposit procedures, reconciliation schedules, and disbursement authorization.** This policy must be communicated to every agent in the office, and brokers should require written acknowledgment of it. An agent who violates the policy is a liability, but a broker who never established one is a bigger liability.

Monthly trust account reconciliation is not optional. Brokers must compare their bank statement to the transaction-level ledger and identify any discrepancies. The reconciliation should be documented and retained for at least five years, consistent with WREC's record-keeping requirements under Chapter 3.

| Broker Responsibility | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|----------------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly (minimum) | WREC Chapter 3 |
| Agent deposit compliance review | Per transaction | W.S. 33-28-303 |
| Record retention (trust account) | At least 5 years | WREC Chapter 3 |
| Written trust account policy | Ongoing / updated annually | Best practice under WREC rules |
| WR-30 collection before disbursement | Per terminated transaction | WAR form requirements |

Brokers should also audit whether agents are using the correct forms. A surprising number of agents use outdated versions of the WR-1 or WR-30, or substitute forms from other states or generic templates found online. Only current WAR-approved forms should be used in Wyoming transactions. Out-of-date forms can contain superseded language that conflicts with current law or Commission rules, creating ambiguity that fuels disputes. ## Escrow Disputes and the Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller disagree about who gets the earnest money, the escrow holder is caught in the middle. Wyoming law allows the holder — typically the broker or title company — to file an interpleader action in district court. This deposits the disputed funds with the court and asks a judge to determine who is entitled to them.

Filing an interpleader protects the escrow holder from liability to both sides. It is not a sign of failure; it is the legally correct response when conflicting demands are made and the parties refuse to execute a mutual release on the WR-30. Brokers who try to resolve the dispute themselves by picking a side and disbursing funds unilaterally risk disciplinary action and a lawsuit from the losing party.

The cost of an interpleader filing — typically attorney fees and court costs — is often deducted from the escrowed funds before the balance is deposited with the court. This should be disclosed to the parties, and many experienced brokers include interpleader language in their office policies so agents can set expectations early.
