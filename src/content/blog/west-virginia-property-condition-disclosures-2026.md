## Why West Virginia Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026 Matter More Than Ever

Getting property condition and environmental disclosures wrong in West Virginia can unravel a deal faster than a failed appraisal. The rules governing **West Virginia property condition & environmental disclosures 2026** have not changed dramatically from prior years, but enforcement patterns and buyer sophistication have—meaning the margin for agent error is thinner than it used to be. Agents who treat these forms as afterthoughts rather than legal instruments are setting themselves up for complaints to the West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC).

West Virginia's Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act, codified at [West Virginia Code Chapter 36, Article 6A](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/WVCODE/code.cfm?chap=36&art=6A), requires sellers of residential real property to provide buyers with a written disclosure statement covering known material defects. The statute applies to most one-to-four-unit residential sales, though several exemption categories exist. Understanding what must be disclosed, when it must be delivered, and what environmental hazards trigger additional federal obligations is fundamental to keeping transactions on track and licenses intact.

## The Statutory Framework: WV Code 36-6A

**The West Virginia Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act (WV Code 36-6A-1 through 36-6A-9) is the primary statute governing seller disclosures.** It mandates that sellers provide a written disclosure form addressing the condition of the property's structural components, systems, and known defects. The seller's obligation is limited to actual knowledge—they are not required to conduct inspections or hire professionals to discover latent defects.

Section 36-6A-2 outlines specific exemptions. Transfers resulting from foreclosure, transfers by a fiduciary administering a decedent's estate, court-ordered sales, transfers between spouses or co-owners, and transfers by a government entity are all exempt. If your listing falls into one of these categories, document the exemption clearly in the file rather than simply skipping the form.

Section 36-6A-4 establishes the seller's duty to disclose in good faith all known material defects. A "material defect" means a condition that would have a significant adverse impact on the property's value or that involves an unreasonable risk to people on the property. Agents do not fill out this form for the seller, but they do have a duty to advise the seller of their disclosure obligations and to deliver the completed form to the buyer or buyer's agent.

## The Forms Agents Actually Use

The WVREC does not publish a single mandatory state-authored disclosure form with a fixed form number the way some states do. Instead, the West Virginia Association of Realtors (WVAR) publishes a standardized Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement that most brokerages adopt. This form tracks the statutory requirements of WV Code 36-6A and is updated periodically by WVAR's forms committee.

Agents should confirm with their brokerage which version is current for 2026 transactions. The WVAR form typically covers structural conditions, roof age and leaks, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, water and sewage systems, environmental hazards (including known radon levels, lead-based paint, underground storage tanks, and mine subsidence), and boundary or easement issues. If your brokerage uses a proprietary form, verify that it covers every category required under 36-6A-4.

For environmental disclosures specifically, two additional forms apply depending on the property:

| Disclosure Type | Governing Law | When Required | Who Provides It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure | 42 U.S.C. 4852d / 24 CFR 35 | Pre-1978 residential properties | Seller and agent jointly |
| Mine Subsidence Disclosure | WV Code 36-6A-4(a)(14) | Properties in areas with known underground mining | Seller on property disclosure form |
| Flood Zone Disclosure | National Flood Insurance Act / WVAR form | Properties in FEMA-designated flood zones | Seller on property disclosure form |
| Radon Hazard | EPA guidance / WVAR form field | All residential sales (recommended) | Seller discloses known test results |

The federal lead-based paint disclosure uses the EPA/HUD form titled "Disclosure of Information on Lead-Based Paint and/or Lead-Based Paint Hazards." This is not optional for pre-1978 homes—it is required by federal law regardless of state exemptions.

## Environmental Hazards Specific to West Virginia

West Virginia's geography and industrial history create environmental disclosure issues that agents in other states rarely encounter. Mine subsidence is the most prominent. Properties sitting above or near historical coal mining operations may be subject to ground movement, and WV Code 36-6A-4(a)(14) specifically requires sellers to disclose known mine subsidence risks.

**Agents working in southern coalfield counties—Boone, Logan, Mingo, McDowell, Raleigh—should treat mine subsidence questions as high-priority items on every disclosure form.** Buyers relocating from out of state often have zero awareness of this risk, and failing to address it creates post-closing disputes that frequently result in complaints to the WVREC.

Underground storage tanks (USTs) present another common environmental issue, particularly for rural properties that previously used fuel oil heating. If the seller knows about an active or decommissioned UST on the property, that information must appear on the disclosure form. The West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection (WVDEP) maintains a database of registered USTs, and agents would be wise to advise sellers to check their records if uncertainty exists.

Radon exposure is a known concern throughout much of West Virginia. While the state does not mandate radon testing or disclosure by statute in the way some states do, the WVAR disclosure form includes a field for known radon test results. If the seller has conducted a radon test, they must disclose the results. Advising buyers to conduct their own radon testing during the inspection period is standard practice and should be documented in writing. ## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance with West Virginia's disclosure laws range from deal cancellation to civil liability to license discipline. Under WV Code 36-6A-5, if a seller fails to provide the required disclosure, the buyer may terminate the purchase agreement. This right of termination typically exists until the disclosure is delivered and the buyer has had a reasonable opportunity to review it.

Civil liability under WV Code 36-6A-7 allows a buyer to bring a cause of action against the seller for actual damages resulting from a failure to disclose known material defects. While the statute targets sellers, agents can be drawn into litigation under negligence theories if they knew or should have known about a defect and failed to disclose it. West Virginia's common law duty of honesty and fair dealing applies to licensees independently of the seller's statutory obligations.

**The WVREC can discipline agents under WV Code 30-40-19 for conduct that demonstrates incompetence, untrustworthiness, or failure to act in accordance with applicable law.** Penalties include formal reprimand, mandatory additional education, fine, license suspension, or license revocation. A pattern of missing disclosure deadlines or failing to ensure delivery of required forms is exactly the type of conduct that triggers an investigation.

For lead-based paint violations specifically, penalties are governed at the federal level. The EPA can assess civil penalties up to $19,507 per violation under the Toxic Substances Control Act, and both listing agents and sellers face liability. HUD enforcement actions for lead paint non-disclosure have increased in recent years, making this one of the highest-risk compliance areas for agents handling pre-1978 inventory.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Disclosures

**Mistake number one: Filling out the disclosure form on behalf of the seller.** This happens more often than brokers want to admit. An agent reads the questions aloud, interprets the seller's verbal responses, and checks boxes themselves. The form is a seller's document reflecting the seller's knowledge. The moment an agent's handwriting appears on that form, they have potentially assumed liability for the accuracy of statements that are not theirs to make.

Mistake number two: Failing to deliver the disclosure before the buyer signs the purchase agreement—or failing to document when delivery occurred. The timing of disclosure delivery matters for the buyer's termination rights. If you cannot prove when the buyer received the form, you cannot prove when the review clock started. Get a signature and date on receipt every single time.

Mistake number three: Treating the lead-based paint disclosure as an afterthought or forgetting it entirely on pre-1978 properties. The federal form must be signed by the seller, the buyer, and both agents. The buyer must also receive the EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home." Missing any element of this creates federal liability that sits on top of—not instead of—state liability.

Mistake number four: Ignoring the "as-is" misconception. Some agents believe that listing a property "as-is" eliminates the seller's disclosure obligation. It does not. WV Code 36-6A applies regardless of the condition language in the purchase agreement. An "as-is" sale affects the buyer's right to demand repairs, not the seller's obligation to disclose known defects.

Mistake number five: Not updating the disclosure when new information surfaces. If the seller discovers a defect between the time the disclosure is signed and closing—say, a pipe bursts or a new crack appears in the foundation—the disclosure must be amended. Agents who learn about new defects and stay silent are inviting both commission complaints and civil claims.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under WV Code 30-40-19 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. A broker who does not have a system to verify disclosure compliance is exposed to liability every time an agent skips a step. Here is what a meaningful audit program should include.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Seller disclosure form present in file | Signed by seller with date; matches current WVAR version | Every transaction |
| Lead paint form for pre-1978 homes | All four signatures present; EPA pamphlet delivery confirmed | Every applicable transaction |
| Delivery confirmation to buyer | Buyer's signed receipt with date | Every transaction |
| Exemption documentation | Written basis for exemption cited in file if no disclosure provided | As applicable |
| Amended disclosures | Any mid-transaction updates reflected in a revised form | Spot-check quarterly |

Brokers should also verify that agents are not advising sellers on how to answer disclosure questions. The line between explaining what the form asks and coaching a seller to minimize responses is thin, and it gets crossed more often in competitive markets where agents fear disclosure items will kill deals. Training sessions at least annually—focused specifically on disclosure obligations rather than general compliance—are one of the most effective ways to reduce risk.

## Integrating Disclosures Into Your Transaction Workflow

Disclosure compliance is not a standalone task—it is embedded in the transaction timeline alongside title work, inspections, and financing contingencies. For agents who handle their own coordination, the disclosure form should go to the seller for completion at the same time the listing agreement is signed. Do not wait until you have a buyer under contract. By then, delays in getting the form completed can compress timelines and trigger the very termination rights you want to avoid.

Pair your disclosure workflow with your understanding of [West Virginia title and closing procedures](/blog/west-virginia-title-and-closing-procedures-2026) to ensure all documents flow to the closing agent's file simultaneously. A title company or attorney receiving a file without a signed disclosure will often flag the deficiency—but relying on them to catch your oversights is not a compliance strategy.

The 2026 landscape for **West Virginia property condition & environmental disclosures 2026** rewards agents and brokers who build disclosure delivery into their checklists from day one rather than scrambling to produce forms at the eleventh hour. Treat every disclosure as a liability shield, not paperwork, and your files will close cleaner, your clients will trust you more, and your license will stay exactly where it belongs.
