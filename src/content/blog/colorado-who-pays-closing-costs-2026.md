## Understanding Colorado Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Colorado who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by a single statute. It is answered by contract terms, county customs, and whatever the parties agree to in the Colorado Real Estate Commission-approved Contract to Buy and Sell (CBS). Agents who treat cost allocation as "standard" without documenting it properly expose themselves and their brokers to liability.

Colorado closing costs typically total between 2% and 5% of the purchase price for buyers and 1% to 3% for sellers, excluding commissions. The split depends on the contract language agents draft or negotiate on behalf of their clients. Getting it wrong does not just frustrate a deal—it can trigger a formal complaint to the Division of Real Estate under the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA).

This post breaks down who customarily pays what, which forms control the allocation, and where agents routinely make errors that put their licenses at risk.

## The Default Allocation: Colorado Custom vs. Contract Terms

**Colorado has no statute that mandates which party pays any specific closing cost.** Unlike states with statutory transfer tax splits, Colorado leaves cost allocation almost entirely to negotiation. What most agents call "standard" is actually county-level custom enforced by local title companies.

In the majority of Front Range counties—Denver, Jefferson, Arapahoe, Boulder, Douglas—custom assigns the owner's title insurance premium to the seller and the lender's title policy to the buyer. The documentary fee (currently $0.01 per $100 of consideration under C.R.S. 39-13-102) is customarily split or paid by the seller, but again, the contract controls.

The CBS forms (CBS1-6-24 for residential, CBS2-6-24 for land, CBS4-6-24 for income property) contain Section 10, titled "Closing Costs." This is where agents must explicitly state who pays which fees. Leaving fields blank or relying on verbal agreements is where deals unravel.

| Owner's Title Insurance | Seller | Seller | Yes |

Mountain communities like Summit, Eagle, and Pitkin counties sometimes shift the settlement fee entirely to the buyer. Agents working transactions in unfamiliar counties should confirm local custom with the title company before drafting the CBS.

## The Forms That Control Everything

The Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC) publishes mandatory-use forms for most residential transactions. Under Commission Rule 7.1 (4 CCR 725-1), licensees must use the most current Commission-approved forms unless an attorney drafts an alternative. The relevant forms for closing cost allocation include:

The CBS1-6-24 (Contract to Buy and Sell - Residential) is the primary document. Section 10 breaks costs into subsections: closing company fees, association fees, taxes, document preparation, and title insurance. Each subsection requires the agent to designate responsibility to buyer, seller, or a split arrangement.

The Counterproposal (CP40-6-24) and Amend/Extend (AE41-6-24) forms are used when parties renegotiate cost allocation after initial contract acceptance. Agents must reference the specific CBS section number and cost line item being changed. A vague counterproposal stating "seller to pay buyer's closing costs" without dollar limits or category specifics creates enforcement problems at settlement.

The Closing Instructions form is not Commission-mandated, but most title companies require one. Agents should ensure the closing instructions mirror the CBS language exactly. Discrepancies between the CBS and closing instructions are a top source of delayed closings in Colorado, particularly when seller credits are capped by the lender.

## Seller Credits and Lender Limitations

**Seller concessions—where the seller credits the buyer a lump sum toward closing costs—remain the most misunderstood element in Colorado transactions.** The CBS allows any dollar amount of seller credit, but the buyer's lender will enforce its own caps. Conventional loans under Fannie Mae guidelines cap seller concessions at 3% for borrowers putting less than 10% down, 6% for 10-25% down, and 9% for more than 25% down.

FHA loans cap seller concessions at 6% of the sale price. VA loans cap at 4% but define "concessions" differently than conventional lenders, excluding certain prepaid items. Agents who write a $30,000 seller credit on a $400,000 FHA purchase will force a contract amendment when underwriting flags the overage.

The proper approach in the CBS is to include language such as "Seller shall credit Buyer up to $X toward Buyer's closing costs, prepaid items, and lender fees, subject to lender limitations." This language appears in Section 4 (Price and Terms) as an additional provision or in Section 10. Putting the credit in the wrong section does not void it, but it can confuse title companies and [slow down the transaction pipeline](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Cost Allocation

Experienced agents still stumble on cost allocation in Colorado. These five errors account for the majority of complaints filed with DORA's Division of Real Estate regarding closing disputes.

**First, agents leave Section 10 fields blank and assume "custom" will apply.** The CBS form does not include a checkbox for "per local custom." If no party is designated for a specific fee, both parties may refuse to pay it at closing, triggering a breach dispute. CREC's position, published in its newsletter guidance, is that agents have a duty to complete all contract provisions.

Second, agents confuse the documentary fee with a transfer tax. Colorado's documentary fee under C.R.S. 39-13-102 is technically paid by the "person who makes, signs, issues, or delivers" the deed, which is the seller. But the statute allows parties to contract otherwise, and some mountain-county customs split it. Agents who tell buyers "you never pay the doc fee" are making a blanket statement unsupported by law.

Third, agents fail to account for HOA fees properly. Colorado's Common Interest Ownership Act (CCIOA), codified at C.R.S. 38-33.3-316, allows HOAs to charge transfer fees, status letter fees, and record change fees. These can total $500 to $1,500. Agents who neglect to allocate these in the CBS leave clients arguing over who writes the check at closing.

Fourth, agents write seller credits that exceed lender limits and then scramble to amend the contract during underwriting. This delays closing, sometimes past the contractual deadline in Section 4, and can give either party grounds to terminate.

Fifth, agents fail to update the Amend/Extend form when inspection negotiations result in cost shifts. If the buyer and seller agree during inspection resolution that the seller will pay for a repair credit applied at closing, that agreement must be memorialized in writing. Verbal agreements carry no weight under Colorado's Statute of Frauds (C.R.S. 38-10-108).

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers in Colorado carry supervisory liability under C.R.S. 12-10-201 and CREC Rule 6.14. When an agent's closing cost error results in a complaint, DORA's investigation typically names both the agent and the employing broker. Brokers cannot delegate compliance awareness to transaction coordinators or office managers and expect to avoid sanction.

Brokers should audit three specific areas on every transaction file. First, verify that Section 10 of the CBS is fully completed with no blank fields. Second, confirm that any seller credit in Section 4 includes a dollar cap and the phrase "subject to lender limitations." Third, ensure that any mid-transaction cost reallocation—arising from inspection objections, appraisal issues, or lender conditions—is documented on the AE41-6-24 form with specific reference to the CBS section being modified.

CREC's [Division of Real Estate enforcement actions page](https://dre.colorado.gov/licensee-lookup) shows that supervisory failures related to contract completion are among the most frequent grounds for broker discipline. Fines range from $1,000 to $5,000 per violation, with suspension or revocation reserved for repeat offenders or cases involving consumer financial harm.

Brokers who manage high-volume teams should consider automating file audits to [catch missing fields before submission to title](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory). A single blank CBS section does not seem catastrophic until a disgruntled party files a complaint six months post-closing.

## Liability Exposure and Deal Cancellation Risks

An agent who misallocates or fails to document closing costs faces exposure on multiple fronts. Under C.R.S. 12-10-217(1)(n), a licensee commits a violation by "failing to disclose to any party all known material facts." If an agent knows the buyer's lender caps seller concessions at 3% but writes a 5% credit anyway without disclosure, that qualifies as a material omission.

The CBS includes deadlines that interact with cost allocation errors. Section 25 (Termination) allows either party to terminate upon material breach. If the seller cannot deliver the contractually promised credit due to lender restrictions, the buyer may argue the contract is frustrated and demand earnest money return. The result is a dead deal and potential E&O claims against both agents.

CREC can impose discipline ranging from a letter of admonition to license revocation. Between January 2024 and March 2026, the Division of Real Estate issued 14 formal actions citing improper contract preparation as a contributing factor. Agents should not assume that "everybody does it this way" constitutes a defense.

## Preparing for 2026 Market Conditions

Colorado's 2026 market conditions—elevated interest rates holding above 6%, increased inventory on the Front Range, and tighter appraisal thresholds—have shifted negotiating power. Sellers are more frequently agreeing to concessions to keep deals together. This means agents on both sides must be more precise in drafting cost allocation language than they were during the 2020-2022 seller's market.

Agents working with first-time buyers should model total closing costs before making offers. Title companies will provide preliminary fee estimates, and agents should use these to back into realistic credit requests. Writing a $15,000 seller credit on a conventional loan with 5% down is pointless—it will be cut to approximately $10,200 in underwriting.
