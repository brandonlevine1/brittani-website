## Why Vermont's Tax Code Makes 1031 Exchanges More Complex Than You Think

Federal Section 1031 exchanges are already detail-heavy transactions, but Vermont layers on state-specific withholding rules that catch agents off guard every year. Understanding Vermont 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is not optional if you handle investment property sales in this state. The consequences of a missed form or a botched withholding calculation land squarely on your client — and sometimes on you.

Vermont conforms to the Internal Revenue Code's treatment of like-kind exchanges under IRC Section 1031, meaning a properly executed exchange defers both federal and Vermont state capital gains tax. That conformity, codified in Vermont's adoption of the federal adjusted gross income as the starting point for state tax calculations under 32 V.S.A. Section 5811, sounds simple on paper. In practice, it introduces a trap that even experienced agents miss: the state's nonresident withholding statute operates independently from the 1031 deferral.

This means a seller who qualifies for full federal deferral can still face a 2.5% withholding at closing unless the right paperwork is filed with the [Vermont Department of Taxes](https://tax.vermont.gov/). That is not a minor administrative detail — on a $600,000 property, it is $15,000 pulled from the exchange proceeds, potentially disqualifying the exchange itself.

## Vermont Nonresident Withholding and the 1031 Collision

**Under 32 V.S.A. Section 5847, any buyer of Vermont real estate from a nonresident seller must withhold 2.5% of the gross sale price and remit it to the Vermont Department of Taxes.** This statute does not carve out an automatic exception for 1031 exchanges. The withholding applies unless the seller obtains a reduced withholding certificate.

The mechanism for requesting relief is Form WH-435, titled "Request for Reduction of Withholding" and filed with the Vermont Department of Taxes. Sellers engaged in a 1031 exchange must submit this form, along with documentation proving the exchange qualifies under IRC Section 1031, before closing. If the Department grants the request, the withholding can be reduced to zero.

Here is where timing becomes a deal-breaker. The Department does not guarantee a response timeline on WH-435 requests. Agents who submit the form a week before closing often find themselves scrambling when the approval has not arrived. The closing attorney may refuse to release funds without either a withholding certificate or the full 2.5% held back, and pulling that money from exchange proceeds can blow the entire deferral.

## Key Forms and Filing Requirements for Vermont 1031 Exchanges

Agents and their clients interact with several state-specific documents during a Vermont 1031 exchange. Getting any of these wrong creates liability.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Who Files It | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form WH-435 | Request reduced or waived withholding for nonresident sellers | Seller or QI | Before closing (recommend 30+ days) |
| Form WHT-434 | Remit nonresident withholding to Vermont | Buyer or closing agent | 30 days after closing |
| Form IN-111 | Vermont individual income tax return (report deferred gain) | Seller | April 15 of following tax year |
| Form BI-471 | Business income tax return (for entity sellers) | Selling entity | Varies by entity type |

**Form WHT-434 is the document the closing attorney or title company files to remit the withholding payment.** If the WH-435 reduction was not approved in time, this form must accompany the 2.5% payment within 30 days of closing. Failure to file it triggers penalties under 32 V.S.A. Section 3202, which imposes a 5% per month late-filing penalty up to a maximum of 25%.

The seller's obligation does not end at closing, either. Even with a successful 1031 exchange, the seller must report the transaction on their Vermont income tax return — Form IN-111 for individuals — and attach federal Form 8824 showing the like-kind exchange details. Vermont does not have its own equivalent of Form 8824, but the Department of Taxes expects it as supporting documentation.

## Vermont 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: What Changed and What Stayed the Same

Vermont has not enacted new legislation in 2026 that alters the fundamental treatment of 1031 exchanges at the state level. The state continues to conform to federal IRC Section 1031 without modification, and the nonresident withholding rate remains at 2.5% of gross proceeds under 32 V.S.A. Section 5847.

What has shifted is enforcement posture. The Vermont Department of Taxes has increased scrutiny on nonresident real estate transactions, particularly those involving out-of-state LLCs and trusts. Agents representing sellers who hold Vermont investment properties through multi-member LLCs registered in states like Delaware or Wyoming should expect additional documentation requests on WH-435 filings. The Department wants to verify that the entity structure does not mask a taxable disposition disguised as an exchange.

The federal 45-day identification period and 180-day exchange period still govern the transaction timeline. Vermont does not impose its own separate deadlines, but agents should understand that Vermont's withholding clock and the federal exchange clock run simultaneously. A delay in one can derail the other, which is why [missing deadlines remains the most common reason deals fall apart](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Vermont 1031 Exchange Transactions

**The following errors show up repeatedly in Vermont 1031 exchange deals, and each one carries real financial consequences for clients.**

The first mistake is treating the WH-435 as a closing-week task. Agents who wait until the final days before closing to file the withholding reduction request almost always face one of two outcomes: either the approval comes late and the closing is delayed, or the full 2.5% is withheld and the seller's exchange proceeds fall short. Filing at least 30 days before the anticipated closing date is the minimum standard.

The second mistake is assuming resident sellers are exempt from all state reporting. Vermont residents do not face the 2.5% withholding, but they are still required to report the 1031 exchange on their state return. Agents who tell resident clients that a 1031 exchange means "no Vermont tax paperwork" are giving incorrect advice that could lead to audit exposure.

Third, agents frequently confuse the buyer's withholding obligation with the seller's tax obligation. The buyer is the party liable under 32 V.S.A. Section 5847 if the withholding is not remitted. When agents represent buyers in these transactions and fail to ensure the closing attorney handles the WHT-434 filing, the buyer — not the seller — faces penalties.

Fourth, some agents do not verify that the qualified intermediary is properly structured under both federal and Vermont requirements. Vermont does not license or regulate QIs separately, but if the QI is a disqualified person under IRC Section 1031(a)(2) — such as the seller's attorney or accountant — the entire exchange fails at both the federal and state level.

Fifth, agents occasionally advise clients to do a partial 1031 exchange without explaining the Vermont tax consequences on the boot. Any cash or non-like-kind property received in the exchange (the "boot") is taxable at both the federal and Vermont level. Vermont taxes this boot as ordinary income, and agents who gloss over that detail create unpleasant surprises at tax time.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents who handle 1031 exchange transactions in Vermont should build compliance checks into their transaction review process. This is not a suggestion — it is a risk management requirement under the Vermont Real Estate Commission's supervisory standards.

| Compliance Check | What the Broker Should Verify | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|
| WH-435 filed on time | Confirm filing date is 30+ days before closing | Closing delay or forced withholding |
| QI independence verified | Ensure QI is not a disqualified person | Entire exchange disqualified |
| WHT-434 filed post-closing | Confirm closing attorney remitted withholding or exemption | Buyer liability for penalties |
| Exchange timeline tracked | 45-day ID and 180-day completion deadlines documented | Exchange fails, full tax due |
| Boot disclosure documented | Written explanation of taxable boot provided to seller | Client complaint or E&O claim |

Brokers should require agents to include a 1031 exchange compliance checklist in every transaction file involving an exchange. This checklist should document the QI's identity, the WH-435 filing date, the closing attorney's confirmation of withholding handling, and copies of the 45-day identification notice. Without this documentation, the brokerage has no defense if a client later claims they were not informed of Vermont's withholding requirements.

The Vermont Real Estate Commission, housed under the Office of Professional Regulation, can discipline agents and brokers for failing to exercise reasonable care in transactions. While the Commission does not enforce tax law directly, an agent's failure to flag known withholding requirements could be characterized as a violation of the duty of competent service under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296. ## Liability Risks When Compliance Fails

The financial exposure for noncompliance is not theoretical. If the 2.5% withholding is not remitted on a nonresident sale, the Vermont Department of Taxes will assess the amount against the buyer, plus the late-filing penalty under 32 V.S.A. Section 3202 and interest at the statutory rate.

For the seller, a failed 1031 exchange means the full capital gain becomes taxable in the year of sale. Vermont's top marginal income tax rate is 8.75% under 32 V.S.A. Section 5822, applied to taxable income over $229,550 for single filers. On a $200,000 gain, that is $17,500 in Vermont income tax alone — money the seller expected to defer.

Agents face a different kind of liability. An errors and omissions claim from a client who lost their 1031 deferral because the agent failed to flag the WH-435 requirement is not far-fetched. E&O insurers are increasingly flagging 1031-related claims in their underwriting questionnaires, and agents without documented compliance steps may find their coverage challenged. This is exactly the kind of process gap that makes [relying on memory instead of checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) so dangerous in transaction management.
