## Understanding Maryland Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Maryland earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real consequences for agents and brokers who treat escrow compliance as an afterthought. The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under the Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing within the Maryland Department of Labor, enforces strict rules about how deposits are received, held, and disbursed. Every licensed agent in the state is bound by the Business Occupations and Professions Article, Title 17, of the Maryland Annotated Code, which governs trust account management and fiduciary obligations.

The stakes are not theoretical. MREC has the authority to suspend or revoke licenses, impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation, and refer cases for criminal prosecution when trust fund mishandling rises to the level of misappropriation. If you have been practicing for years without a deep reading of COMAR 09.11.02 (the Code of Maryland Regulations governing real estate escrow accounts), now is the time.

## Statutory Framework: Where the Rules Live

Maryland's escrow requirements originate in two primary sources. The Business Occupations and Professions Article, Title 17, Subtitle 5, establishes the statutory obligations for brokers holding client funds. COMAR 09.11.02 provides the detailed regulatory guidance on account maintenance, recordkeeping, and disbursement procedures.

**Brokers—not agents—bear the ultimate fiduciary responsibility for escrow accounts.** Associate brokers and salespersons act under the broker's license, which means escrow errors by a team member land squarely on the broker's desk. The distinction matters when MREC opens an investigation.

Maryland also requires that all trust accounts be held in federally insured institutions located within the state, unless an exception is granted. Interest-bearing escrow accounts are permitted, but the contract must specify who earns the interest and how it will be disbursed at settlement or termination. These details are typically addressed in the Maryland REALTORS residential contract of sale.

## Required Forms and Contract Provisions

The standard Maryland REALTORS Residential Contract of Sale (Form 1101) includes specific provisions for earnest money deposits. Paragraph 4 of that form addresses the deposit amount, the holder of the deposit, and the timeline for deposit. Agents must fill in the blanks accurately—leaving them empty or writing "TBD" creates enforcement problems if a dispute arises.

| Form/Document | Purpose | Who Completes It |
|---|---|---|
| Form 1101 (Residential Contract of Sale) | Specifies deposit amount, holder, and deposit deadline | Buyer's agent, reviewed by listing agent |
| Form 1107 (Release of Deposit) | Authorizes disbursement of earnest money | Both buyer and seller (or attorneys) |
| MREC Trust Account Records | Documents all deposits and disbursements | Broker or designated trust account manager |
| Escrow Agreement (attorney-held) | Governs terms when an attorney holds the funds | Settlement attorney |

Form 1107, the Release of Deposit form, is the mechanism for getting funds out of escrow when a deal falls apart. Without dual signatures—or a court order—the broker cannot unilaterally release disputed funds, regardless of which party the broker represents. Agents who promise their client a quick refund without the other party's consent are making commitments they cannot legally fulfill.

## Deposit Deadlines and Compliance Timelines

Maryland does not impose a single statutory deadline for depositing earnest money. Instead, the timeline is governed by the contract itself. The standard Form 1101 typically specifies that the deposit must be delivered to the holder within a set number of days after contract acceptance—commonly five to seven business days. However, COMAR 09.11.02.03 requires that once a broker receives the funds, the deposit must reach the trust account within seven business days.

**This creates a two-step clock that agents frequently misunderstand.** Step one is the buyer delivering the deposit to the holder (or the buyer's agent delivering it to the listing broker). Step two is the holder depositing those funds into the trust account. If an agent sits on a check for three days before handing it to the broker, and the broker then takes six more days to deposit it, the broker may be in violation even though the buyer met the contractual deadline.

The [escrow regulations in Washington State](/blog/washington-escrow-regulations-2026) impose different timeframes, but the underlying principle—that the clock starts upon receipt, not contract ratification—is consistent across jurisdictions. Maryland agents who also hold licenses in neighboring states need to track these differences carefully.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

MREC enforcement actions follow a predictable escalation pattern. Minor first-time recordkeeping violations may result in a letter of education or a consent order requiring continuing education in trust account management. Repeated violations, late deposits, or commingling of funds trigger formal charges that can result in license suspension, probation, or revocation.

Financial penalties under Title 17, Section 17-322 can reach $5,000 per violation. When misappropriation is proven—even if the agent intended to return the funds—MREC refers the case to the Maryland Attorney General's Office for potential criminal prosecution under theft statutes. A felony conviction for misappropriation carries prison time and permanent license revocation.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, and Maryland courts have awarded both compensatory and punitive damages in escrow mishandling cases. The [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/washington-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) that agents follow for incoming funds are equally relevant to protecting escrow integrity once the money is in the account.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Earnest Money in Maryland

The first mistake is treating the deposit as the buyer's agent's responsibility alone. In Maryland, once the listing broker's office is designated as the escrow holder on the contract, the listing broker has independent duties under COMAR to deposit and maintain those funds—regardless of what the buyer's agent does or fails to do. Blame-shifting does not impress MREC investigators.

The second mistake is accepting earnest money in cash without issuing a receipt. COMAR 09.11.02.05 requires written receipts for all trust account deposits. Cash deposits without documentation create audit failures that can unravel an otherwise clean brokerage record.

**The third mistake is releasing earnest money based on a verbal agreement between the parties.** Maryland law is explicit: release requires written authorization from all parties or a court order. An agent who releases funds because "the buyer said it was fine on the phone" faces personal liability and regulatory discipline.

The fourth mistake is failing to note the correct deposit holder on Form 1101. When agents write the brokerage name but the funds actually go to a title company, or vice versa, the mismatch creates confusion during disputes and potential breach of contract claims. Accuracy on the form is not optional—it is a binding contractual term.

The fifth mistake is assuming that a terminated contract automatically entitles the seller to the deposit. Maryland's default position is that disputed funds remain in escrow until both parties sign the Release of Deposit (Form 1107) or a court orders disbursement. Agents who tell their seller-client the money is "theirs now" before obtaining proper authorization are creating liability for themselves and their broker.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Maryland face a unique exposure because MREC holds them responsible for every dollar in their trust accounts, including funds deposited by associate licensees. Regular auditing is not optional—it is a regulatory expectation under [COMAR 09.11.02.06](https://dsd.maryland.gov/Pages/COMARHome.aspx), which requires monthly reconciliation of trust accounts.

**A broker's audit checklist should confirm three things every month: that all deposits match contract terms, that no funds have been held beyond their authorized purpose, and that the account balance reconciles to the penny with the ledger of individual client deposits.** Discrepancies must be investigated and resolved immediately—not at quarter's end.

Brokers should also audit their agents' use of Form 1107. If an agent obtained a release signature from one party but not the other before the broker disbursed funds, the broker shares liability. Training agents on this form—and requiring broker approval before any release—is a defensible practice that protects the entire office.

For brokerages managing high transaction volumes, the administrative burden of tracking deposit deadlines across dozens of active contracts is substantial. This is where systems built for [transaction coordination](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) become essential rather than optional. Missing a single seven-day deposit deadline because it fell through the cracks during a busy week is not a defense MREC accepts.

## Disputed Deposits and Interpleader Actions

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who receives the earnest money after a failed transaction, the broker is stuck. Maryland does not authorize brokers to make independent determinations about entitlement. The broker must hold the funds until receiving a signed Form 1107 from both parties or a court order.

If the dispute lingers, brokers can file an interpleader action in Circuit Court, depositing the funds with the court and asking a judge to determine entitlement. This protects the broker from claims by either party but involves legal fees. Some brokerages build interpleader cost provisions into their listing agreements or buyer representation agreements to address who bears that expense.

The practical takeaway is that agents should set realistic expectations with their clients from day one. Telling a buyer "you'll get your deposit back within a week if the inspection is bad" is risky unless the contract's contingency terms clearly support automatic release. Maryland courts look at the specific contract language, not general assumptions about how contingencies work.
