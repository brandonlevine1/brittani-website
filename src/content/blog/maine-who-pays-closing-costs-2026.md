## Understanding Maine Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of "Maine who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026" comes up in nearly every transaction, yet agents still get it wrong often enough to trigger complaints with the Maine Real Estate Commission. Maine is an attorney-closing state with specific statutory requirements that override general assumptions agents may carry from other markets. Getting the allocation right protects your license, your client's money, and the deal itself.

Maine's closing cost framework is governed by a combination of Title 33 (property law), Title 36 (taxation), and the rules of the Maine Real Estate Commission under 32 M.R.S. Chapter 114. Unlike states where title companies run the show, Maine closings require attorney supervision, which adds a layer of professional responsibility — and an additional fee line item that must be properly allocated between the parties.

## The Default Cost Split in Maine Transactions

**Maine does not have a single statute that dictates who pays which closing cost.** Instead, the allocation is driven by local custom, the purchase and sale agreement, and lender requirements. That said, established practice in Maine creates a baseline that most agents and attorneys follow unless the contract specifies otherwise.

The Maine Association of Realtors (MAR) Form 102 — the standard Purchase and Sale Agreement — includes Section 11 addressing closing costs and adjustments. This is where the allocation is memorialized. If an agent leaves this section incomplete or relies on verbal agreements about cost-splitting, the deal is vulnerable to disputes at the closing table.

Here is the customary breakdown that applies across most Maine counties:

| Cost Item | Typically Paid By Buyer | Typically Paid By Seller |
|-----------|:-----------------------:|:------------------------:|
| Owner's title insurance premium | Yes | — |
| Lender's title insurance | Yes | — |
| Recording fees (deed) | — | Yes |
| Recording fees (mortgage) | Yes | — |
| Transfer tax ($2.20/$500) | Split equally | Split equally |
| Attorney fees (buyer's counsel) | Yes | — |
| Attorney fees (seller's counsel) | — | Yes |
| Septic/well inspections | Negotiable | Negotiable |

This table reflects custom, not law. Every item except the transfer tax default can be shifted by contract language. The transfer tax under [36 M.R.S. § 4641-A](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/36/title36sec4641-A.html) defaults to equal split but can also be contractually reassigned.

## Transfer Tax: The One Cost With a Statutory Default

Maine's real estate transfer tax is $2.20 per $500 of the sale price, which works out to $4.40 per $1,000. The statute presumes an equal split — $1.10 per $500 for each party. On a $400,000 home, that is $3,520 total, or $1,760 per side.

There are exemptions under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C, including transfers between spouses, transfers to or from governmental entities, and certain foreclosure-related conveyances. Agents who fail to identify applicable exemptions cost their clients money and create unnecessary friction at closing.

**The transfer tax is due at the time of recording, and the county registry will not record the deed without it.** If the parties dispute who owes what and the closing attorney cannot resolve it from the contract language, the deal stalls. This is why precision in MAR Form 102, Section 11 matters.

## Attorney Fees and Maine's Closing Requirement

Maine is one of a handful of states requiring attorney involvement at closing. The [Maine Board of Overseers of the Bar](https://www.mebaroverseers.org/) has issued opinions confirming that title examination, closing document preparation, and disbursement of funds constitute the practice of law in Maine. A non-attorney closing agent conducting these activities would be engaged in unauthorized practice.

For agents, this means two separate attorney fee line items appear on most settlement statements: one for the buyer's attorney (who also typically handles the title search and certification) and one for the seller's attorney (who prepares the deed and handles payoff coordination). Each party pays their own attorney unless the purchase and sale agreement states otherwise.

Buyer's attorney fees in Maine typically range from $800 to $1,500 for a standard residential transaction, including title work. Seller's attorney fees tend to run $400 to $800. These figures vary by county and complexity, but agents should prepare clients for these ranges during the listing or buyer consultation — not at the closing table.

## Seller Concessions and Lender Limits

Seller concessions — where the seller agrees to pay some of the buyer's closing costs — are common in Maine and explicitly addressed in MAR Form 102. The concession amount is written into the agreement, often as a dollar cap or a percentage of the sale price. Agents must understand that lender guidelines impose caps on seller concessions that override whatever the parties agree to.

For conventional loans, Fannie Mae caps seller concessions at 3% for buyers putting down less than 10%, 6% for 10-25% down, and 9% for 25% or more down. FHA loans cap at 6%, and VA loans cap at 4% in concessions (though VA allows additional seller-paid items outside that cap). If an agent writes a concession that exceeds the lender's limit, the deal must be renegotiated or the buyer brings additional funds to closing.

**Writing a seller concession without verifying the buyer's loan program and down payment percentage is one of the most common — and most preventable — agent errors in Maine.** The concession language should reference the lender's maximum to protect against last-minute renegotiation.

## Common Mistakes Maine Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

The first error is leaving MAR Form 102, Section 11 blank or using vague language like "per local custom." Local custom is not uniform across Maine's sixteen counties, and what applies in Cumberland County may not apply in Aroostook. Vague language invites disputes and gives the closing attorney no contractual basis to allocate contested items.

The second mistake involves failing to disclose the transfer tax split in the preliminary estimate provided to clients. Under 32 M.R.S. § 13197, agents have an obligation not to make material misrepresentations. Omitting a $1,760 cost from a buyer's or seller's estimated net sheet qualifies. The Maine Real Estate Commission has disciplined agents for providing materially inaccurate cost estimates that caused harm at closing.

Third, agents sometimes promise seller concessions verbally during negotiations without documenting them on the purchase and sale agreement or an addendum. Verbal promises about who pays what are unenforceable and create liability exposure. If a buyer expects a $5,000 seller credit that never made it into the contract, the agent who made the promise faces a complaint.

Fourth, some listing agents fail to account for municipal charges that appear on seller settlement statements — items like unpaid property taxes, sewer liens, or outstanding code violation fees. These are seller-side costs that reduce net proceeds, and agents who provide seller net sheets without running a preliminary title search leave their clients exposed to surprises. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising Maine agents have specific obligations under 32 M.R.S. § 13176 and Commission Rule Chapter 520 regarding transaction oversight. The broker's duty extends to ensuring that purchase and sale agreements are complete, that cost allocations are documented, and that agents are not making representations about closing costs that the broker has not reviewed.

**Every broker should audit MAR Form 102 submissions for completeness of Section 11 before the agreement goes to the closing attorney.** A missing or ambiguous cost allocation is a compliance gap that invites post-closing complaints. Brokers should also verify that estimated net sheets and buyer cost estimates match what is documented in the contract.

Brokers must also enforce the requirement that agents provide the Maine Real Estate Commission's Consumer Guide (required under Commission Rule Chapter 570) at first substantive contact. This guide includes information about closing costs, and failure to provide it is a citable violation. The penalty framework under 32 M.R.S. § 13197-A allows fines up to $5,000 per violation and license suspension for pattern violations.

Transaction file reviews should specifically check for documented seller concession amounts that align with lender guidelines, transfer tax allocation language, and attorney fee responsibility assignments. ## Liability Exposure When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of mishandling closing cost allocation in Maine range from deal cancellation to disciplinary action. If a buyer arrives at closing and discovers $3,000 in unexpected costs because their agent omitted or misrepresented fees, the buyer can refuse to close. The seller then has grounds to retain the earnest money deposit under the contract's default provisions, and both agents face potential complaints.

The Maine Real Estate Commission investigates complaints under 32 M.R.S. § 13196 and can impose consent agreements, mandatory continuing education, fines, license suspension, or revocation. The Commission's public disciplinary records show multiple cases where agents faced sanctions for providing inaccurate closing cost information. Civil liability is also on the table — agents can be sued for negligent misrepresentation if a client suffers financial harm due to incorrect cost estimates.

For agents who want to reduce exposure, the answer is documentation discipline. Use the MAR forms as designed, fill in every applicable section, and never rely on assumptions about who pays what. If you struggle with [tracking deadlines and form completeness](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), build a system that catches gaps before they become complaints. The difference between a smooth closing and a Commission investigation often comes down to one incomplete line on a form.

The question of Maine who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is ultimately a contract question, not a statutory one (with the narrow exception of transfer tax defaults).
