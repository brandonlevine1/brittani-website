## Montana 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: The Ground Rules

Montana 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 demand attention from every agent handling investment property transactions in the state. The federal tax deferral under IRC Section 1031 does not automatically exempt your client from Montana's own withholding and reporting obligations. Agents who assume federal conformity covers all bases expose their clients—and themselves—to liability that can unwind a deal or trigger Department of Revenue enforcement actions.

Montana generally conforms to the federal 1031 like-kind exchange framework, meaning that if a transaction qualifies for deferral under IRC 1031, Montana will also defer the state capital gains tax. But conformity is not the same as immunity. The state imposes separate withholding requirements on nonresident sellers, and those requirements create a procedural minefield at the closing table.

**The Montana Department of Revenue (DOR) treats incomplete or improperly filed withholding exemptions as non-exempt transactions, triggering the full 2.5% withholding regardless of the seller's intent to complete a 1031 exchange.** That means a paperwork failure at closing can strip thousands of dollars from your seller's proceeds and delay the exchange timeline beyond the 45-day identification window.

## Montana's Nonresident Withholding Requirement Under MCA 15-30-1558

The statute that governs this entire process is Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Section 15-30-1558. It requires that any transferee (buyer) of Montana real property from a nonresident seller must withhold 2.5% of the gross sales price at closing and remit it to the DOR. This applies unless a valid exemption is established.

For 1031 exchanges, the exemption hinges on the seller—or their qualified intermediary (QI)—filing the appropriate documentation before or at closing. The DOR's Form RW-1 (Exemption from Withholding on Sales of Real Property by Nonresidents) is the critical filing. Without it, the closing agent must withhold.

Resident sellers are not subject to the 2.5% withholding. However, agents should not assume residency status without verification. A Montana-licensed agent who closes a transaction without confirming residency or securing the RW-1 is helping create a potential deficiency notice for their buyer client.

## How Federal 1031 Conformity Works (and Where It Breaks Down)

Montana's conformity with IRC 1031 is codified through its general adoption of the Internal Revenue Code for individual and corporate income tax purposes under [MCA 15-30-2101](https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0150/chapter_0300/part_0210/section_0010/0150-0300-0210-0010.html). The state does not have a separate 1031 exchange statute—it piggybacks on federal qualification.

This means that if the IRS later determines a transaction did not meet 1031 requirements—perhaps because the 180-day exchange period lapsed or the replacement property was not truly like-kind—the Montana deferral also fails. Your client then owes Montana capital gains tax at rates up to 6.75% for tax year 2026, plus interest from the original filing date.

| Scenario | Federal Treatment | Montana Treatment | Agent Action Required |
|----------|------------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Valid 1031, resident seller | Deferred | Deferred | No withholding needed |
| Valid 1031, nonresident seller | Deferred | Deferred, but RW-1 required | Ensure RW-1 filed at closing |
| Failed 1031, resident seller | Taxable gain | Taxable gain | Seller files amended return |
| Failed 1031, nonresident seller | Taxable gain | Taxable gain + withholding deficiency | Buyer may owe 2.5% to DOR |
| Partial 1031 (boot received) | Boot taxed federally | Boot taxed by Montana | Withholding may apply on boot portion |

That last row catches many agents off guard. When a seller receives boot—cash or non-like-kind property in excess of the exchange amount—Montana taxes that boot even though the rest of the exchange qualifies for deferral. The withholding obligation on the boot portion is not always collected at closing, creating future liability.

## Montana 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: Forms and Filing Deadlines

The primary forms agents and their clients encounter in Montana 1031 exchange transactions are straightforward, but the deadlines are unforgiving.

**Form RW-1 must be completed and delivered to the closing agent before the close of escrow.** If it arrives late, the closing agent is legally obligated to withhold. There is no grace period, and a retroactive filing does not cure the withholding obligation once the transaction has funded.

| Form | Purpose | Who Files | Deadline |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|
| Form RW-1 | Exemption from nonresident withholding | Seller or QI | Before closing |
| Form IT-1 (with Schedule D) | Montana individual income tax return reporting gain | Seller | April 15 following tax year |
| Form CLT-4 | Corporate income tax (if entity seller) | Entity seller | April 15 or fiscal year end |
| Federal Form 8824 | Like-kind exchange report | Seller | Filed with federal return |

Agents should also be aware that the Montana DOR cross-references Form 8824 filings with state returns. If a seller reports a 1031 exchange federally but does not file a Montana return—common with nonresidents who believe no tax is owed—the DOR may issue a notice of deficiency based solely on the information mismatch.

The [similar withholding rules in Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026) provide a useful comparison for agents working across state lines, though Montana's 2.5% rate and RW-1 form are state-specific.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Montana 1031 Transactions

**First: Assuming the qualified intermediary handles all state obligations.** A QI's primary role is to hold exchange funds and maintain the exchange structure under federal rules. Many QIs do not file Montana Form RW-1 unless specifically instructed by the seller or the seller's tax advisor. Agents who tell their clients "the QI will take care of everything" are creating a gap that results in withholding at closing.

Second: Confusing FIRPTA withholding with Montana withholding. These are entirely separate obligations. A foreign seller triggers both FIRPTA (federal, 15% withholding) and Montana's 2.5% nonresident withholding. A domestic nonresident—say, a California investor selling a Bozeman rental property—triggers only Montana's withholding. Agents who conflate the two often miss one or the other.

Third: Failing to verify residency status early in the listing process. Montana defines residency for tax purposes under MCA 15-30-2104. An individual who maintains a permanent home in Montana and spends more than 180 days in the state is generally a resident. Agents who wait until the week of closing to discover their seller is a nonresident leave insufficient time to coordinate the RW-1 filing.

Fourth: Not flagging boot scenarios during negotiation. If an exchange will produce boot—because the replacement property costs less than the relinquished property, or the seller takes cash out—the agent needs to communicate this to the closing agent and the seller's CPA before closing. Failing to account for partial withholding on the boot portion creates a DOR deficiency notice months later.

Fifth: Ignoring the 45-day and 180-day federal deadlines as a state concern. Because Montana's deferral depends on federal qualification, a missed identification deadline (day 45) or exchange deadline (day 180) retroactively converts the Montana treatment from deferred to taxable. Agents should document these deadlines in their transaction files and confirm the QI is tracking them.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, Liability, and Deal Collapse

The DOR's enforcement mechanism is blunt. If the 2.5% withholding is not collected at closing, the buyer becomes personally liable for the amount plus penalties under MCA 15-30-1558(3). Penalties can include a 10% late payment penalty and interest at the statutory rate (currently around 6% annually). For a $500,000 sale, that is $12,500 in withholding plus $1,250 in penalties—assessed against your buyer.

Agents face indirect consequences. A buyer who receives a DOR deficiency notice months after closing will often pursue their agent for failing to flag the withholding requirement. While agents are not closing agents and do not bear direct statutory withholding liability, professional negligence claims based on failure to disclose known obligations are viable in Montana courts.

Deals can also collapse in real-time when these issues surface. A title company that discovers at the closing table that no RW-1 has been filed will refuse to disburse without withholding. If the seller's exchange funds are suddenly reduced by 2.5%, the seller may not have sufficient proceeds to acquire the replacement property within the exchange parameters. The exchange fails, and the seller faces full capital gains taxation—federal and state.
