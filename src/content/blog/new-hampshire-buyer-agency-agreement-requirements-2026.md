## Understanding New Hampshire Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The New Hampshire buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 affect every licensee who works with purchasers in the state. Following the NAR settlement changes that took effect in August 2024, New Hampshire agents face a regulatory environment where written buyer representation is no longer optional or informal. The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC) has reinforced these obligations through its administrative rules and guidance memos issued in late 2024 and early 2025.

**If you are showing property, advising on offers, or performing any substantive brokerage service for a buyer, you must hold a signed agreement first.** This is not a soft recommendation. It is an enforceable requirement tied to your license, your commission eligibility, and your brokerage's compliance standing.

The governing statute remains RSA 331-A, the New Hampshire Real Estate Practice Act, which grants NHREC the authority to discipline licensees who fail to document agency relationships properly. Combined with [NHREC Administrative Rule Rea 701](https://www.oplc.nh.gov/real-estate-commission), the framework is clear: written agreements define the relationship, and agents who skip this step are exposed.

## The Forms You Need to Know

New Hampshire does not mandate a single state-issued form for buyer agency agreements. However, the New Hampshire Association of Realtors (NHAR) provides standardized forms that most brokerages adopt. The primary document is NHAR Form 2508, the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement. This form covers the term of representation, scope of services, compensation structure, and termination provisions.

For agents working on a non-exclusive basis, Form 2509 offers an alternative that allows the buyer to engage multiple agents simultaneously. There is also a property-specific or touring agreement—sometimes referred to informally as a "one-showing agreement"—that some brokerages use for initial contact before a full exclusive engagement.

| Form | Name | Use Case | Exclusivity |
|------|------|----------|-------------|
| NHAR 2508 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Full-service buyer representation | Exclusive |
| NHAR 2509 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Buyer working with multiple agents | Non-exclusive |
| Brokerage-specific | Touring/Property-Specific Agreement | Single showing or open house follow-up | Limited/single property |
| NHAR 2501 | Agency Disclosure | Must accompany any agency agreement | Disclosure only |

Every buyer agency agreement must be accompanied by the agency disclosure form (NHAR Form 2501), which explains the types of agency relationships available in New Hampshire. Agents are required under Rea 701.01 to provide this disclosure at the earliest substantive contact. Failing to pair the disclosure with the agreement is one of the most common audit findings brokers report.

## What Must the Agreement Contain

NHREC does not publish a rigid checklist of required clauses, but the administrative rules and NAR settlement terms together create a clear minimum. The agreement must state the duration of the representation, including a specific start and end date. Open-ended agreements with no termination date violate both NAR policy and NHREC expectations.

**Compensation must be clearly disclosed and cannot be open-ended or ambiguous.** The agreement must specify how the buyer agent will be paid—whether through a seller concession, direct buyer payment, or a combination. Since cooperating compensation is no longer guaranteed through MLS, this section has become the most scrutinized clause in any buyer agreement.

The scope of services must be defined. Agents cannot sign a buyer to a full exclusive agreement and then provide only limited services without disclosing the limitation. The agreement should also state the geographic area or property types covered, and it must include provisions for how disputes are handled and how either party can terminate the relationship.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

Agents who fail to secure a written buyer agency agreement before providing substantive services face multiple layers of risk. NHREC can initiate disciplinary proceedings under RSA 331-A:28, which authorizes fines up to $2,000 per violation, license suspension, and in repeat cases, revocation. The commission has publicly stated that buyer agency compliance is an enforcement priority through 2026.

Beyond regulatory penalties, there are transactional consequences. A buyer who never signed an agreement can dispute the agent's entitlement to compensation after closing. Without a written contract, the agent has no enforceable claim—even if they performed months of work. New Hampshire courts consistently hold that oral agency agreements for real estate are unenforceable under the state's statute of frauds principles.

**Civil liability is the third layer.** If a buyer alleges they did not understand the agent was representing them—or believed the agent was a neutral party—the agent and brokerage face potential negligence or misrepresentation claims. Errors and omissions insurance may not cover claims arising from undocumented agency relationships, depending on the policy terms.

| Compliance Failure | Potential Consequence | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| No written agreement before services | Fine up to $2,000, license suspension | RSA 331-A:28, NHREC |
| Missing agency disclosure form | Disciplinary action, deal vulnerability | Rea 701.01 |
| No compensation clause or ambiguous terms | Loss of commission, buyer dispute | Contract law, NAR policy |
| Agreement lacks termination date | Non-compliant under NAR settlement | NAR settlement terms |
| Failure to provide copy to buyer | Audit finding, disciplinary risk | Rea 701 |

The practical risk is compounded when deals fall apart. A disgruntled buyer who walks away from a transaction will often scrutinize the paperwork for an exit. If the buyer agency agreement is missing, incomplete, or was signed after services began, the buyer's attorney will find it.

## Common Mistakes New Hampshire Agents Make

Three to five errors appear repeatedly in NHREC complaint files and brokerage audits. Recognizing these patterns is the fastest way to tighten your practice.

The first mistake is treating the agreement as a formality to be signed "whenever the buyer is ready." Under current rules, substantive services cannot precede the agreement. Showing a home, running a CMA for a specific property, or advising on offer strategy all qualify as substantive services. Agents who wait until the buyer is emotionally committed to a home before presenting the agreement are operating without a safety net during the most active phase of their work.

The second error is using outdated forms. NHAR updated its buyer agency forms in late 2024 to conform with settlement requirements. Agents still circulating pre-settlement versions—those without explicit compensation disclosures or definite termination dates—are using documents that do not meet current standards. Your brokerage should have purged old versions from its document management system by now. If it has not, that is itself a compliance failure.

The third common mistake is omitting or poorly explaining the compensation section. Many agents gloss over this section during the signing conversation because it feels awkward to discuss money with a buyer upfront. But vague language like "compensation to be determined" or blank fields create unenforceable provisions. The amount or method must be definite—whether it is a flat fee, a percentage, an hourly rate, or a specific dollar amount.

A fourth error involves dual agency scenarios. When a buyer under an exclusive agreement becomes interested in the listing agent's own property, agents sometimes forget to execute the dual agency disclosure and obtain written consent before proceeding. In New Hampshire, dual agency is permitted under RSA 331-A:25-d but only with informed written consent from both parties. Proceeding without it can void the transaction and trigger commission complaints.

The fifth mistake is failing to provide the buyer with a signed copy of the agreement. This sounds basic, but NHREC auditors routinely find files where the brokerage retained the original and never delivered a copy to the client. [Agents who miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on administrative tasks like this create unnecessary exposure.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:16 for the conduct of their affiliated agents. A single agent's failure to secure proper buyer agency agreements can result in brokerage-level discipline if the broker cannot demonstrate adequate supervision and systems.

**Every brokerage should audit transaction files for three specific elements.** First, confirm that a signed buyer agency agreement exists in every file where the brokerage represented a buyer. Second, verify that the agreement was executed on or before the date of first substantive service—typically the first showing or the first property-specific advice. Third, confirm that a corresponding agency disclosure (Form 2501) is present and dated at or before the agreement.

Brokers should also review compensation clauses quarterly. With cooperating compensation no longer standardized through MLS, individual agents may be drafting their own language or modifying form fields in ways that create ambiguity. Standardizing compensation language across the brokerage—while still allowing for deal-specific negotiation—reduces risk and makes auditing faster.

Training is not optional. Brokers who hold quarterly compliance sessions and document attendance have a stronger defense if a single agent's failure triggers a commission complaint. [Understanding why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) applies directly here—systematic file review catches gaps before they become complaints.

Finally, brokers should implement a policy requiring agents to upload signed buyer agency agreements to the brokerage's transaction management platform before scheduling any showing or submitting any offer. This creates an automatic gatekeeping function. If the document is not in the system, the showing does not happen.

## How New Hampshire Differs From Neighboring States

New Hampshire's approach to buyer agency shares DNA with other New England states but has distinct characteristics worth noting. Unlike Massachusetts, which relies heavily on facilitator relationships, New Hampshire's statute explicitly contemplates exclusive buyer agency as a standard practice. The state's dual agency rules are also more permissive than Vermont's, where dual agency is effectively prohibited.

Compared to [Wisconsin's buyer agency agreement requirements](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026), New Hampshire provides less prescriptive statutory language about what must appear in the agreement. Wisconsin's administrative code specifies mandatory provisions in greater detail, while New Hampshire relies more on NHREC interpretive guidance and NHAR form standardization. This means New Hampshire agents bear more responsibility for ensuring their agreements are substantively complete, because the statute alone will not save a poorly drafted document.

The NAR settlement terms apply uniformly, but state-level enforcement intensity varies. NHREC has signaled through its 2025 enforcement bulletins that buyer agency compliance is a top-three audit focus alongside trust account management and advertising disclosures.

## Practical Steps for 2026 Compliance

Start every buyer interaction with a clear conversation about representation. Before discussing properties, school districts, or market conditions in a substantive way, present the agency disclosure form and the buyer agency agreement. Frame it as a professional standard, not a sales tactic.

Keep a supply of current NHAR forms—both exclusive (2508) and non-exclusive (2509)—accessible on your phone, tablet, and laptop. Digital signature platforms make it possible to execute agreements in a parking lot before walking into an open house. There is no legitimate reason to delay.

Document the date and time of execution. If a buyer later claims services were provided before the agreement was signed, your timestamped digital signature record is your primary defense. Pair it with a brief CRM note confirming the context of the signing.
