## Understanding South Carolina Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of South Carolina who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not purely academic. It dictates how you draft offers, advise clients on net proceeds, and stay within the guardrails the South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) expects you to respect. Getting it wrong doesn't just embarrass your brokerage — it can trigger license discipline under S.C. Code Section 40-57-145 and expose you to civil claims for negligent misrepresentation.

South Carolina is an attorney-closing state. That single fact reshapes the cost allocation picture more than most agents appreciate when they move here from a title-company state. The closing attorney's fee, title examination, and document preparation all flow through a licensed South Carolina attorney, and who bears those costs depends on contract language, local custom, and sometimes lender requirements that override both.

**This article breaks down the default allocation, the forms that control it, the mistakes that sink agents, and the audit points brokers should enforce before every file reaches the closing table.**

## The Default Cost Split: What Custom Dictates When the Contract Is Silent

South Carolina contract law follows the principle that custom fills gaps where the written agreement is silent. In the Lowcountry, Upstate, and Midlands markets, certain defaults have hardened over decades of practice. They are not statutes — they are trade customs — but courts treat them as implied contract terms when the parties fail to specify otherwise.

Sellers in South Carolina customarily pay for deed preparation, the deed recording fee, the owner's title insurance premium, the real estate transfer tax (commonly called "deed stamps"), and the closing attorney's fee associated with the seller's side of the transaction. Buyers customarily pay for the lender's title insurance policy, the buyer's share of the closing attorney's fee, survey costs, and all lender-related charges including the appraisal, credit report, and origination fees.

| Cost Item | Customary Payer | Controlling Reference |
|-----------|----------------|----------------------|
| Deed preparation | Seller | SCR Form 310, Para. 8 |
| Transfer tax (deed stamps) | Seller | S.C. Code 12-24-10 |
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | Local custom; SCR Form 310 |
| Closing attorney fee (seller side) | Seller | Attorney engagement letter |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Survey | Buyer | SCR Form 310, Para. 8 |
| Recording of mortgage | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Appraisal and credit report | Buyer | TRID / lender fees |

These defaults shift the moment the parties put pen to paper on a different arrangement using South Carolina REALTORS (SCR) Form 310 (Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate) or Form 331 (New Construction). Paragraph 8 of Form 310 is the battleground where cost allocation lives or dies.

## Key South Carolina Statutes and Forms That Control the Allocation

**S.C. Code Section 12-24-10 imposes the deed recording fee (transfer tax) at a rate of $1.85 per $500 of value on the seller, though the statute permits contractual reassignment.** Agents who tell buyers "you'll never pay deed stamps" are technically correct about custom but legally wrong about what's possible. If a seller negotiates that cost onto the buyer in writing, it stands.

S.C. Code Section 40-57-135 requires licensees to account for and disclose all costs material to a transaction. Failing to explain who is responsible for a cost — even when the agent believes custom makes it obvious — can constitute a violation of the licensee's duty under SCREC Regulation 105-1 (Standards of Practice).

The forms agents must know cold include SCR Form 310 (residential purchase agreement), SCR Form 320 (vacant land), SCR Form 390 (addendum to address additional terms), and SCR Form 325 (seller's property disclosure). Paragraph 8 of Form 310 specifically addresses closing costs, prorations, and the selection of the closing attorney. The 2025 revision to this paragraph added clarifying language about who pays for the owner's title policy when the buyer selects the closing attorney — a change that carries into 2026 transactions.

Agents should also reference the SCREC's [published guidelines on licensee responsibilities](https://llr.sc.gov/re/) when questions arise about disclosure obligations tied to cost allocation.

## Where Negotiations Get Tricky: Seller Concessions and Lender Limits

Seller concessions — where the seller agrees to pay a portion of the buyer's closing costs — remain one of the most misunderstood areas in South Carolina practice. Conventional loans cap seller concessions at 3% for buyers putting down less than 10%, 6% for 10-25% down, and 9% for 25% or more. FHA caps at 6%. VA caps at 4% plus allowable additional fees.

**Agents who draft a seller concession exceeding the lender's cap create a problem that surfaces at underwriting, not at contract execution, meaning weeks of wasted time.** The fix is simple: confirm the buyer's loan type and down payment percentage before drafting the concession figure into paragraph 8 or an SCR Form 390 addendum.

A related trap involves the [misunderstanding of how earnest money interacts with closing cost credits](/blog/where-deals-break-down). When a seller concession reduces the buyer's cash-to-close below what the lender needs for reserves, the deal can collapse at clear-to-close. South Carolina agents who track these numbers early — rather than assuming the lender will sort it out — save themselves a blown closing date and a furious client.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in SCREC complaint files and brokerage audit findings. Each one is preventable.

First, agents fail to specify the closing attorney in the contract. Paragraph 8 of SCR Form 310 asks who selects and pays the closing attorney. Leaving this blank does not default to custom — it creates ambiguity. SCREC has issued guidance that an incomplete contract provision is a supervision failure traceable to the licensee and the broker-in-charge.

Second, listing agents advertise "seller pays all closing costs" in MLS remarks without confirming the seller's actual authority or willingness to cover every line item. When the buyer's lender charges exceed what the seller anticipated, the listing agent faces a misrepresentation claim. The MLS statement becomes an inducement that may bind the seller under promissory estoppel principles if the buyer relied on it.

Third, buyer's agents neglect to disclose that the buyer is responsible for the lender's title insurance premium. Many first-time buyers confuse "owner's title insurance" (seller's cost) with "lender's title insurance" (buyer's cost). When the Closing Disclosure arrives showing a $1,200 charge the buyer claims nobody mentioned, the agent's [failure to set expectations becomes a liability event](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Fourth, agents mishandle prorations. South Carolina practice prorates property taxes to the day of closing, but the method — whether using the current year's tax bill or the prior year's — must be specified. SCR Form 310, paragraph 8(c) addresses this, yet agents routinely leave the checkbox unmarked, forcing the closing attorney to guess.

Fifth, dual agents fail to disclose the conflict inherent in advising both parties on who should pay disputed costs. Under S.C. Code Section 40-57-350 (dual agency disclosure), the agent must not advocate for one party's financial interest over the other. Recommending that "the seller should just pay the survey cost" while representing both sides is a textbook violation.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Broker-in-charge responsibility under SCREC Regulation 105-5 includes reviewing all contracts before or immediately after execution. For closing cost compliance, brokers should audit five specific checkpoints in every file.

Confirm that paragraph 8 of SCR Form 310 is fully completed — no blank checkboxes, no missing dollar amounts for seller concessions, and a named closing attorney with a clear fee-responsibility designation. Verify that any seller concession falls within the buyer's lender-imposed cap by cross-referencing the pre-approval letter on file. Check MLS remarks against contract terms to ensure no public-facing promise exceeds what the seller agreed to in writing.

**Brokers should also verify that the closing attorney's preliminary settlement statement matches the contract's cost allocation before the file moves to final review.** Discrepancies between the contract and the attorney's HUD-1 replacement (Closing Disclosure for TRID transactions or settlement statement for cash deals) must be flagged and corrected before closing day.

Finally, brokers must enforce documentation of all verbal agreements about cost-sharing. South Carolina's Statute of Frauds (S.C. Code Section 32-3-10) requires real estate agreements to be in writing. A verbal promise that "the seller will cover the termite letter cost" is unenforceable and exposes the brokerage to a complaint if the buyer relied on it. Every cost commitment must appear on a signed SCR form or addendum, and brokers who do not audit for this gap are co-liable under SCREC supervision rules.

| Broker Audit Point | Form/Reference | Risk if Missed |
|-------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Paragraph 8 completeness | SCR Form 310 | Contract ambiguity, SCREC complaint |
| Seller concession vs. lender cap | Pre-approval letter | Deal collapse at underwriting |
| MLS remarks vs. contract | MLS input sheet | Misrepresentation claim |
| Settlement statement vs. contract | Closing Disclosure | Closing delay, cost disputes |
| Verbal agreements documented | SCR Form 390 addendum | Statute of Frauds violation |
