## Understanding Connecticut Escrow Regulations 2026 and Why They Matter Now

Connecticut escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth, and the Connecticut Real Estate Commission (CREC) has signaled increased enforcement focus on deposit handling this year. If you hold a Connecticut license and touch residential transactions, your escrow practices are not optional compliance theater—they are a direct line to your license, your brokerage's viability, and your clients' trust. Agents who treat deposit handling as a mere administrative step routinely find themselves on the wrong end of disciplinary proceedings.

The statutory backbone for escrow regulation in Connecticut sits in **Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-324d through § 20-324f**, which govern deposit receipt, account maintenance, disbursement, and penalties. CREC enforces these statutes under the authority of the Department of Consumer Protection. Every agent should know these section numbers cold—especially § 20-324e, which outlines the specific acts that constitute grounds for license discipline.

Connecticut is an attorney-state for closings, but that does not relieve agents or brokers of escrow responsibility before the transaction reaches the closing table. The deposit phase is squarely in the brokerage's domain unless the purchase contract explicitly assigns escrow duties to an attorney or title company from the outset.

## The Statutory Framework: Key Sections Agents Must Know

**Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-324d mandates that any deposit received by a licensee in connection with a real estate transaction must be placed into an appropriate escrow account within two banking days.** The only exception is when the purchase contract specifies an alternative timeline—and even then, the contract language must be unambiguous. A handshake agreement to "hold the check for a few days" does not qualify.

Section 20-324e lists prohibited acts, including commingling client funds with the broker's personal or operating accounts, failing to maintain proper records, and disbursing escrow without authorization from all parties or a court order. Section 20-324f addresses the commission's enforcement powers, including fines, license suspension, and revocation.

The Connecticut Association of Realtors (CAR) publishes standardized forms that incorporate these statutory requirements. The **CAR Form RT-1 (Residential Purchase and Sales Agreement)** contains escrow deposit provisions in Section 2, specifying the amount, holder, and deposit timeline. Agents must also be familiar with **CAR Form RT-3 (Earnest Money Disbursement Authorization)**, which is the release document both parties sign when a transaction closes, terminates, or when a dispute arises.

| Statute Section | What It Governs | Key Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| § 20-324d | Deposit handling | Two banking days to deposit |
| § 20-324e | Prohibited acts | No commingling, no unauthorized disbursement |
| § 20-324f | Enforcement authority | Fines up to $25,000, license action |
| § 20-328a | Record retention | Three years minimum for escrow records |

## Deposit Timelines and the Two-Banking-Day Rule

The two-banking-day deposit rule trips up more Connecticut agents than any other single requirement. "Banking days" excludes weekends and federal holidays, so a deposit received on Friday afternoon must hit the escrow account by end of business Tuesday. Agents who receive deposits at open houses on Sundays still start the clock on Monday morning.

**The statutory language does not distinguish between personal checks, cashier's checks, or wire transfers—every form of deposit triggers the same two-day clock.** Wire transfers may arrive faster, but the obligation to initiate the deposit still falls on the agent or broker receiving the funds. Holding a check in a desk drawer "until the buyer's lender confirms approval" is a textbook violation.

One nuance worth noting: if the CAR Form RT-1 contract specifies that the deposit will be held by the buyer's attorney rather than the listing brokerage, the agent's obligation shifts to delivering the funds to that attorney within the stated timeframe. The agent cannot simply pocket the check and wait for instructions. Documentation of delivery—a signed receipt, email confirmation, or bank record—protects the agent if CREC later investigates.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Escrow in Connecticut

The first and most frequent error is **late deposit**. Agents receive earnest money at a showing or an offer presentation and forget to deliver it to the office before the two-day window closes. Even a single day late constitutes a violation, and CREC auditors check deposit dates against contract execution dates during routine brokerage audits.

The second mistake is improper disbursement during a dispute. When a deal falls apart and both buyer and seller claim the deposit, agents sometimes release funds to the party they believe is "right" rather than following the statutory process. Connecticut law requires either a signed CAR Form RT-3 from all parties or an [interpleader action](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_392.htm) filed with the Superior Court. Agents who pick sides expose themselves and their brokerage to personal liability.

The third error is poor record-keeping. CREC requires brokers to maintain escrow records for a minimum of three years under § 20-328a, and those records must include individual transaction ledgers, bank statements, and reconciliation reports. Agents who operate on teams sometimes assume "someone else is tracking this," which is exactly how discrepancies go unnoticed until an audit reveals a shortfall.

A fourth mistake—less obvious but increasingly common—is failing to update the escrow holder designation when contract amendments change the closing attorney or title company. If a deposit sits with Brokerage A but an amendment redirects closing to Attorney B, the deposit must physically move per the amended terms. Leaving it in limbo creates both a contractual breach and a regulatory exposure.

The fifth error involves interest-bearing accounts. Connecticut permits escrow deposits to be held in interest-bearing accounts, but only if the contract explicitly authorizes it and designates who receives the interest. Agents who default to interest-bearing accounts without contract language supporting that choice violate both fiduciary duty and CREC rules.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokers bear ultimate responsibility for escrow compliance at the firm level, and CREC holds the designated broker personally accountable for every deposit that passes through the brokerage.** This is not a delegable duty. A broker who assigns escrow management to an office manager or transaction coordinator still faces license action if violations occur.

Monthly reconciliation is the minimum standard. The broker or their authorized designee must compare the escrow account balance against the sum of all individual transaction ledgers, confirm that no outstanding checks have been unresolved for more than 30 days, and document the reconciliation in writing. Any discrepancy—even a few dollars attributable to bank fees—must be resolved immediately and noted in the file.

Brokers should also audit deposit timing on a rolling basis, cross-referencing the contract execution date (from the signed RT-1) against the bank deposit date. Automated [transaction coordination systems](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) can flag deposits that haven't been recorded within the statutory window, removing the human-error factor from tracking.

| Broker Obligation | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Account reconciliation | Monthly | Written reconciliation report |
| Deposit timing audit | Per transaction | Contract date vs. bank deposit date |
| Ledger maintenance | Ongoing | Individual transaction ledger entries |
| Record retention | 3+ years | Bank statements, ledgers, contracts |
| Interest authorization check | Per transaction | Contract clause or addendum |

Beyond internal audits, brokers should conduct annual training on escrow handling for every agent in the office. CREC's continuing education requirements do not mandate escrow-specific coursework every renewal cycle, so agents may go years without formal instruction. A 30-minute annual review of the firm's escrow procedures—covering deposit timing, the RT-3 disbursement form, and dispute protocols—prevents the kind of sloppy habits that trigger enforcement actions.

## Penalties and Liability: What Failure Actually Costs

CREC's penalty structure is graduated but severe. A first-time late deposit with no harm to the consumer may result in a letter of reprimand or a fine in the $1,000–$5,000 range. Commingling or unauthorized disbursement jumps to potential suspension, fines up to $25,000 per violation, and mandatory restitution. Repeated violations or large-dollar shortfalls can result in permanent license revocation and criminal referral to the State's Attorney.

**Civil liability runs parallel to regulatory action.** A buyer whose deposit is improperly disbursed can sue the agent, the broker, and the brokerage entity for conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, and violation of the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act (CUTPA) under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-110a. CUTPA claims carry the possibility of treble damages and attorney's fees, which means a $20,000 deposit dispute can quickly become a $60,000-plus judgment.

Deal cancellation is another consequence that agents underestimate. If a buyer discovers that their deposit was not properly handled—deposited late, held in the wrong account, or commingled—they may have grounds to void the contract entirely. The resulting fallout includes lost commission, potential claims from the seller for damages, and reputational harm that no amount of marketing can repair. Agents who think [missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) only matter on the inspection side have never faced an escrow-related rescission.

## Dispute Resolution and the Interpleader Process

When buyer and seller both claim entitlement to an escrow deposit and refuse to sign the CAR Form RT-3 release, the broker is stuck. Connecticut law does not authorize the broker to make a judgment call. The broker must either hold the funds indefinitely (which creates its own accounting headaches) or file an interpleader action in Superior Court under Practice Book § 10-41.

An interpleader deposits the disputed funds with the court and asks a judge to determine entitlement. The broker is typically released from liability once the funds are deposited, though the cost of filing—attorney's fees, court costs—comes out of the brokerage's pocket. Some brokerages build interpleader costs into their operating budget as a cost of doing business; others attempt to recover those costs from the parties, with mixed success.

The timeline for interpleader resolution varies by judicial district but typically runs three to six months. During that period, the deal is dead, the property may be tied up, and neither party has access to the funds. Agents who understand this reality are better positioned to [mediate disputes](/blog/where-deals-break-down) before they reach the interpleader stage.
