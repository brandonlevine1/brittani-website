The **Alabama commission & licensing rules 2026** cycle brings several enforcement priorities that agents and brokers cannot afford to ignore. The Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) has continued tightening audit procedures and updating its form library, making compliance a moving target even for experienced licensees. Understanding the statutory framework—and the specific paperwork that keeps you on the right side of it—separates a thriving practice from one staring down disciplinary action.

## How AREC Governs Licensing and Commission in Alabama

Alabama's real estate licensing authority operates under Title 34, Chapter 27 of the Code of Alabama. AREC administers examinations, issues and renews licenses, and enforces the Alabama Real Estate License Law. The commission also promulgates administrative rules found in Chapter 790-X of the Alabama Administrative Code, which detail everything from advertising standards to trust account management.

**Every dollar of commission earned by a salesperson must flow through their qualifying broker.** Alabama Code Section 34-27-36(a)(15) makes it a violation for any licensee to pay or receive compensation from anyone other than the broker under whom they are licensed. This single rule creates the backbone of commission compliance in the state.

AREC's nine-member board meets monthly, and disciplinary hearings are published on the [AREC official website](https://arec.alabama.gov). Agents who assume the rules haven't changed since they first got licensed in 2019 or 2020 often discover—through a complaint—that enforcement interpretations have shifted.

## Required Forms and Documentation for 2026

Alabama agents operate with a specific set of AREC-approved forms that must be used correctly to document commission agreements, agency relationships, and licensing status. The most relevant forms for commission and licensing compliance include:

| Form | Purpose | When Required |
|------|---------|---------------|
| AREC Form 1.0 | Salesperson License Application | Initial licensure |
| AREC Form 3.0 | Transfer of License | Broker-to-broker moves |
| AREC Form 4.0 | License Renewal | Every two years |
| AREC Form CE-1 | Continuing Education Certification | At renewal |
| AREC Form 7.0 | Broker Trust Account Registration | Account opening or change |

The Transfer of License form (Form 3.0) trips up more agents than you might expect. Both the releasing broker and the receiving broker must sign before AREC processes the transfer. An agent who begins conducting business under a new broker before that form clears AREC's system is technically practicing without proper licensure—a violation that can carry fines and retroactively invalidate any commission agreements signed during the gap.

For commission-related documentation, Alabama does not mandate a single statewide purchase agreement form the way some states do. However, AREC requires that any written buyer-broker agreement or listing agreement clearly state the compensation terms, the duration of the agreement, and the duties owed by the licensee. Failing to include these elements violates AREC Rule 790-X-3-.03.

## Commission Payment Rules Under Alabama Law

The commission payment chain in Alabama follows a strict hierarchy. A seller or buyer pays the brokerage. The brokerage—specifically the qualifying broker—then disburses the agent's share according to their independent contractor or employment agreement. There is no legal mechanism for a consumer to pay a salesperson directly.

This matters more than ever in 2026 given the post-settlement environment around buyer compensation. Alabama agents working with buyers under written agreements must ensure the agreement specifies how compensation will be sourced. If a listing broker offers cooperation compensation through MLS, it flows broker-to-broker. If the buyer is paying their agent directly, the payment still must be made to the brokerage entity, not the individual salesperson.

**Alabama Code Section 34-27-36(a)(6) also prohibits splitting fees with unlicensed persons.** Paying a referral fee to an out-of-state individual who does not hold an active license—or compensating a former agent whose license has lapsed—violates this statute. The penalty can reach $2,500 per occurrence plus license suspension.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance with Alabama's commission and licensing rules triggers a cascading set of consequences. AREC's enforcement arm can impose penalties ranging from a formal reprimand to permanent license revocation. The specific consequence depends on the severity and pattern of the violation.

For commission-related violations, AREC typically issues fines between $500 and $2,500 per occurrence under its administrative authority. Repeated offenses or violations involving consumer harm escalate to license suspension or revocation hearings. Beyond AREC's administrative actions, a licensee who earns a commission while improperly licensed has no legal standing to collect that fee—courts have consistently ruled that [unlicensed activity voids the right to compensation](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Deal cancellation is another real risk. If a buyer or seller discovers their agent was operating outside the scope of proper licensure during the transaction, they may have grounds to rescind the contract or pursue civil damages. Alabama courts have upheld rescission claims where a material misrepresentation about licensing status occurred.

Criminal liability is rare but not impossible. Under Alabama Code Section 34-27-80, practicing real estate without a license is a Class A misdemeanor punishable by up to one year in jail and a $6,000 fine. This statute primarily targets unlicensed individuals but has been applied to lapsed licensees who continued practicing.

## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Make

**First: operating during a license lapse.** Alabama licenses expire on September 30 of even-numbered years. Agents who miss the renewal deadline by even one day are technically unlicensed. AREC offers a 60-day late renewal window with a penalty fee, but any transactions conducted during a lapse are legally vulnerable. Agents have lost commission disputes in court solely because their license expired three days before they submitted an offer on behalf of a client.

Second: failing to update their license address within 30 days of a move. AREC Rule 790-X-1-.14 requires licensees to notify the commission of any change of address—business or residential—within 30 calendar days. This seems minor until AREC attempts to serve a complaint notice to an outdated address and the agent misses their response deadline by default.

Third: advertising without broker identification. Every advertisement—including social media posts, yard signs, and email signatures—must include the qualifying broker's name or the brokerage trade name as registered with AREC. An agent who runs Facebook ads featuring only their personal brand and team name, without mentioning their brokerage, violates AREC Rule 790-X-3-.02. Fines start at $500.

Fourth: co-mingling commission advances with trust account funds. Some brokerages issue commission advances from operational accounts, which is permissible. But when a broker deposits advance funds into the trust account—even temporarily—they create a co-mingling violation that triggers an automatic audit under AREC's trust account inspection program.

Fifth: [missing critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on continuing education. Alabama requires 15 hours of CE every two-year cycle, including a mandatory Risk Management course. Agents who complete generic online courses without verifying AREC approval discover at renewal that their hours don't count.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Qualifying brokers in Alabama carry personal liability for the actions of their affiliated agents. Under Alabama Code Section 34-27-36(a)(20), a broker who fails to supervise their agents adequately faces the same penalties as if they committed the violation themselves. This creates a powerful incentive to build audit routines.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|------------|-----------|---------------|
| License status of all agents | Monthly | Active status on AREC's online verification portal |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Bank statement vs. ledger, zero co-mingling |
| Advertising compliance | Quarterly | Social media, signs, websites for broker name inclusion |
| Commission disbursement records | Per transaction | Payment only to licensed entities, proper splits documented |
| Buyer-broker agreements | Per transaction | Required elements per AREC Rule 790-X-3-.03 |

**Brokers should pull a license verification report for every affiliated agent at least once per month.** AREC's online system allows bulk lookups, and catching a lapsed license before that agent writes an offer prevents a chain reaction of liability. A single transaction conducted by a lapsed agent can expose the brokerage to commission disgorgement claims, consumer lawsuits, and AREC disciplinary proceedings against the broker personally.

Trust account audits deserve particular attention. AREC's trust account inspector conducts random audits of brokerages each year, and violations—even inadvertent ones—trigger mandatory reporting. Brokers must maintain a trust account journal, individual ledger sheets for each deposit, and monthly reconciliation statements. The records must be retained for [three years minimum under AREC rules](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system).

Commission disbursement documentation should include a closing statement showing the gross commission, the brokerage's share, and the agent's share. Retaining copies of 1099-MISC forms issued to agents is not just a tax requirement—it's evidence of proper commission flow if AREC ever questions a payment.

## Alabama's 2026 Enforcement Priorities

AREC's 2025 annual report flagged three areas receiving increased scrutiny heading into 2026: buyer representation agreements, team advertising, and unlicensed assistant activity. These priorities reflect national trends, but AREC is applying them through Alabama's specific statutory framework.

On buyer representation, AREC now expects every agent working with a buyer to have a signed, written agreement before showing properties. The agreement must specify compensation—whether paid by the buyer, offered through MLS cooperation, or some combination. Agents who show homes without a signed agreement and then claim a commission are operating in a gray zone that AREC has signaled it will no longer tolerate.

Team advertising continues to generate complaints. Alabama permits teams to operate under a brokerage, but the team name cannot imply it is an independent entity. A team called "Smith Realty Group" that does not include the actual brokerage name in its advertising creates a deceptive impression under AREC Rule 790-X-3-.02.

Unlicensed assistant activity is the quiet violation that blows up loudly. An unlicensed assistant who negotiates contract terms, provides pricing opinions, or shows property to prospective buyers is engaged in activities that require licensure. Brokers who permit this—or turn a blind eye—face personal disciplinary action.
