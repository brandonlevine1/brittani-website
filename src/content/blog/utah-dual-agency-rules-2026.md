## Understanding Utah Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The rules governing dual agency and designated agency in Utah carry real consequences that most agents underestimate until they face a complaint. **Utah dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 remain governed primarily by Utah Code Title 61, Chapter 2f, which was last amended to tighten disclosure timing and consent documentation.** Agents who treat agency disclosure as a checkbox exercise rather than a legal obligation put their license, their brokerage, and their clients' transactions at risk.

Utah is a state that permits both limited dual agency and designated agency, but the statutory framework demands strict adherence to disclosure, consent, and documentation requirements. The [Utah Division of Real Estate](https://realestate.utah.gov/) under the Department of Commerce enforces these rules through complaint investigations, audits, and formal disciplinary proceedings. Getting this wrong is not a theoretical problem—it results in real fines, license actions, and deal cancellations every year.

## The Statutory Framework: Utah Code 61-2f-401 Through 61-2f-409

Utah's agency law lives in Title 61, Chapter 2f of the Utah Code, specifically sections 401 through 409. Section 401 establishes that a licensee owes duties based on the type of agency relationship disclosed and consented to by the parties. Section 402 defines the duties owed in each capacity—seller's agent, buyer's agent, or limited agent (Utah's statutory term for a dual agent).

**A limited agent in Utah owes both parties duties of honesty, reasonable care, disclosure of material facts, and confidentiality of certain information—but cannot advocate for one party's negotiating position over the other.** This is the critical distinction. The moment you become a limited agent, you lose the ability to advise either side on price negotiation strategy, and you cannot disclose one party's financial motivation or willingness to accept terms without that party's explicit permission.

Section 409 addresses designated agency (called "assigned agency" in some brokerage policies). Under this provision, a principal broker may assign different licensees within the same brokerage to represent the buyer and seller separately. Each assigned agent then owes full fiduciary duties to their respective client, and the principal broker functions as a neutral supervisor who does not share confidential information between the two sides.

## Required Forms and Disclosure Timing

Utah agents must work with specific forms that align with Division of Real Estate requirements. The primary documents are the Agency Disclosure form (provided at first substantive contact) and the Confirmation of Agency section within the Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC), which is Form 24 published by the Utah Association of Realtors.

| Document | When Required | Who Signs | Purpose |
|----------|--------------|-----------|---------|
| Agency Disclosure | First substantive contact | Prospective client acknowledges | Explains all possible agency relationships |
| Consent to Limited Agency | Before dual agency begins | Both buyer and seller | Confirms informed consent to limited representation |
| REPC Section 8 (Form 24) | At contract formation | Both parties | Confirms the actual agency relationship in the transaction |
| Brokerage Designated Agency Policy | On file before transaction | Agents and broker | Internal documentation of assignment |

Timing matters enormously. The Division of Real Estate has consistently held that "first substantive contact" means the first conversation where you discuss a specific property, the client's buying or selling needs, or anything beyond casual social interaction. Handing the disclosure form to a buyer at the showing of a home they want to write an offer on is almost certainly too late.

The consent to limited agency must be obtained before you begin acting in a dual capacity. You cannot retroactively obtain consent after you have already been advising both sides. [Understanding where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often traces back to exactly this kind of timing failure.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: A Side-by-Side Comparison

Many agents conflate these two concepts, but they create fundamentally different legal obligations and risk profiles.

| Factor | Limited Dual Agency | Designated (Assigned) Agency |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| Number of agents | One agent represents both parties | Two separate agents in the same brokerage |
| Fiduciary duties | Reduced—no advocacy or negotiation advice | Full fiduciary duties to respective clients |
| Consent required | Written consent from both parties | Disclosure to both parties; broker assignment |
| Confidentiality risk | High—single agent holds all information | Lower—information is siloed between agents |
| Broker's role | Supervises the limited agent | Acts as neutral party; manages information barriers |
| Liability exposure | Highest for the individual agent | Distributed between agents and broker |

**Designated agency significantly reduces the risk of inadvertent confidentiality breaches because each agent only possesses their own client's confidential information.** However, it requires the brokerage to have a written policy establishing how designated agency works, how information barriers are maintained, and how the principal broker will handle supervisory responsibilities without compromising either side.

## Common Mistakes That Get Utah Agents in Trouble

Five errors appear repeatedly in Division of Real Estate disciplinary actions related to agency disclosure. Each one is entirely preventable with proper systems and awareness.

The first and most frequent mistake is late disclosure. Agents meet a buyer at an open house, spend thirty minutes discussing properties, exchange contact information, and never provide the initial agency disclosure until days later when a showing is scheduled. By that point, the agent has already been functioning in an advisory capacity without any defined relationship, which violates Section 401's requirement.

The second mistake is assuming that dual agency consent carries over between transactions. An agent who previously obtained limited agency consent from a seller for one transaction cannot rely on that same consent when the seller lists a new property. Each transaction requires fresh, transaction-specific consent.

Third, agents fail to confirm agency in writing within the REPC itself. Even if a separate consent form was signed earlier, Section 8 of Form 24 must be completed accurately to reflect the actual agency relationship at the time the contract is formed. Leaving it blank or checking the wrong box creates a documentary conflict that can unravel the transaction.

Fourth, agents acting as limited agents continue to provide negotiation advice to one or both parties. The moment you tell a buyer "I think the seller will take $10,000 less" or tell a seller "This buyer is pre-approved for $50,000 more than their offer," you have breached your limited agency duties. These conversations happen casually, often over text, and they become damning evidence in disputes.

Fifth, brokerages operating under designated agency fail to maintain genuine information barriers. If both agents share an office, attend the same team meetings where client strategies are discussed, or have access to each other's transaction files in the CRM, the "designated" separation is illusory. The Division has taken action against brokerages where the barrier existed only on paper, as explored in the context of [teams and supervision structures](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) in other states.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The Division of Real Estate has enforcement authority under Utah Code 61-2f-503 to impose a range of penalties. These include civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension or revocation, mandatory continuing education, and probationary periods with enhanced supervision requirements.

Beyond regulatory penalties, an aggrieved party can seek rescission of the transaction itself. If a buyer or seller can demonstrate that they were not properly informed of the dual agency relationship or did not give valid consent, the contract may be voidable. This does not just mean the deal falls apart—it means the agent and brokerage face potential civil liability for damages, including the other party's lost opportunity costs, inspection fees, appraisal costs, and potentially attorney fees.

> "A licensee who acts as a limited agent without first obtaining the informed written consent of all parties to the transaction is subject to disciplinary action and civil liability." — Utah Code 61-2f-401(4)

Errors and omissions insurance may cover some exposure, but most E&O policies contain exclusions for knowing violations of statutory duties. If the Division determines that the agent knowingly failed to disclose, the carrier may deny coverage entirely.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry independent liability for agency compliance within their brokerage. The Division of Real Estate audits brokerages and expects to find documented policies, training records, and transaction file evidence of proper disclosure.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of closed transaction files specifically checking for three things: timely agency disclosure, properly executed consent forms, and accurate completion of REPC Section 8.** A single missing form in an audited file can trigger a broader investigation into the brokerage's compliance culture.

Brokerages that offer designated agency must maintain a written policy describing how agent assignments are made, how confidential information is segregated, and what the principal broker's supervisory role looks like in practice. This policy should be signed by every affiliated agent and updated annually. The Division expects to see this document during any routine audit or complaint investigation.

Brokers should also audit electronic systems. If your CRM allows any agent to view any transaction's notes, you do not have a functional information barrier for designated agency purposes. Access controls must restrict each agent to only their own assigned transactions. Similarly, shared team communication channels—Slack rooms, group texts, weekly pipeline meetings where strategy is discussed openly—can defeat the entire purpose of designated agency. Implementing [a structured pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that respects information barriers is one practical solution.

Training documentation matters as much as the training itself. The Division wants to see dated records showing that agents received instruction on agency duties, disclosure timing, and consent requirements. Annual training is the minimum; best practice is to address agency compliance in new agent onboarding and at least one additional session per year tied to any regulatory updates.
