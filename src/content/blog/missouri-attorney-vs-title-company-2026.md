## Missouri Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Definitive Guide for Agents

The question of Missouri attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up even experienced agents because the answer defies the binary framing. Missouri is neither a strict "attorney state" nor a pure "title company state," and that gray area creates real liability exposure for agents who guess wrong or overstep their role. Understanding the legal framework here is not optional — it protects your license, your brokerage's E&O policy, and your clients' deals.

Missouri sits in a category sometimes called "attorney-optional with title company primary," meaning a title company can handle settlement without a lawyer present, but certain legal functions still require attorney involvement behind the scenes. The Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC), the Department of Insurance, and the Missouri Bar Association each regulate different pieces of the closing puzzle, and agents need to know which authority governs what.

## What Missouri Law Actually Says About Closing Representation

**Missouri does not have a statute that explicitly mandates attorney attendance at residential closings.** Unlike states such as Georgia, South Carolina, or New York, there is no provision in the Missouri Revised Statutes requiring a licensed attorney to sit at the table when buyers and sellers sign documents. Title insurance companies licensed by the Missouri Department of Commerce and Insurance (DCI) can issue policies and conduct closings through their authorized agents.

However, Missouri's unauthorized practice of law statutes — RSMo Chapter 484, specifically Section 484.010 — define the boundaries of what title companies and their employees can do without crossing into legal practice. Preparing original legal documents, rendering opinions on title defects, and advising parties on their legal rights all require a licensed attorney. Title companies typically operate under standing arrangements with attorneys who review title commitments and approve document language.

The practical result: most closings in Missouri are conducted by title companies or their closing agents, but an attorney's work product is embedded in the process even when no lawyer is physically present. In the St. Louis metro area, attorney-conducted closings remain more common by local custom, while Kansas City and outstate Missouri lean heavily on title companies.

## The Regulatory Bodies Agents Must Know

Three distinct regulatory bodies govern different aspects of the closing process, and agents [who miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) or misunderstand roles often run afoul of more than one simultaneously.

| Regulatory Body | What They Govern | Key Rule for Agents |
|---|---|---|
| Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC) | Agent licensing, conduct, supervision | 20 CSR 2250-8.060 — prohibits agents from practicing law |
| Missouri Department of Commerce and Insurance (DCI) | Title company licensing, insurance rates | RSMo Chapter 381 — title insurance company requirements |
| The Missouri Bar / Supreme Court | Unauthorized practice of law | RSMo 484.010 — defines legal practice boundaries |
| CFPB / RESPA (Federal) | Settlement procedures, disclosures | 12 CFR 1024 — Closing Disclosure timing, anti-kickback |

MREC's administrative rules under 20 CSR 2250 explicitly state that licensees shall not draft legal instruments, give legal opinions, or advise clients on legal rights related to title. Agents who fill in deed blanks, explain lien priority to clients, or opine on easement issues at closing are practicing law without a license — a Class B misdemeanor under RSMo 484.020.

## How Closings Actually Work in Missouri: Step by Step

The standard Missouri residential closing uses the Missouri Association of Realtors (MAR) Form 100 — Residential Sale Contract, which designates the title company in Section 8 and addresses closing logistics. Agents should also be familiar with MAR Form 115 (Addendum to Contract) and MAR Form 140 (Disclosure of Affiliated Business Arrangement) when recommending settlement service providers.

**Once a contract is executed, the title company orders a title search, issues a commitment for title insurance (Commitment for Title Insurance — ALTA Commitment Form), and prepares the Closing Disclosure per TRID rules.** The closing agent — typically a title company employee or, in attorney-conducted closings, the attorney or their paralegal — handles document execution, fund disbursement, and recording with the county recorder of deeds.

Missouri follows a "deed state" model where the seller delivers a general warranty deed or special warranty deed at closing. The deed must meet the requirements of RSMo 442.130 (acknowledgment) and RSMo 59.330 (recording requirements). An agent who attempts to fill in deed language beyond inserting names and legal descriptions from the title commitment is crossing the line.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make at Closing

Mistake one: advising clients that they do not need an attorney review because "the title company handles everything." While technically a title company can conduct closing, some transactions — particularly those involving boundary disputes, estate sales, or commercial-to-residential conversions — require legal counsel. When agents make blanket assurances, they expose themselves to negligence claims if something goes wrong.

Mistake two: filling in legal document language beyond form fields. MAR contracts are designed with fill-in-the-blank formatting for a reason. Agents who draft custom addendum language addressing title defects, lien subordination, or easement modifications are practicing law. MREC has issued disciplinary actions for exactly this conduct under 20 CSR 2250-8.060, with penalties ranging from reprimand to license suspension.

**Mistake three: failing to disclose affiliated business arrangements.** Under RESPA Section 8(c) and Missouri DCI regulations, agents who refer clients to a title company in which they or their brokerage hold a financial interest must provide written AfBA disclosure at the time of referral — not at closing. The fine for RESPA violations can reach $10,000 per occurrence, and the deal itself may be voidable.

Mistake four: representing that a closing agent has authority they do not possess. Some title company closing agents are not licensed attorneys, and their scope is limited to executing pre-approved documents and collecting signatures. When an agent tells a client that "the closer can explain the deed language," they are potentially directing the client to receive unauthorized legal advice.

Mistake five: scheduling closings without confirming the title company's authority to close in the relevant county. Missouri has 114 counties plus the City of St. Louis, and some title agencies are only authorized to issue policies in certain jurisdictions. An agent who books closing with a company lacking proper county authority risks a last-minute scramble — or worse, an uninsured transaction.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under RSMo 339.150 and MREC's rules at 20 CSR 2250-4.010, which means an agent's closing-table misstep becomes the broker's problem. Periodic file audits should check for five specific compliance markers.

First, confirm every transaction file contains evidence that the title company or attorney selected holds proper licensure with Missouri DCI. Second, review any custom addenda or amendments for language that crosses into legal drafting — if an agent wrote it and it addresses title, liens, or easements, it needs attorney review retroactively. Third, verify that AfBA disclosures were provided at the time of referral and are signed by the consumer, not just generated at closing.

**Fourth, brokers should audit Closing Disclosure delivery timelines.** TRID requires the buyer receive the CD at least three business days before closing. While this is the lender's obligation, agents who pressure title companies to rush delivery create E&O exposure for the brokerage when timelines are missed and closings are contested. Fifth, ensure agents are not instructing clients on [wire transfer procedures](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) without directing them to the title company's verified instructions — wire fraud liability has hit Missouri brokerages hard in recent years.

## Attorney vs. Title Company: When to Recommend Each

| Scenario | Better Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Standard residential resale, clear title | Title company | Cost-effective, handles 90%+ of Missouri closings |
| Estate sale or probate transfer | Attorney-conducted | Fiduciary issues, court approval may be required |
| Boundary dispute or survey conflict | Attorney review | Title company cannot render legal opinions on disputes |
| New construction with mechanic's lien risk | Attorney review + title company | Lien waiver compliance under RSMo Chapter 429 |
| Commercial-to-residential conversion | Attorney-conducted | Zoning, use restrictions require legal analysis |
| FSBO with no agent on other side | Attorney recommended | Unrepresented party needs independent legal advice |

Agents should frame this recommendation carefully. Under MREC rules, you cannot tell a client they "need" or "don't need" an attorney — that itself is legal advice. Instead, present the option factually: "Missouri law does not require an attorney at closing, but given the complexity of this transaction, you may want to consult one." Document this conversation in your file.

## Liability Consequences for Non-Compliance

The penalties for overstepping at closing are not theoretical. MREC publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and unauthorized practice of law violations appear regularly. A first offense typically results in a formal reprimand and mandatory continuing education hours beyond the standard 12-hour requirement under [RSMo 339.511](https://www.sos.mo.gov/adrules/csr/current/20csr/20c2250-8.pdf). Repeat violations can result in 30-day to one-year license suspensions.

Beyond MREC discipline, agents face civil liability. If a client suffers financial harm because an agent drafted defective addendum language or misrepresented title status, the agent and brokerage can be sued for negligence and potentially fraud. E&O policies frequently exclude coverage for acts that constitute unauthorized practice of law, meaning the agent pays out of pocket.

Federal penalties add another layer. RESPA violations carry up to $10,000 per incident for referral fee arrangements and up to $100,000 in criminal penalties for pattern violations. The CFPB has enforcement authority and has pursued cases in Missouri involving title company kickback schemes tied to agent referral networks.
