## Understanding Hawaii Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC) governs roughly 22,000 active licensees across the islands, and its enforcement posture heading into 2026 leaves little room for sloppy paperwork or undisclosed commission arrangements. If you hold a Hawaii salesperson or broker license, the **Hawaii commission & licensing rules 2026** directly affect how you get paid, how you advertise, and how long you stay licensed. Ignoring these rules does not merely risk a slap on the wrist—it risks deal cancellations, personal liability, and license revocation.

Hawaii's regulatory framework sits primarily in Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 467 and Title 16, Chapter 99 of the Hawaii Administrative Rules (HAR). HIREC operates under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), and its [official licensing portal](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) is the authoritative source for forms, fee schedules, and disciplinary actions. The statutes are more prescriptive than many mainland states, particularly around commission disbursement and agency disclosure.

## How Commission Disbursement Actually Works

**All commissions flow through the principal broker—no exceptions.** HRS Section 467-14(8) explicitly prohibits a salesperson from receiving compensation from anyone other than the broker under whom the salesperson is licensed. This means a listing agent cannot accept a referral fee from an out-of-state brokerage directly, nor can a buyer's agent receive a bonus check from a builder without it routing through the PB.

The principal broker must maintain records of every commission disbursement for at least five years under HAR Section 16-99-3(i). These records must show the transaction address, the gross commission amount, the brokerage's share, and the split paid to the individual licensee. Any side arrangements—team splits, mentorship fees, or transaction fees charged to agents—must also appear in the broker's books.

Hawaii does not cap commission rates. Rates remain fully negotiable, and any printed or advertised "standard" commission percentage could trigger an antitrust complaint. The 2024 NAR settlement reinforced this reality nationwide, but Hawaii agents should note that HIREC already had language in HAR 16-99-3 discouraging rate-fixing discussions among competitors.

| Commission Rule | Hawaii Requirement | Consequence of Violation |
|---|---|---|
| Disbursement path | Must pass through principal broker (HRS 467-14) | License suspension, fine up to $2,500 per count |
| Written agreement | Commission must be agreed in writing before services rendered | Commission may be deemed unenforceable |
| Record retention | Broker retains records for 5 years (HAR 16-99-3) | Audit failure, potential license revocation |
| Advertising commission rates | Cannot advertise a "standard" rate | Antitrust liability, HIREC complaint |
| Referral fees | Must be disclosed and paid through PB | Unauthorized compensation violation |

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Deadlines

Hawaii licenses renew on a biennial cycle, with the current renewal period ending November 30, 2026 for those who last renewed in 2024. Salespersons must complete 20 hours of continuing education—10 core hours prescribed by HIREC and 10 elective hours from approved providers. Brokers face the same 20-hour requirement but with additional broker-specific core topics covering trust accounting and supervision.

**Late renewals trigger an automatic forfeiture if not cured within one year of expiration.** During forfeiture, the licensee cannot participate in any transaction, solicit clients, or collect commissions. Reinstatement after forfeiture requires retaking the state exam and submitting a new application, which can take 60 to 90 days.

Form changes for 2026 include the updated HIREC Real Estate Salesperson Application (Form REB-2) and the Broker Application (Form REB-3). HIREC also requires the Compliance Certificate (Form REB-7) when applying for license restoration after disciplinary action. Agents moving between brokerages must file Form REB-5 (Change of Status/Transfer) within 10 calendar days of the move—missing this window means practicing on an invalid registration.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Agents Their License

Three to five errors account for the majority of HIREC disciplinary hearings each year. These are not obscure technicalities; they are basic compliance failures that happen when agents get busy and cut corners.

**First: Collecting a commission directly from a cooperating broker.** This happens most often in dual-state referral situations where a mainland agent sends a check to the Hawaii agent personally. Even if the amount is correct and the arrangement is legitimate, bypassing the principal broker violates HRS 467-14(8). HIREC treats this as unauthorized compensation, which carries a fine and mandatory compliance course.

Second: Failing to disclose dual agency or a personal interest in time. Hawaii requires the Agency Disclosure form (REC-67) to be presented at the earliest practicable time—not at the offer stage, not at the inspection. Agents who wait too long give buyers or sellers grounds to rescind the contract under HRS 467-14(18), which covers misrepresentation by omission. This issue comes up frequently in [situations where deals fall apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down) late in escrow.

Third: Letting continuing education lapse and continuing to transact. HIREC conducts random audits of CE completion records, and the DCCA database flags licensees whose CE hours do not reconcile. An agent caught transacting on an expired license faces retroactive voiding of commission entitlements, which means returning money already earned.

Fourth: Advertising without the brokerage name. HAR 16-99-3(l) requires that all advertising—including social media posts, yard signs, and email signatures—display the legal name of the brokerage. An agent who runs Instagram ads with only a team name and no brokerage identifier risks a $1,000 fine per occurrence.

Fifth: Commingling earnest money deposits. While this overlaps with trust accounting rules, it appears in commission-related enforcement because agents sometimes hold checks "temporarily" rather than delivering them to the broker's trust account within the required timeline. The deadline under HAR 16-99-3(f) is the end of the next banking day after acceptance.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

The principal broker holds vicarious liability for every licensee under their supervision. HIREC does not treat ignorance of an agent's misconduct as a defense—HAR 16-99-3(h) imposes a duty of reasonable supervision that includes periodic audits of advertising, file completeness, and commission records.

Brokers should audit these items quarterly at minimum: confirmation that all agents hold active licenses (check the DCCA PVL database), verification that the REC-67 agency disclosure appears in every transaction file, reconciliation of commission ledger entries against closed escrow statements, and advertising samples from every agent and team. Missing even one of these can expose the brokerage to a consent order, public censure, or forced disgorgement of commissions.

**A broker who discovers a compliance gap must act immediately.** HIREC's enforcement guidelines consider self-reporting and prompt correction as mitigating factors during disciplinary hearings. Conversely, a broker who learns of a violation and fails to report or correct it may face independent charges under HRS 467-14(3) for "failure to maintain a level of competence sufficient to serve the public interest."

| Audit Area | Frequency | Governing Rule | Risk if Skipped |
|---|---|---|---|
| License status verification | Monthly | HRS 467-1.6 | Broker liable for unlicensed activity |
| Agency disclosure (REC-67) | Every file | HRS 467-14(18) | Contract rescission, fine |
| Commission ledger reconciliation | Quarterly | HAR 16-99-3(i) | Trust account violations |
| Advertising compliance | Quarterly | HAR 16-99-3(l) | $1,000 fine per occurrence |
| CE completion records | Annually | HAR 16-99-5 | Agent forfeiture, broker censure |

## The Interplay Between NAR Settlement Changes and Hawaii Rules

The 2024 NAR settlement eliminated cooperative compensation offers from MLS systems nationwide. In Hawaii, this means buyer's agent commissions must now be negotiated and documented separately from the listing agreement. The Hawaii Association of REALTORS updated its purchase contract forms in late 2024 to include a buyer-broker compensation addendum, and these forms remain the standard for 2026 transactions.

Agents must present their buyer-broker agreement (HBAR Form RR-310B) before showing any property. Failure to secure a signed agreement before touring a home creates exposure—not just under HIREC rules, but under the [deadline management protocols](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that keep transactions on track. Without a written agreement in place, the agent's entitlement to compensation is legally ambiguous if the buyer later disputes the fee.

For brokers, this shift means the old practice of simply reviewing the MLS co-op field to verify an agent's expected income no longer works. Each file must contain a standalone compensation agreement, and the broker should verify that the amount matches what appears on the closing disclosure.

## Penalties and Enforcement Trends for 2026

HIREC published 47 final disciplinary orders in 2024, up from 38 in 2023. The increase correlates with post-settlement confusion about compensation disclosures and a spike in advertising violations tied to social media. Fines ranged from $500 for minor paperwork lapses to $10,000 for repeated unauthorized practice.

The most severe penalty—license revocation—was imposed three times in 2024, each involving agents who collected commissions while their licenses were in forfeited status. HIREC also imposed two consent orders requiring full commission disgorgement, totaling over $87,000 returned to consumers. These cases should alarm any agent who treats renewal notices as low-priority mail.

Beyond HIREC enforcement, agents face civil liability. A buyer or seller who discovers an undisclosed commission arrangement can sue for damages under Hawaii's Unfair and Deceptive Acts Practices statute (HRS Chapter 480). Treble damages are available under that statute, meaning a $30,000 commission dispute can become a $90,000 judgment.
