## Understanding Delaware Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of **Delaware who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026** is not purely academic for licensed agents. It determines how you draft offers, how you advise clients during negotiations, and where your liability exposure sits if something goes wrong at the settlement table. Delaware's attorney-state framework, combined with a realty transfer tax that operates differently from most Mid-Atlantic states, creates a unique allocation structure that agents must master.

Delaware real estate transactions are governed primarily by Title 24, Chapter 29 of the Delaware Code (the Real Estate Licensing Act) and Title 30, Chapter 54 (the Realty Transfer Tax statute). The Delaware Real Estate Commission, housed under the Division of Professional Regulation, enforces agent conduct standards. Getting closing cost allocation wrong does not just embarrass you — it can trigger Commission complaints, E&O claims, and deal collapse.

## The Default Cost Split: What Delaware Custom Dictates

**Delaware does not have a statute that mandates which party pays each specific closing cost, with one major exception: the realty transfer tax.** Everything else falls to contract negotiation and local custom. That said, custom in Delaware is remarkably consistent across New Castle, Kent, and Sussex counties.

Here is how costs typically break down in a standard residential transaction:

| Cost Item | Typically Paid By | Statutory Basis |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Realty Transfer Tax (4% total) | Split 50/50 buyer/seller | 30 Del. C. § 5401 |
| Owner's Title Insurance | Buyer | Contract negotiation |
| Lender's Title Insurance | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Settlement Attorney Fee | Split or buyer | Custom varies by county |
| Recording Fees | Buyer (deed), Seller (release) | 9 Del. C. § 9605 |
| Real Estate Commission | Seller | Listing agreement |
| Property Survey | Buyer | Contract negotiation |

The realty transfer tax deserves its own discussion. Under [30 Del. C. § 5401](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title30/c054/index.html), the tax is imposed on the "transfer" itself — not on either party specifically. The rate is 4% of the sale price (combining the state's 2.5% and county portions of 1.5%). Custom dictates a 50/50 split, but the statute makes both parties jointly and severally liable for the full amount if the other party defaults.

## Key Forms and Where Cost Allocation Lives

Agents in Delaware primarily work with the Delaware Association of REALTORS (DAR) standard forms. The Agreement of Sale (DAR Form 35) is where closing cost allocation is memorialized. Paragraph 11 of this form addresses settlement costs, and Paragraph 12 covers the realty transfer tax split specifically.

**If you leave Paragraph 12 blank or use ambiguous language, you have created a dispute waiting to happen.** The DAR Seller's Estimated Net Proceeds worksheet and the Buyer's Estimated Costs worksheet should align with whatever allocation the Agreement of Sale specifies. Inconsistencies between these documents and the final HUD-1 replacement (the TRID Closing Disclosure) generate last-minute objections that delay or kill closings.

The DAR Addendum of Sale (Form 36) is where agents typically document any negotiated deviations from the default split. When a seller agrees to pay a buyer's share of the transfer tax or contribute toward the buyer's title insurance, it must appear in writing on Form 36 or within the body of Form 35's additional terms section. Verbal agreements about cost allocation are unenforceable under [6 Del. C. § 2714](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title6/c027/index.html), Delaware's Statute of Frauds.

## Attorney Involvement and Its Impact on Cost Allocation

Delaware is an attorney-state. The Delaware Supreme Court's rulings on unauthorized practice of law mean that a non-attorney cannot conduct a real estate settlement. This requirement directly affects closing costs because the settlement attorney's fee becomes a fixed line item that someone must pay.

In New Castle County, the buyer's attorney typically handles the closing and the buyer pays that fee. In Sussex County, it is more common for the seller to have separate counsel and for each party to pay their own attorney. Kent County falls somewhere in between. None of this is codified — it is practice-driven — but agents who fail to set expectations early create friction at closing.

The attorney also handles the disbursement of funds, title examination, and preparation of the deed. When agents promise clients that "closing costs will be around X" without confirming the attorney's fee schedule, they set themselves up for credibility problems. Attorney fees in Delaware residential transactions typically range from $800 to $2,500 depending on complexity, but some firms charge significantly more for transactions involving estates, trusts, or entity sellers.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The Delaware Real Estate Commission has enforcement authority under 24 Del. C. § 2912. Misrepresenting closing costs — whether to a buyer or seller — constitutes a violation that can result in license suspension, fines of up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory continuing education, or probation. The Commission publishes disciplinary actions quarterly.

Beyond regulatory exposure, agents face civil liability. If a buyer walks away from a deal because closing costs at the table exceeded what the agent represented, the seller may pursue damages against the buyer's agent for tortious interference or negligent misrepresentation. E&O carriers in Delaware report that closing cost disputes rank among the top five claim categories for residential agents.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. Under DAR Form 35, if the Closing Disclosure shows costs materially different from what was represented or agreed upon, either party may have grounds to terminate depending on the contingency language. The three-day review period required by TRID does not give agents much runway to fix a cost allocation error discovered at the last minute.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make

**First, agents routinely forget that the realty transfer tax creates joint and several liability.** When a buyer cannot fund their half of the 4% tax at closing, the seller does not simply get to walk away. The state can pursue either party. Agents who fail to explain this exposure to sellers during listing presentations leave their clients vulnerable.

Second, agents misquote the transfer tax rate. First-time homebuyers in Delaware may qualify for a partial exemption under 30 Del. C. § 5410, reducing the state portion from 2.5% to 0.5% on the first $400,000 of value. Agents who do not verify buyer eligibility for this exemption either overstate buyer costs (losing deals) or promise the exemption without confirming qualification (creating settlement day surprises). The buyer must occupy the property as a primary residence and must not have owned residential property in the prior three years.

Third, agents neglect to address who pays for the municipal transfer tax in Wilmington. The City of Wilmington imposes an additional 1.5% transfer tax on top of the state and county taxes, bringing the total to 5.5% within city limits. Agents listing or selling in Wilmington who quote the standard 4% rate are making a material misrepresentation.

Fourth, agents fail to distinguish between seller concessions (credits toward buyer closing costs) and actual cost responsibility shifts. A seller credit of $8,000 toward buyer closing costs is not the same as the seller agreeing to pay specific line items. Lenders treat these differently for qualification purposes, and agents who conflate them create underwriting complications that [delay transactions past critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Fifth, agents assume that "customary" equals "required." No buyer in Delaware is legally obligated to pay for their own title insurance simply because custom says so. Everything is negotiable unless a statute says otherwise. Agents who tell clients "that's just how it's done" without explaining the negotiability of each cost item are providing incomplete representation.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising Delaware agents should implement file reviews specifically targeting closing cost representations. Every transaction file should contain a copy of the signed Closing Disclosure alongside the original Agreement of Sale to verify that the cost allocation in the contract matches what actually happened at settlement.

**Brokers should confirm that agents are using current DAR forms.** Outdated form versions may not include updated transfer tax language or may reference superseded statutory provisions. The DAR updates forms periodically, and agents using a 2023 version of Form 35 in a 2026 transaction risk enforceability challenges.

Audit the estimated cost worksheets agents provide to clients. Under 24 Del. C. § 2912(a)(1), making "any substantial misrepresentation" is grounds for discipline. A broker who discovers that an agent routinely underestimates buyer closing costs by 20% or more has an affirmative obligation to correct the behavior. Failure to supervise is itself a violation under 24 Del. C. § 2913, exposing the broker's license.

Brokers should also verify that agents clearly document any seller concession or cost-sharing arrangement in the addendum or additional terms section — never in side communications or verbal agreements. The [transaction coordination process](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) should include a compliance checkpoint specifically for closing cost documentation before files reach the settlement attorney.

Review how agents handle the first-time homebuyer transfer tax exemption. If an agent represented to the seller that the buyer qualified for the exemption (thus reducing the seller's expected contribution), but the buyer ultimately did not qualify, the resulting cost shift at closing can torpedo the deal. Brokers should require verification of exemption eligibility before the Agreement of Sale is ratified.

## Buyer vs. Seller: A Negotiation Framework for 2026

The 2026 market in Delaware reflects post-NAR settlement realities. Buyer agent compensation is no longer presumed, which means closing cost negotiations have become more complex. Some buyers are now asking sellers to cover their agent's fee as a closing cost, which blurs the traditional allocation framework.

| Negotiation Lever | Buyer Advantage | Seller Advantage |
|-------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Transfer Tax Split | Request seller pay 75-100% | Hold firm at 50/50 in multiple-offer situations |
| Attorney Fees | Ask seller to cover buyer's attorney | Offer to split evenly to speed deal |
| Title Insurance | Request seller-paid owner's policy | Standard is buyer-paid; hold firm |
| Seller Concession Cap | Push toward lender max (typically 3-6%) | Offer concession but reduce sale price |
| First-Time Buyer Exemption | Reduces buyer cost; less need for concessions | Exemption lowers total tax burden on deal |

Agents who understand these levers write stronger offers and counter-offers. The key is documenting every allocation decision in writing on the appropriate DAR form. When agents rely on [checklists and systematic processes](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rather than memory alone, fewer cost allocation errors reach the settlement table.
