# Alaska Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: What Every Agent Needs to Know

The question of Alaska who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is one that surfaces in nearly every transaction, yet agents still get tripped up by its details. Alaska's real estate market operates under a unique combination of state statutes, local customs, and lender requirements that make closing cost allocation anything but automatic. Getting this wrong does not just frustrate clients — it can blow up deals, trigger regulatory complaints, and expose your brokerage to real liability.

This article breaks down the specific cost allocations, the forms that govern them, the statutes that create agent obligations, and the mistakes that keep showing up in Alaska Real Estate Commission (AREC) disciplinary records.

## How Alaska Law Frames Closing Cost Allocation

**Alaska does not have a statute that mandates which party pays specific closing costs.** Unlike states with prescribed transfer tax splits or mandatory seller-paid title insurance, Alaska leaves almost everything to negotiation between the parties. The governing framework sits primarily in Alaska Statute Title 34 (Property), Title 08 Chapter 88 (Real Estate Licensing), and the rules adopted by the [Alaska Real Estate Commission](https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/cbpl/ProfessionalLicensing/RealEstateCommission.aspx) under 12 AAC 64.

What this means for agents is straightforward: the purchase agreement is the single document that determines who pays what. If it is silent on a particular cost, that cost becomes a source of dispute at the closing table. Alaska's standard residential purchase agreement — the Alaska Association of Realtors (AAR) Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement — contains specific line items for allocating title insurance, escrow fees, survey costs, and inspections, but agents must actually fill them in correctly.

The absence of a statutory default also means that local custom varies significantly. In the Anchorage market, sellers customarily pay for the owner's title insurance policy and half the escrow fee. In Fairbanks or Juneau, practices can differ. Custom is not law, and agents who rely on "the way it's always done" without documenting it in the contract are inviting problems.

## The Forms That Control the Deal

Agents working with AAR forms need to pay close attention to the specific sections that address closing cost allocation. The AAR Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement (Form No. 2026-PSA, most recently revised for 2026) includes a dedicated section — typically Section 6 — covering closing costs, title insurance, and escrow instructions.

**Section 6 of the PSA is where most errors originate.** Agents who leave blanks, check conflicting boxes, or fail to specify dollar amounts for seller concessions create ambiguity that title companies and escrow officers cannot resolve on their own. When a blank field reaches the closing table, the escrow officer will flag it, and the parties will have to execute an addendum — burning time and goodwill.

Beyond the PSA, agents should be familiar with the AAR Seller's Disclosure Statement (Form No. 2026-SDS), which while primarily focused on property condition, can interact with closing cost negotiations when undisclosed defects trigger repair credits or cost-shifting requests. The AAR Addendum to Purchase and Sale Agreement (Form No. 2026-ADD) is the proper vehicle for any mid-transaction changes to the cost allocation originally agreed upon.

For transactions involving FHA, VA, or USDA financing, agents must also account for lender-specific restrictions on what sellers can contribute. VA loans, for instance, limit seller concessions to 4% of the purchase price and define which costs the buyer is prohibited from paying — a constraint that overrides whatever the purchase agreement says.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

Alaska Statute 08.88.401 establishes grounds for disciplinary action against licensees, including negligence, incompetence, and failure to account for money belonging to others. An agent who misrepresents closing cost obligations — whether to a buyer, seller, or both — risks a complaint to AREC that can result in license suspension, fines up to $25,000 per violation under AS 08.88.401(a), or mandatory education requirements.

The more common consequence is deal cancellation. When a buyer arrives at closing expecting the seller to cover the escrow fee and the contract says otherwise, the deal stalls. If the transaction falls apart over a cost allocation error that the agent created or failed to catch, the agent's errors and omissions insurance may face a claim. Brokerages operating under 12 AAC 64.110, which governs supervision of licensees, may also face commission-level scrutiny if the error reflects a pattern.

**Misrepresenting who pays a particular cost in a competitive offer scenario is an especially dangerous practice.** Some agents verbally promise seller concessions or cost coverage that never makes it into the written agreement. Under Alaska's statute of frauds (AS 09.25.010), real estate agreements must be in writing to be enforceable. A verbal promise about closing costs is worth nothing at the closing table, and the agent who made it is exposed.

## Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make on Closing Costs

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in AREC complaints and title company rejection logs. Here are the most damaging.

**First, agents leave Section 6 of the PSA incomplete.** They fill in the purchase price and earnest money but skip the closing cost allocation fields, assuming "standard practice" will fill the gaps. It will not. Escrow officers are not authorized to allocate costs based on local custom when the contract is silent.

Second, agents miscalculate or misstate seller concession limits for government-backed loans. Writing a 6% seller concession into a VA contract violates lender guidelines and will be flagged during underwriting, forcing a contract amendment or killing the deal entirely. Agents must know the concession caps: 3% for conventional loans with less than 10% down, 6% for conventional with 10-25% down, 9% for conventional with 25%+ down, 6% for FHA, 4% for VA, and 6% for USDA.

Third, agents fail to address prorated property taxes correctly. Alaska's property tax calendar varies by borough — the Matanuska-Susitna Borough, Fairbanks North Star Borough, and Municipality of Anchorage all have different assessment and billing cycles. Agents who do not confirm the proration method (calendar year vs. fiscal year, per diem vs. monthly) with the escrow officer create discrepancies that delay closing.

Fourth, agents neglect to update closing cost terms when the transaction changes. A price reduction, a repair credit, or a closing date extension can all affect the math. If the original PSA allocated a specific dollar amount for seller concessions and the purchase price drops, the percentage may now exceed lender limits. Using the AAR Addendum (Form No. 2026-ADD) to memorialize every change is not optional — it is a compliance requirement.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under 12 AAC 64.110 and AS 08.88.401. A broker who fails to review closing cost allocations on their agents' transactions is not just risking bad deals — they are risking their own license.

| Audit Item | What Brokers Should Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Section 6 completion on PSA | All blanks filled, no conflicts | Every transaction |
| Seller concession limits | Matches loan type guidelines | Every financed transaction |
| Addendum usage for changes | Form 2026-ADD executed for cost changes | As needed per transaction |
| Proration calculations | Confirmed with escrow officer | Pre-closing review |
| Written vs. verbal promises | No undocumented cost commitments | Agent file review quarterly |

**Brokers should implement a pre-closing file review that specifically flags incomplete closing cost sections.** This single step eliminates the majority of closing-day disputes and AREC complaints tied to cost allocation. A quarterly audit of agent files — checking for unsigned addenda, blank PSA fields, and concession math errors — is the minimum standard of care most E&O carriers expect.
