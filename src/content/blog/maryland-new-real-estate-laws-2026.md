## Maryland New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Shake-Up

The Maryland General Assembly passed more than a dozen bills affecting residential real estate transactions during its 2026 session, and several carry enforcement teeth that agents have never faced before. Understanding **Maryland new real estate laws 2026** is not optional — it is the difference between a clean closing and a Commission complaint. Agents who treated the post-NAR settlement period as a one-time adjustment are about to learn that Annapolis had its own ideas.

Most of the changes take effect October 1, 2026, which gives brokerages roughly two months to update their forms, retrain their agents, and audit existing listings. The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) published its updated guidance bulletin in May, and the Maryland Association of Realtors revised five standard forms in response. If your office has not held a compliance meeting yet, this article is your crash course.

## Buyer Representation Agreements Get a Statutory Mandate

**House Bill 307 amends Maryland Real Property Article Section 17-532 to require a written buyer representation agreement before an agent provides "substantive real estate assistance."** That phrase is defined broadly: scheduling a showing, providing a CMA, or discussing offer strategy all qualify. The agreement must include a fixed compensation amount or a maximum percentage, a defined term not exceeding 12 months, and a clear termination clause.

Maryland's version differs from what other states adopted. The agreement must reference the specific MREC-approved form — currently designated as Form 1045 — and the agent must provide a copy of the "Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent" pamphlet (Form 1001) simultaneously. The dual delivery requirement is new; previously, Form 1001 could be delivered at any first "scheduled meeting."

Agents working open houses face a carve-out, but it is narrow. You may answer general questions about a listed property without triggering the agreement requirement. The moment you discuss comparable sales, suggest an offer price, or agree to show additional properties, you have crossed the statutory threshold.

## Updated Disclosure Forms and Delivery Timelines

The Residential Property Disclosure and Disclaimer Statement — known informally as the seller disclosure — received its first major revision since 2019. The updated form, designated as **MAR Form 1035-R (Revised 06/2026)**, adds three new sections: previous insurance claims related to water intrusion, knowledge of PFAS contamination within one mile, and flood zone reclassification history. Sellers who use the disclaimer option (electing not to disclose) must still complete the PFAS and flood sections.

Delivery timelines also shifted. Under the prior rule, the seller had to provide the disclosure "on or before" contract ratification. Senate Bill 104 now requires delivery no later than five calendar days before the buyer's inspection contingency deadline. If the disclosure arrives late or is materially amended, the buyer gains an independent five-day rescission right that runs from the date of actual receipt.

| Requirement | Prior Rule (Pre-Oct 2026) | New Rule (Effective Oct 1, 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery deadline | On or before ratification | 5 calendar days before inspection deadline |
| PFAS disclosure | Not required | Required even with disclaimer election |
| Flood reclassification | Not required | Required for all sellers |
| Buyer rescission right | Limited to material misrepresentation | 5-day independent right upon late delivery |
| Form version | MAR 1035 (2019) | MAR 1035-R (06/2026) |

Agents listing properties should build the new disclosure into their onboarding workflow from day one. Waiting until an offer arrives creates timing pressure that leads to exactly the kind of late-delivery scenario the statute now penalizes. If you are looking at how other states handle [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026), the Maryland approach is notably more aggressive on environmental factors.

## Transfer Tax Surcharge and Recording Fee Increases

**HB 412 introduces a tiered transfer tax surcharge on residential transactions with a sales price exceeding $1 million.** The surcharge is 0.25% on the portion above $1 million, collected at settlement and remitted to the Maryland Department of Assessments and Taxation. The standard state transfer tax of 0.5% and county transfer taxes remain unchanged.

Recording fees also increased. The Maryland Judiciary raised the per-page recording fee from $20 to $25 effective July 1, 2026, and added a $50 flat "electronic submission surcharge" for all documents filed through the Maryland Electronic Courts (MDEC) system. For a typical transaction with 40-60 recorded pages, that adds $250 to $350 in closing costs that many buyer's agents are not yet quoting in their estimates.

Settlement agents will handle the math, but listing agents owe their sellers accurate net sheets. Using a 2025 fee schedule on a 2026 listing is a fast way to erode client trust — and to hand a competing agent an easy objection at a listing presentation.

## Wire Fraud Prevention Becomes a Licensed Agent's Obligation

Senate Bill 219 — the Maryland Secure Settlements Act — does something unusual: it extends wire fraud prevention responsibility to licensed agents, not just settlement companies. Starting October 1, both the listing agent and the buyer's agent must independently verify wire instructions through a secondary communication channel (phone call to a known number, in-person confirmation, or encrypted portal verification) and retain documentation of that verification for three years.

Failure to comply does not automatically create civil liability for stolen funds, but it does create a presumption of negligence that plaintiffs' attorneys can use in subsequent litigation. MREC also designated wire fraud verification failure as a ground for disciplinary action under COMAR 09.11.01.17. Agents who want to understand how [wire instructions verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) works in practice should study their settlement company's specific protocols and build parallel documentation.

The three-year retention requirement means your transaction files need a dedicated section — physical or digital — for wire verification records. This is not a "set it and forget it" compliance item; MREC auditors have signaled they will request these records during routine brokerage audits beginning in Q1 2027.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

Every legislative cycle produces the same pattern: agents learn the new rules in theory, then botch them in practice. Here are the five errors most likely to generate complaints or deal failures under the 2026 changes.

**First, agents will use the old Form 1035 instead of Form 1035-R.** The prior version lacks the PFAS and flood reclassification sections. A disclosure delivered on the superseded form does not satisfy the statute, which means the buyer's rescission clock never starts running — leaving the deal perpetually vulnerable to cancellation.

Second, buyer's agents will show property before securing a signed Form 1045. The "open house exception" will be read too broadly. If you pull up a second listing on your phone during an open house conversation and say "I can get you in tomorrow," you have crossed the line. The Commission's May 2026 bulletin includes that exact example.

Third, agents will forget the dual-delivery requirement for Form 1001 and Form 1045. Delivering the buyer agreement without the representation pamphlet is a technical violation. MREC treats it as a disclosure failure, not a paperwork technicality.

Fourth, listing agents will continue using 2025 net sheet calculations that omit the transfer tax surcharge on properties above $1 million. This creates a material misrepresentation of expected net proceeds, which can unwind a deal at the settlement table when the seller refuses to sign.

Fifth, agents will treat the wire verification requirement as the settlement company's problem. SB 219 is explicit: the obligation runs to the licensee individually. Delegating it without documentation is not a defense.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Maryland Real Property Article Section 17-322, which means every compliance failure by an agent is potentially a brokerage-level violation. The 2026 changes demand a structured audit approach, not a memo and a prayer.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required | Consequence of Failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form 1045 signed before showing | Every buyer transaction | Timestamped copy in file | MREC complaint, possible deal rescission |
| Form 1035-R (correct version) | Every listing | Executed copy with delivery confirmation | Buyer rescission right, disciplinary action |
| Wire verification records | Every transaction | Agent's verification log, 3-year retention | Negligence presumption, MREC discipline |
| Transfer tax surcharge on net sheets | Listings above $1M | Revised net sheet with surcharge line item | Client trust breach, potential misrepresentation claim |
| Form 1001 dual delivery | Every buyer transaction | Signed receipt or electronic confirmation | Disclosure violation |

Brokers should conduct a full file audit of any transaction opened after October 1 within 30 days of closing. Waiting for MREC to audit you is reactive; running your own quarterly reviews is the only defensible supervisory posture. The [supervision framework used in other states](/blog/washington-teams-supervision-rules-2026) provides a useful model for brokerages building their own audit calendars.

MREC's enforcement division added two full-time investigators in 2026, funded specifically by the recording fee increases. Expect complaint investigations to move faster and produce results more quickly than in prior years.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Beyond a Slap on the Wrist

**The 2026 amendments increase maximum administrative fines from $5,000 to $15,000 per violation for individual licensees and $25,000 per violation for brokerages.** These are not theoretical numbers; MREC imposed fines on 47 licensees in FY2025, and the Commission's chair publicly stated that the higher caps will be used "early and visibly" to establish precedent.

Beyond fines, the buyer rescission rights embedded in the new disclosure timing rules create a practical nightmare. A buyer who receives a late or deficient Form 1035-R can walk away from a ratified contract without forfeiting their earnest money deposit, even after the inspection period has passed. For sellers, that means weeks of market time lost and the stigma of a "back on market" status.

Civil liability exposure also grows under SB 219. If a buyer loses funds to wire fraud and the agent cannot produce verification documentation, plaintiffs' counsel will argue negligence per se — a violation of a statute designed to protect a specific class of persons. Maryland courts have historically been receptive to that theory, and the statutory documentation requirement makes the evidentiary burden significantly lighter for plaintiffs.

The [Maryland Real Estate Commission's public disciplinary database](https://www.dllr.state.md.us/license/mrec/) already shows a spike in disclosure-related complaints filed in anticipation of the October 1 effective date. Agents should treat the next two months as preparation, not grace period.

Confirm with your broker that your office has a 2026-compliant audit protocol in writing.
