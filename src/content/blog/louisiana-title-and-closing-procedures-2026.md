## Understanding Louisiana Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Louisiana's civil law tradition makes its real estate closing process unlike any other state in the country. Agents licensed here must understand that Louisiana title & closing procedures 2026 operate under a Napoleonic Code framework where the "act of sale" replaces what most states call a closing or settlement. Misunderstanding these distinctions does not just embarrass you at the table — it exposes you to liability under both the Louisiana Real Estate License Law (La. R.S. 37:1430-1470) and the state's unauthorized practice of law statutes.

The Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC) enforces agent conduct through its administrative rules found in LAC 46:LXVII. The commission has repeatedly sanctioned agents who overstep their role during the title and closing phase. Your job is to facilitate the transaction, not to practice law, and that line is thinner than most agents realize.

## The Attorney Requirement and Why It Matters

**Louisiana is an attorney-closing state, full stop.** Unlike states where title companies independently conduct closings, Louisiana law under La. R.S. 37:212 reserves the preparation of acts of sale, mortgage documents, and title opinions exclusively for licensed attorneys. Agents cannot prepare these instruments, explain their legal effect to clients, or offer opinions on title quality.

The closing attorney performs the title examination (or relies on a title abstractor working under attorney supervision), prepares the act of sale, certifies the title, and issues the title insurance commitment. Agents who recommend skipping attorney review or who attempt to expedite by summarizing legal documents in writing to clients risk disciplinary action from LREC and potential civil liability for unauthorized practice.

This means your role at closing is limited to ensuring your client shows up prepared, that contractual contingencies have been met, and that commission disbursement instructions are accurate. You do not interpret title exceptions. You do not explain mortgage clauses. If a client asks you what a title encumbrance means, the correct answer is always to direct them to the closing attorney.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Handle

Louisiana agents work primarily with forms promulgated or approved by the Louisiana Real Estate Commission and the Louisiana REALTORS association. The purchase agreement — form LR-100 (Residential Purchase Agreement) — is the backbone of every residential transaction. This form includes specific provisions for title examination periods, typically granting the buyer 14 calendar days from acceptance to complete the title search.

| Document | Who Prepares It | Agent's Role |
|----------|----------------|--------------|
| LR-100 Purchase Agreement | Agent (with buyer/seller) | Draft, present, negotiate |
| Title Commitment/Abstract | Closing attorney or abstractor | Deliver to client, track deadline |
| Act of Sale (Authentic Act) | Closing attorney | Attend, verify commission |
| Seller's Property Disclosure (LR-200) | Seller (with agent guidance) | Ensure completion before acceptance |
| ALTA Settlement Statement | Closing attorney or title company | Review for commission accuracy |

Agents are also responsible for ensuring the Property Disclosure Document (form LR-200) is completed and delivered per La. R.S. 9:3198. Failure to provide this form does not void the sale, but it does create a rebuttable presumption that the seller failed to disclose known defects — a liability trap your seller clients stumble into when agents neglect follow-through.

The [transaction coordination process](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) is where many of these form deadlines get tracked. Without a system for monitoring the title examination period, the loan contingency window, and the act of sale date, agents routinely miss contractual triggers that can unwind deals entirely.

## Title Examination: The 30-Year Search and Beyond

Louisiana's title examination standards, published by the Louisiana State Bar Association's Title Standards Committee, require a minimum 30-year search of the public records in the parish where the property is located. This search must trace the chain of title through conveyance records, mortgage records, judgment records, and tax records maintained by the parish Clerk of Court.

**Successions (Louisiana's term for probate) are the single biggest source of title complications in the state.** When a property owner dies in Louisiana, their interest does not automatically transfer to heirs — it must pass through a judicial process. An unresolved succession can cloud title for decades, and agents who push to close before succession judgments are recorded invite post-closing litigation.

The closing attorney issues a title opinion or commitment letter identifying any exceptions, liens, or encumbrances. Common exceptions include mineral rights reservations (extremely common in Louisiana, governed by the Mineral Code, La. R.S. 31:1 et seq.), outstanding mortgage balances, and servitudes (what other states call easements). Agents must understand that mineral rights in Louisiana are separate from surface rights and can be owned by third parties without appearing on a standard property survey.

## Common Mistakes Louisiana Agents Make at Closing

The first mistake is treating the title examination period as a mere formality. Under the LR-100, the buyer has a specific number of days (typically 14) to complete the title examination. If the title search reveals defects and the buyer fails to object within this window, they may be deemed to have accepted title as-is. Agents who do not calendar this deadline and actively follow up with the closing attorney risk losing their client's right to demand cure.

The second mistake involves mineral rights disclosure failures. Louisiana agents frequently forget to discuss mineral rights with buyers, particularly those relocating from states where subsurface rights are not commonly severed. While the LR-100 includes a mineral rights provision, agents must affirmatively draw attention to it and recommend buyers research mineral ownership through the parish conveyance records. Ignoring this conversation has generated complaints to LREC and civil claims against agents.

The third error is disbursing or accepting commission before the act of sale is recorded. Louisiana law requires that real estate commissions be paid through the broker (La. R.S. 37:1447), and agents who pressure closing attorneys to release commission checks before the act is filed with the Clerk of Court create compliance problems. LREC Rule 2503 prohibits agents from receiving compensation from any party other than their employing broker.

The fourth mistake is providing legal opinions disguised as "advice." When a title commitment arrives with Schedule B exceptions, agents often try to explain what those exceptions mean to anxious clients. Even well-intentioned explanations can constitute unauthorized practice of law under La. R.S. 37:212. The proper action is always referral to the closing attorney.

The fifth mistake is [missing contractual deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) embedded in addenda. Louisiana transactions frequently involve addenda for flood zone determinations, environmental assessments, or repairs. Each addendum carries its own timeline, and agents who track only the major dates in the LR-100 while ignoring addendum-specific deadlines create breach-of-contract exposure for their clients.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Louisiana bear direct supervisory liability under La. R.S. 37:1447 and LREC Rules. If a supervised agent engages in unauthorized practice of law, fails to deliver required disclosures, or mishandles escrow instructions, the broker faces potential license suspension, fines up to $5,000 per violation, and civil liability.

| Audit Point | What to Look For | Consequence of Failure |
|-------------|------------------|----------------------|
| Disclosure delivery timing | LR-200 delivered before acceptance | Rebuttable presumption of non-disclosure |
| Title period compliance | Objection letters sent within contractual window | Loss of buyer's right to demand title cure |
| Commission routing | All payments through broker's account | LREC fine, potential license revocation |
| Mineral rights discussion | Documented in transaction file | Client complaint, malpractice exposure |
| Act of sale attendance | Agent present or accounted for | Client abandonment claims |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits focusing specifically on the title and closing phase.** The LREC conducts random audits of brokerage files and expects to see evidence that agents operated within their licensed authority. Files should contain copies of the title commitment, evidence the agent forwarded it to the client, and written referral to the attorney for any questions. Brokers who rely on [memory rather than systematic checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) for compliance are setting themselves up for audit failures.

Brokers must also verify that agents are not recommending specific closing attorneys in exchange for referral fees or kickbacks, which violates both RESPA (12 U.S.C. 2607) and LREC Rule 2503. Affiliated business arrangement disclosures must be provided when applicable.

## Recording, Transfer Taxes, and Post-Closing Obligations

Once the act of sale is executed before a notary (all authentic acts in Louisiana require notarization per La. C.C. Art. 1833), the closing attorney records the document with the parish Clerk of Court. Louisiana does not impose a state transfer tax, but there are recording fees that vary by parish — typically between $50 and $150 for the first page plus per-page charges for additional pages.

Agents should confirm with the closing attorney that the act of sale has been recorded within a reasonable time after closing. Delays in recording can expose buyers to intervening liens or claims from third parties who may file against the seller's name between execution and recording. This is not a theoretical risk — it happens in parishes with backlogged recording offices.

The Louisiana Department of Revenue requires that any withholding obligations for non-resident sellers under La. R.S. 47:303(B) be addressed at closing. If the seller is a non-resident of Louisiana, the closing attorney must withhold a portion of the sales price for state income tax purposes unless an exemption applies. Agents representing non-resident sellers should flag this requirement early so it does not surprise the client at the closing table.
