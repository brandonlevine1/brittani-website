## Understanding Oregon Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026

Missing a single contingency deadline can collapse a transaction, expose an agent to an Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) complaint, or cost a buyer their earnest money deposit. Oregon buyer contingency deadlines 2026 demand precision because the Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) committee updated several timeline provisions following feedback from the 2025 legislative session, and agents who rely on old habits will find themselves out of compliance. The stakes are straightforward: know your deadlines, deliver notices on time, or face the consequences.

Oregon's contingency framework is governed by the sale agreement forms published by OREF, the contractual language therein, and the licensing standards enforced by the Oregon Real Estate Agency under ORS Chapter 696. The forms themselves are the contract — there is no separate statutory deadline schedule that overrides what the parties agree to in writing. This means the burden of accuracy falls squarely on the agents who fill in the blanks and advise their clients on performance.

## The Forms That Control the Timeline

**Every deadline in an Oregon residential transaction traces back to a specific blank on a specific OREF form.** The primary document is the OREF Residential Sale Agreement (Form No. 001), which contains the inspection contingency, financing contingency, appraisal contingency, and title contingency deadlines in designated sections. Agents also work with the OREF Contingency Removal form (Form No. 036) and the OREF Buyer's Notice to Seller Regarding Inspections (Form No. 035).

The Sale Agreement's Section 5 establishes the Professional Inspection Contingency, including the number of business days the buyer has to complete inspections and deliver a repair request or notice of termination. Section 6 addresses financing, specifying when the buyer must provide proof of loan approval. Section 7 governs the appraisal contingency with its own independent timeline.

Each of these sections requires the agent to insert a specific number of business days. If the blank is left empty, the OREF default applies — and many agents do not memorize those defaults. For the 2026 revision cycle, OREF shortened certain default periods to align with lender processing standards, making it even more dangerous to leave blanks unfilled.

## How Business Days Are Calculated Under OREF

The counting method trips up agents more than any other single issue. Per Section 31 of the OREF Sale Agreement, time periods expressed in "business days" exclude Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays recognized under [ORS 187.010 and 187.020](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors187.html). The first business day is the day after mutual acceptance. If the deadline falls on a non-business day, it rolls to the next business day.

Calendar days, when specified, include weekends and holidays. Some addenda — particularly those used for short sale transactions or new construction — use calendar days instead of business days. Agents must read the specific language in every addendum attached to their file rather than assuming uniformity.

| Contingency | Default Period (Business Days) | Controlling Form Section | Key Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Professional Inspection | 10 business days | Sale Agreement, Section 5 | Buyer's Notice (Form 035) or termination |
| Financing | 21 business days | Sale Agreement, Section 6 | Written loan commitment evidence |
| Appraisal | 5 business days after receipt | Sale Agreement, Section 7 | Notice of acceptance or termination |
| Title Review | 5 business days after receipt | Sale Agreement, Section 8 | Written objection or deemed accepted |
| HOA Document Review | 5 business days after receipt | Sale Agreement, Section 9 | Written objection or deemed accepted |

These defaults apply only when agents leave the blanks unfilled. Most experienced listing agents negotiate shorter windows, so buyer's agents must confirm the actual numbers on every signed agreement.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

**The consequences of missing a contingency deadline cascade in three directions: contractual, regulatory, and financial.** Contractually, a buyer who fails to deliver timely notice under Section 5 is deemed to have accepted the property's condition, forfeiting the right to request repairs or terminate based on inspection findings. The seller can then demand the buyer proceed to closing or forfeit earnest money.

From a regulatory standpoint, the Oregon Real Estate Agency can pursue disciplinary action under ORS 696.301 if an agent's failure to track deadlines constitutes negligent or incompetent conduct. Sanctions range from a letter of reprimand to license suspension. The OREA has publicly stated in its 2025 enforcement bulletin that deadline management failures are among the top five complaint categories received from consumers.

Financial liability is the third rail. If a buyer loses earnest money because their agent missed a deadline, the agent's errors and omissions insurance may cover the claim — but the deductible, premium increase, and reputational damage remain. In cases where the agent failed to notify the client of the deadline entirely, courts have found agents personally liable for the buyer's damages beyond the earnest money amount, including lost opportunity costs and increased housing expenses.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Oregon Contingency Deadlines

The first error is assuming that emailing the listing agent constitutes proper delivery. Under OREF form provisions, delivery means actual receipt by the other party or their agent during business hours, or electronic delivery confirmed by the receiving party's system. An email that bounces, lands in spam, or arrives after 5:00 PM on the deadline day may not qualify. Agents who lack delivery confirmation have no proof they performed on time.

The second mistake is conflating the inspection contingency with the repair negotiation period. Section 5 of the OREF Sale Agreement gives the buyer a set number of days to complete inspections AND deliver their notice. This is not two separate timelines — it is one window for both activities. Agents who schedule inspections on Day 8 of a 10-day window leave almost no time to review reports, consult with the buyer, and draft a proper notice. The reasons [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) often trace back to this exact scheduling miscalculation.

**The third common error is failing to account for the "receipt" trigger on certain contingencies.** The title review and HOA document review periods do not start at mutual acceptance — they start when the buyer actually receives the documents. If a title company is slow to deliver the preliminary title report, the buyer's clock has not started. Agents who calendar these deadlines from the acceptance date rather than the receipt date either give their clients too little time or falsely believe a deadline has passed.

The fourth mistake involves the financing contingency removal. Some buyer's agents believe their client's pre-approval letter satisfies the financing contingency. It does not. The OREF form requires evidence of actual loan commitment — a conditional approval or clear-to-close from the lender. Delivering a pre-approval letter on Day 21 and calling it done is a compliance failure that exposes the buyer to termination by the seller.

The fifth error is neglecting to use the correct form for contingency removal. Agents sometimes send a casual email saying "my buyer removes their inspection contingency" without executing OREF Form 036. The listing agent and seller are not obligated to accept informal communication as a binding contingency removal when the contract references specific form requirements.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.890 for the acts of their affiliated agents. A broker who discovers — or should have discovered — that an agent routinely misses contingency deadlines faces the same OREA sanctions as the agent. Proactive audit systems are not optional; they are a legal obligation.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deadline calendar accuracy | Every new file | Correct business day count from mutual acceptance | Blanks left unfilled on sale agreement |
| Delivery confirmation | At each deadline | Timestamp of email/fax/hand delivery receipt | No proof of delivery in file |
| Form usage | At contingency removal | Correct OREF form number executed and countersigned | Informal email used instead of Form 036 |
| Client communication log | Weekly | Written evidence agent informed buyer of upcoming deadline | No communication within 48 hours of deadline |
| Financing milestone tracking | Days 14 and 21 | Lender status update documenting progress toward commitment | No lender contact after initial application |

Brokers should establish a written policy requiring agents to submit deadline tracking sheets within 24 hours of mutual acceptance. The policy should mandate that all contingency-related notices be reviewed by a transaction coordinator or compliance officer before delivery. Firms that rely solely on individual agents to self-manage deadlines are building liability exposure into their business model — a reality explored in detail in our analysis of [why transaction coordination is broken](/blog/why-transaction-coordination-is-broken).

**Every brokerage file audit should include a reverse-engineering check:** start from the closing date and work backward to confirm that every contingency was either formally removed via the correct OREF form or properly terminated within the contractual window. Files where contingencies simply "disappeared" without documentation are ticking time bombs for post-closing disputes.

## The Intersection of Deadlines and Earnest Money

Oregon earnest money disputes frequently originate from ambiguous contingency deadline performance. When a buyer terminates after the inspection deadline passes, the seller claims the earnest money. When the buyer argues they terminated before the deadline using an email that the listing agent denies receiving, both brokerages end up holding disputed funds under ORS 696.581.

The Oregon Real Estate Agency requires that disputed earnest money be held in the broker's clients' trust account until the parties reach agreement, a court orders disbursement, or interpleader is filed. This process takes months, during which the transaction remains unresolved and both parties incur legal costs. Agents who understand that [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often centers on these exact earnest money disputes will invest far more care in delivery confirmation.

Proper deadline management eliminates the vast majority of these disputes. When an agent can produce a timestamped delivery confirmation showing that OREF Form 035 was received by the listing agent at 2:47 PM on Business Day 9 of a 10-day inspection period, there is simply nothing to argue about.
