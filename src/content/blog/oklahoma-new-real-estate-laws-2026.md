## Oklahoma New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Changes That Matter Most

The Oklahoma Real Estate Commission (OREC) has rolled out a slate of regulatory updates that take effect between January 1 and July 1, 2026. For agents still running on 2024-era muscle memory, the gap between old habits and Oklahoma new real estate laws 2026 requirements is wide enough to kill deals or trigger license complaints. The changes touch disclosure timelines, buyer representation agreements, wire fraud prevention, and commission advertising rules.

These are not cosmetic tweaks. The Oklahoma Legislature passed HB 2847 and SB 1193 in the spring 2025 session, both of which amend portions of the Oklahoma Real Estate License Code (Title 59, Sections 858-101 through 858-833) and the Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act (Title 60, Section 831-839). OREC followed up with administrative rule changes finalized in January 2026 under OAC 605:10.

Agents who attended the 2025-2026 continuing education cycle likely received an overview of these shifts. But the real test comes at the transaction level, where a missed form revision or a blown deadline can unwind a deal or expose a licensee to disciplinary proceedings.

## Updated Disclosure Timelines Under Title 60

**The delivery window for the Residential Property Condition Disclosure Statement has been cut in half.** Under the amended Title 60, Section 833, sellers must deliver the completed disclosure to buyers within five calendar days of mutual acceptance. The previous statute allowed ten days, and many agents treated that as a suggestion rather than a hard deadline.

The new timeline applies to all residential transactions involving one-to-four unit properties unless a statutory exemption under Section 832 applies. Exemptions include court-ordered sales, foreclosures, transfers between co-owners, and sales by personal representatives of an estate. If your listing does not fall into one of those categories, the five-day clock starts the moment both parties sign.

OREC Form 140A has been revised to reflect the 2026 changes. The updated form adds three new disclosure items: known flooding history (regardless of whether the property sits in a FEMA-designated zone), previous insurance claims for mold remediation, and the presence of any unpermitted structural additions. Agents must use the July 2026 version of this form for any contract executed on or after July 1.

| Requirement | Pre-2026 Rule | 2026 Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure delivery deadline | 10 calendar days after acceptance | 5 calendar days after acceptance |
| Flooding history disclosure | Not required | Required (OREC Form 140A) |
| Mold remediation claims | Not required | Required (OREC Form 140A) |
| Unpermitted additions | Not specifically addressed | Required disclosure item |
| Buyer rescission period | 5 days from receipt | 5 days from receipt (unchanged) |

The buyer's rescission period remains five days from receipt of the disclosure. That means the compressed seller timeline does not shortchange the buyer's review window. It does, however, demand that listing agents educate their sellers early in the relationship about the documentation they need to gather before going active on MLS.

## Buyer Representation Agreements: No More Informal Touring

SB 1193 codifies what the [NAR settlement practices](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) already suggested. Oklahoma now requires a written buyer representation agreement before an agent provides any real estate services to a prospective buyer. "Services" includes property tours, whether in person or virtual.

The statutory language in the amended Title 59, Section 858-354.1 defines the minimum contents of the agreement: the duration of representation, the specific services to be provided, the compensation amount or method, and a statement confirming the buyer's right to terminate. OREC Form 201B (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) was updated in March 2026 to include these required fields.

**Agents who show properties without a signed Form 201B risk more than an ethics complaint.** Under the new enforcement framework, OREC can impose fines of up to $2,500 per transaction where no written agreement existed at the time of service. Repeated violations trigger a mandatory appearance before the Commission and possible suspension.

This requirement applies even in open house settings where a buyer who is unrepresented asks the listing agent substantive questions about the property. If the conversation crosses from general information into advisory territory, the agent must pause and execute an agreement before continuing. OREC's guidance bulletin from February 2026 provides a three-part test for determining when the advisory threshold has been crossed.

## Wire Fraud Prevention: Oklahoma's New Statutory Shield

HB 2847 creates Title 59, Section 858-751, a new provision that imposes affirmative duties on all parties in a real estate transaction to verify wire transfer instructions through a secondary confirmed communication channel. This is Oklahoma's legislative response to escalating wire fraud losses, which the FBI's IC3 reported at $145 million nationally in 2024.

The statute requires closing agents (title companies and attorneys) to establish and communicate a verified callback number at the start of every transaction. Listing agents and buyer's agents are required to include a wire fraud warning addendum with the executed purchase agreement. OREC has published Form 225W (Wire Fraud Acknowledgment and Verification) for this purpose.

If an agent forwards wire instructions received via email without independently verifying them through the closing agent's established callback number, the agent may be held jointly liable for losses under the new Section 858-751(C). This is not a vague best-practice recommendation. It is statutory civil liability with no cap specified.

Brokerages should consider reviewing their procedures around [wire instruction verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) immediately. The statute took effect January 1, 2026, meaning any transaction closing now must comply.

## Commission Advertising and Fee Transparency Rules

**OREC's revised advertising rules under OAC 605:10-11-4 prohibit any public marketing that implies a commission rate is "standard" or "typical" for the market.** Agents cannot state or suggest that a specific percentage is the norm in Oklahoma. The administrative rule change aligns with post-settlement expectations around commission transparency.

Additionally, any written communication to consumers that references compensation must include a statement that commission rates are negotiable. This applies to listing presentations, buyer consultations, pre-listing packets, and CMA cover letters. OREC did not create a mandatory form for this notice, but agents must document that the disclosure was made.

The penalty for violation is a fine of up to $1,000 per occurrence for a first offense and up to $5,000 for subsequent offenses within a 24-month period. OREC has signaled that it will prioritize enforcement through consumer complaints and brokerage audits beginning in Q3 2026.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

The first wave of transactions under these new rules has revealed predictable patterns of non-compliance. Here are five specific errors OREC is already flagging through its complaint intake process.

**Using outdated disclosure forms.** Agents who printed batches of Form 140A before July 2026 are submitting the pre-revision version. The old form lacks the flooding, mold, and unpermitted addition disclosure lines. Transactions using the old form are technically non-compliant and give buyers a potential rescission argument post-closing.

**Treating the buyer representation agreement as optional for repeat clients.** The statute requires a written agreement for each discrete period of representation. An expired agreement from a previous transaction does not carry forward. Agents assuming their ongoing relationship with a buyer covers them are mistaken under the plain language of Section 858-354.1.

**Failing to deliver the wire fraud acknowledgment at contract execution.** Many agents are attaching Form 225W at closing rather than at the time the purchase agreement is signed. The statute specifies "contemporaneous with execution of the purchase agreement," which means the form must accompany the contract package.

**Mishandling the five-day disclosure clock.** Some agents are counting business days instead of calendar days. The statute says "calendar days." A listing executed on a Wednesday gives the seller through Monday to deliver, including weekends. Agents who count only business days are blowing the deadline by two or three days.

**Advertising commission rates without the negotiability statement.** Social media posts, property flyers, and even MLS remarks that reference a specific co-op commission percentage without the required disclosure violate OAC 605:10-11-4. Agents who manage their own social media accounts are particularly vulnerable here.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Title 59, Section 858-312 for the actions of their affiliated licensees. When an agent violates the 2026 requirements, the broker's license is also at stake. Here is what managing brokers should be reviewing at minimum on a quarterly basis.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Form version control | All active files use July 2026 Form 140A and March 2026 Form 201B | Deal rescission, OREC complaint |
| Buyer representation compliance | Signed Form 201B exists in every buyer file before first showing | $2,500 fine per violation |
| Wire fraud acknowledgment timing | Form 225W dated same day or before purchase agreement | Joint civil liability for wire losses |
| Disclosure delivery documentation | Timestamped proof of delivery within 5 calendar days | Buyer rescission rights extended indefinitely |
| Advertising compliance | All public-facing materials include negotiability language | $1,000-$5,000 per occurrence |

Brokers should implement a file review checklist that flags these items at three stages: listing intake, contract execution, and pre-closing. The old model of reviewing files only at closing is insufficient under the compressed timelines. A post-closing audit catches problems too late to fix.

Transaction management systems that auto-check form versions and flag missing documents can reduce the audit burden significantly. Brokers running high-volume offices will find manual file reviews unsustainable, particularly given that [checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in compliance-heavy environments.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences break into three categories: regulatory, transactional, and civil. Regulatory consequences flow from OREC's enforcement authority under Title 59, Section 858-208. The Commission can issue fines, require additional education, impose probation, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely depending on severity and history.

Transactional consequences are often more immediate. A buyer who does not receive the disclosure within the five-day window retains rescission rights indefinitely until proper delivery occurs. That means a buyer could rescind the contract three weeks into the transaction if the seller's agent failed to deliver on time. Earnest money disputes, lost inspection costs, and appraisal fees follow.

Civil consequences under the wire fraud statute are the most financially dangerous. Section 858-751(C) creates a private cause of action for buyers or sellers who suffer financial losses due to an agent's failure to verify wire instructions. There is no statutory cap, meaning a $400,000 wire loss becomes the agent's personal exposure if they forwarded fraudulent instructions without verification.
