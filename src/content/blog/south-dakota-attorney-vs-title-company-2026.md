# South Dakota Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing in 2026

The question of South Dakota attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up agents more often than you might expect. South Dakota sits in a category of states where neither an attorney nor a specific type of closing agent is mandated by statute, which sounds simple until you realize the flexibility itself creates compliance traps. Understanding exactly who can do what at the closing table — and where your license stops — is the difference between a clean file and a complaint to the South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC).

South Dakota is not an attorney-state for real estate closings. Unlike states such as New York, Massachusetts, or Georgia that require attorney involvement, South Dakota permits licensed title insurance companies and their agents to handle settlement without a lawyer in the room. This framework is codified through the state's title insurance licensing statutes under [SDCL Title 58, Chapter 58-30](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/58-30), which governs title insurance companies and their authority to issue policies and conduct closings.

## Who Is Legally Authorized to Conduct a Closing

**Three categories of professionals can legally oversee a residential closing in South Dakota: licensed attorneys, licensed title insurance companies, and licensed title insurance agents.** No state statute requires any specific one of these to be present. The parties to the transaction choose.

Title insurance agents in South Dakota must hold a license issued by the South Dakota Division of Insurance under SDCL 58-30-7. They can search title, issue commitments and policies, prepare settlement statements, and disburse funds. Attorneys admitted to the South Dakota State Bar can perform these same functions under their law license, plus offer legal counsel — a critical distinction agents need to internalize.

Real estate agents cannot conduct a closing. Your license authorizes you to fill in the blanks on pre-approved forms — primarily those published by the South Dakota Association of Realtors (SDAR) — and to facilitate the transaction. Drafting legal instruments, interpreting contract clauses for clients, or preparing deeds crosses into unauthorized practice of law territory under SDCL 16-18-34.

## What Agents Can and Cannot Do at the Closing Table

The line between facilitation and legal practice is thinner than many agents treat it. South Dakota courts and the SDREC have been consistent: agents may fill in blanks on standardized forms but may not draft custom provisions or advise clients on the legal effect of contract terms.

| Activity | Real Estate Agent | Title Company/Agent | Attorney |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fill in blanks on SDAR standard forms | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Draft custom contract addenda | No | No | Yes |
| Prepare warranty deeds or quit claim deeds | No | Yes (limited) | Yes |
| Provide legal advice on contract terms | No | No | Yes |
| Prepare HUD-1/Closing Disclosure | No | Yes | Yes |
| Disburse closing funds from escrow | No | Yes | Yes |
| Issue title insurance commitments | No | Yes | Yes (if also licensed) |

The SDAR Purchase Agreement form and its associated addenda — including the SDAR Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement — are the standard instruments agents work with. When a situation requires language outside these forms, the correct move is referring your client to an attorney, not improvising contract clauses on a blank addendum.

## The Unauthorized Practice of Law Risk

**SDCL 16-18-34 makes it a Class 2 misdemeanor for any person not admitted to the South Dakota Bar to practice law.** Penalties include up to 30 days in county jail and a $500 fine. Beyond criminal exposure, an agent who engages in unauthorized practice of law faces SDREC disciplinary action, including license suspension or revocation under SDCL 36-21A-71.

The South Dakota Supreme Court has interpreted the practice of law broadly. In its administrative rules, preparing legal documents for others — even without charging a fee — can constitute unauthorized practice if the documents require legal judgment beyond filling in blanks. An agent who drafts a custom earnest money agreement from scratch or writes a hold-harmless clause into an addendum is squarely inside this danger zone.

Civil liability adds a third layer. If a client suffers financial harm because you drafted a defective legal instrument or gave incorrect legal advice, you are personally exposed. Your E&O policy likely excludes coverage for acts that exceed the scope of your license, meaning the judgment comes straight from your pocket.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in South Dakota Closings

Knowing the rules in the abstract is not the same as following them under the pressure of a deal that needs to close Friday. Here are the errors that generate the most complaints and liability exposure.

**First, agents routinely explain title commitment exceptions to buyers as if they were attorneys.** A title commitment lists exceptions to coverage — easements, mineral rights reservations, restrictive covenants. When a buyer asks "What does this exception mean for me?", the compliant answer is "Your attorney or the title company can explain the legal effect." The noncompliant answer is any substantive interpretation you offer, no matter how accurate you think it is.

Second, agents draft custom addenda when standard SDAR forms do not fit the situation. A repair agreement with a holdback escrow, a boundary dispute resolution clause, or a mineral rights carve-out requires attorney drafting. Using a generic "Additional Provisions" blank to write three paragraphs of custom legal language is unauthorized practice, period.

Third, agents fail to disclose affiliated business arrangements. If your brokerage has an ownership interest in a title company — or you receive any compensation for referrals — RESPA Section 8 and the affiliated business arrangement (AfBA) disclosure requirements apply. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's RESPA guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act/) makes clear that failure to provide the AfBA disclosure form at or before referral is a federal violation carrying penalties up to $10,000 per occurrence.

Fourth, agents attend closings and answer client questions in ways that blur their role. Sitting at the table and saying "I think the title company is right about that easement" or "You should probably just sign the indemnification" is giving legal advice. You are not a party to the transaction and your opinion on legal documents carries liability.

Fifth, agents misunderstand who is responsible for ensuring the deed is properly executed and recorded. In South Dakota, the title company or closing attorney handles recording with the Register of Deeds. Agents who promise their clients that recording will happen by a certain date — or who take possession of original documents to hand-deliver them — create unnecessary liability when delays occur.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-65, which requires designated brokers to supervise the activities of every licensee affiliated with the brokerage. The SDREC can discipline a broker for failing to ensure agents comply with the law, even if the broker had no direct knowledge of the violation.

**Every brokerage operating in South Dakota should audit three things quarterly.** First, review closed transaction files for any non-standard addenda drafted by agents. If you find custom legal language that did not come from an attorney, you have a training problem and an exposure problem. Address both immediately.

Second, confirm that AfBA disclosures are present in every file where the brokerage or any agent has a referral relationship with a title company, lender, or settlement service provider. Missing AfBA forms are one of the most common RESPA audit findings, and the fines are not theoretical.

Third, review your office policy on closing attendance. Some brokerages encourage agents to attend every closing as a service differentiator. That is fine — but agents attending closings need clear written guidance on what they can and cannot say. A one-page policy memo is not overkill; it is insurance against a complaint that your agent practiced law at the closing table.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Custom addenda in closed files | Quarterly | Non-SDAR language drafted by agents | UPL complaint, SDREC discipline |
| AfBA disclosure forms | Every file | Signed disclosure at or before referral | RESPA fines up to $10,000/occurrence |
| Closing attendance policy | Annual review | Written guidance on agent conduct at closings | Vicarious liability for UPL |
| Earnest money handling records | Monthly | Proper trust account documentation per SDCL 36-21A-55 | License suspension, fines |

The SDREC does not need a client complaint to initiate an investigation. Routine audits of brokerage trust accounts and transaction files happen, and they uncover the same patterns repeatedly. Brokers who build compliance checks into their workflow avoid the unpleasant surprise of an investigator requesting three years of files.

## How the Closing Process Typically Unfolds in South Dakota

Most residential closings in South Dakota follow a title-company-led model. The purchase agreement is executed on SDAR standard forms, earnest money is deposited into the listing brokerage's trust account or directly with the title company, and the title company orders a title search and issues a commitment for title insurance. There is no statutory requirement to use the same entity for title search and closing, but in practice, the same title company usually handles both.

The title company prepares the Closing Disclosure (which replaced the HUD-1 for most transactions under TRID rules), coordinates with the lender on loan documents, and schedules the closing. At the table, the title company's closing agent walks the parties through document execution. Funds are collected, disbursed, and the deed and mortgage are recorded with the county Register of Deeds. If an attorney is involved — typically retained by one of the parties rather than required by law — that attorney reviews documents, advises their client, and may attend closing.

This is where exceptions surface, surveys reveal encroachments, and clients panic. Your job during this window is coordination, not legal interpretation.
