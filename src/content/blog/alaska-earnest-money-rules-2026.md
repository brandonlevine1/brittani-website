## Understanding Alaska Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Alaska earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real teeth, and agents who treat trust account compliance as a formality are inviting trouble they cannot undo with a phone call. The Alaska Real Estate Commission, operating under the Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing, enforces these rules through audits, complaint investigations, and disciplinary hearings that can end careers. Every dollar of earnest money you touch is governed by a framework of statutes, administrative code provisions, and standard forms that demand precision.

**Alaska Statute 08.88.401 establishes the foundational duty: all money received by a licensee on behalf of others must be deposited into a trust account maintained by the broker.** This is not optional, not negotiable, and not subject to verbal workarounds. The supporting regulations live in 12 AAC 64.550, which spells out deposit timing, record-keeping standards, and reconciliation requirements that apply to every brokerage in the state.

The stakes are straightforward. Violate these rules and the Commission can impose fines up to $25,000 per violation, suspend or revoke your license, and refer the matter for criminal prosecution if the facts support it. Civil lawsuits from aggrieved buyers or sellers pile on top.

## The Statutory Framework Agents Must Know

Alaska's earnest money and escrow rules sit in two primary sources: Alaska Statutes Title 8, Chapter 88 (the Real Estate Licensing Act) and Title 12 of the Alaska Administrative Code, Chapter 64 (regulations of the Real Estate Commission). Agents should also be aware of AS 08.88.071, which outlines the Commission's disciplinary authority, including the power to investigate trust account complaints without a formal hearing.

12 AAC 64.550 is the regulation that governs trust accounts in detail. It requires that all earnest money and deposits be placed in an [FDIC-insured or NCUA-insured](https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/cbpl/ProfessionalLicensing/RealEstateCommission.aspx) account in an Alaska financial institution. The account must be clearly labeled as a trust or escrow account, and it must identify the brokerage by name.

The deposit deadline is two business days after receipt of the funds, unless the purchase agreement explicitly states a different timeline. This two-day window is one of the most frequently misunderstood rules in the state, and it is the source of a disproportionate share of complaints filed with the Commission.

## Standard Forms and Where They Apply

The Alaska Association of REALTORS provides the standard forms most agents use in residential transactions. The primary purchase agreement is the **AAR Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement**, and it contains the earnest money provisions that define the amount, deposit deadline, and the trust account or escrow company where the funds will be held. Agents must fill in these fields completely; leaving them blank or writing "TBD" creates ambiguity that benefits no one in a dispute.

For transactions involving alternative deposit arrangements, AAR also publishes addendum forms that allow parties to specify conditions such as a delayed deposit tied to a contingency waiver. The **AAR Earnest Money Addendum** is used when the parties want to modify the standard earnest money terms, such as increasing the deposit upon removal of an inspection contingency.

When deals fall apart, the **AAR Release of Earnest Money form** is the document that both parties must sign to authorize the broker to disburse the funds. Without mutual written consent on this form, the broker is stuck holding the money. Unilateral release by the broker without proper authorization is a violation of 12 AAC 64.550 and a fast track to a disciplinary hearing.

## Earnest Money Disputes: What the Code Actually Says

**Disputed earnest money is the scenario that keeps brokers up at night, and Alaska law provides a clear but narrow set of options.** When buyer and seller both claim entitlement to the deposit and refuse to sign a mutual release, the broker cannot simply pick a side. The funds must remain in the trust account until both parties agree in writing or a court orders disbursement.

Alaska Civil Rule 22 allows the broker to file an interpleader action, depositing the disputed funds with the court and asking a judge to decide who gets the money. This removes the broker from the middle of the fight. The cost of filing an interpleader is typically deducted from the escrowed funds, though the purchase agreement may allocate that cost differently.

Some brokers attempt to avoid interpleader by waiting indefinitely, hoping the parties will eventually agree. This is risky. The Commission expects brokers to take affirmative steps to resolve trust account disputes within a reasonable timeframe. Sitting on disputed funds for six months without action can itself trigger a complaint.

| Scenario | Broker's Required Action | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| Both parties sign mutual release | Disburse per the release terms | Within 2 business days of signed release |
| One party demands, the other refuses | Hold funds; attempt mediation or interpleader | Reasonable timeframe; no statutory cap |
| Court orders disbursement | Comply with court order | Per court order |
| No response from either party | Continue holding; document all attempts to reach parties | Ongoing obligation |

## Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make With Earnest Money

Mistake number one is missing the two-business-day deposit window. Agents who receive a check on Friday afternoon and wait until the following Wednesday to deliver it to the brokerage are already out of compliance. The clock starts when the licensee receives the funds, not when the broker gets them, so agents who hold checks over a weekend without notifying their broker are creating liability for the entire brokerage.

Mistake number two is failing to specify the escrow holder in the purchase agreement. If the AAR form's field for the trust account holder is left blank or contains vague language like "to be determined," the parties have no enforceable agreement about where the money goes. This becomes a serious problem when a dispute arises and neither side can point to a clear contractual term. Always name the specific brokerage trust account or the title company escrow account on the form.

**Mistake number three is commingling.** It sounds like something only a shady operator would do, but it happens in small brokerages more often than the Commission would like. A broker deposits a personal commission check into the trust account to "hold it for a day" or uses trust funds to cover a temporary shortfall in the operating account. Both scenarios violate AS 08.88.401 and 12 AAC 64.550, and both can result in license revocation.

Mistake number four is releasing earnest money without signatures from both parties. An agent who tells the title company to release funds to the buyer after a failed inspection, without getting the seller's written consent on the AAR Release of Earnest Money form, has exposed the brokerage to a claim from the seller and a potential Commission complaint. Even if the contract clearly entitles the buyer to a refund, the procedural step of mutual written release cannot be skipped.

Mistake number five is poor record-keeping on trust account transactions. 12 AAC 64.550 requires brokers to maintain a ledger for each transaction showing deposits, disbursements, and the running balance. Agents who handle earnest money but fail to provide their broker with timely documentation of receipt make accurate reconciliation impossible. This is exactly the kind of gap that gets flagged in a [routine deadline review](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) or Commission audit.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry the primary regulatory burden for trust account compliance. The Commission holds the broker responsible for every dollar that moves through the trust account, regardless of which agent in the office handled the transaction. This means brokers need systems, not just policies.

Monthly trust account reconciliation is not optional. 12 AAC 64.550 requires brokers to reconcile the trust account balance with the individual transaction ledgers at least monthly. The reconciliation must be documented and retained for at least three years. Brokers who delegate this task to an unlicensed office manager should still review and sign off on each reconciliation personally.

**Brokers should also audit the timing of deposits on a random-sample basis.** Pull five to ten transaction files per quarter and compare the date the agent received the earnest money check (as documented on the receipt or the AAR form) against the date the deposit hit the trust account. If the gap exceeds two business days and no written extension exists, that is a compliance failure that needs corrective action before the Commission finds it first.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | 12 AAC 64.550 |
| Transaction ledger review | Monthly | 12 AAC 64.550 |
| Deposit timing spot-check | Quarterly (recommended) | AS 08.88.401 / 12 AAC 64.550 |
| Signature verification on releases | Per transaction | AAR Release of Earnest Money form |
| Retention of records (minimum) | 3 years | 12 AAC 64.900 |

Training is another area brokers underestimate. New agents in Alaska often receive zero instruction on trust account procedures during pre-licensing education, and continuing education courses rarely cover escrow handling in operational detail. Brokers who run a [structured checklist system](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) for every transaction reduce the odds of an agent making an earnest money error that cascades into a regulatory problem.

## Liability When Things Go Wrong

The consequences of earnest money violations in Alaska fall into three categories: regulatory, civil, and criminal. On the regulatory side, the Commission can issue a letter of reprimand, impose fines up to $25,000 per violation under AS 08.88.071, suspend a license for a defined period, or revoke it permanently. Consent agreements, where the licensee admits the violation and accepts a negotiated penalty, are common but still appear on the licensee's public disciplinary record.

Civil liability is often more expensive than the regulatory penalty. A buyer who loses a $25,000 earnest money deposit because the broker released it improperly has a straightforward breach of fiduciary duty claim. Attorney fees, damages, and potential punitive damages can dwarf the original deposit amount.

Criminal exposure exists where the facts support a charge of theft, conversion, or fraud. A broker who diverts trust funds for personal use is committing a crime under Alaska's theft statutes (AS 11.46), and the Commission will refer such cases to the Alaska Department of Law. These referrals are not hypothetical; they happen, and they result in prosecutions.

## Practical Steps to Stay Compliant in 2026

The single most effective thing an agent can do is document the moment of receipt. When you receive an earnest money check, note the date and time on the check itself, photograph it, and transmit a copy to your broker immediately. This creates an indisputable record that protects you if questions arise later about deposit timing.

Agents should also confirm, before presenting an offer, that the escrow holder named in the purchase agreement is prepared to accept the deposit. Title companies occasionally change their escrow procedures or close branch offices, and an earnest money check sent to a defunct address creates avoidable chaos.
