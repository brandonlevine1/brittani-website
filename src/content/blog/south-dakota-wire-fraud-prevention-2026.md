## Why South Dakota Agents Face Growing Wire Fraud Exposure

Real estate wire fraud losses exceeded $446 million nationally in 2023, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. South Dakota is not immune. The **South Dakota wire fraud prevention requirements 2026** landscape places direct responsibility on licensed agents and their brokers to warn consumers, verify wire instructions, and document every step.

The South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) enforces professional standards under South Dakota Codified Law (SDCL) Title 36, Chapter 21A. While no single statute uses the phrase "wire fraud," several provisions create duties that apply squarely to wiring-related misconduct. Agents who dismiss these obligations as someone else's problem are betting their licenses on luck.

This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, enforcement risks, and broker-level audit requirements that govern wire fraud prevention in South Dakota transactions heading into 2026.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind South Dakota Wire Fraud Duties

South Dakota does not have a standalone wire fraud prevention statute the way some states do. Instead, the SDREC relies on a combination of existing professional conduct rules under SDCL 36-21A-69 through 36-21A-72 and administrative rules found in ARSD 20:69. These provisions require agents to act in good faith, disclose material facts, and protect the interests of their clients during the transaction.

**SDCL 36-21A-71 is the section most often cited in disciplinary actions involving failed disclosures.** It grants the commission authority to suspend, revoke, or condition a license when an agent engages in conduct that demonstrates incompetence, negligence, or failure to safeguard client funds. A missing wire fraud advisory, when a buyer loses money as a result, fits squarely within that definition.

The [South Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/) has signaled through 2025 bulletins that wire fraud awareness is an expected part of agent competency. The commission's position is that failing to warn a buyer about wire fraud schemes constitutes a breach of the agent's fiduciary obligation under SDCL 36-21A-69, which defines the duties owed by licensees to their clients.

## The Forms South Dakota Agents Must Know

The South Dakota Association of REALTORS (SDREALTORS) publishes a standardized transaction forms library updated annually. The form most relevant to wire fraud is the **Wire Fraud Advisory**, which is distributed as part of the SDREALTORS transaction packet and is typically presented alongside the purchase agreement or buyer advisory documents.

This form warns clients that criminals frequently impersonate real estate professionals, title companies, and lenders via email, then redirect wire transfers to fraudulent accounts. The form instructs clients to verify all wiring instructions by calling a known, independently verified phone number before sending any funds. Most brokerages require both buyer and seller signatures on this advisory.

In addition to the SDREALTORS Wire Fraud Advisory, many South Dakota title companies issue their own wire instruction verification forms at or before closing. Agents should treat these as complementary, not as substitutes for the brokerage-level advisory. If your brokerage has an internal wire fraud acknowledgment form, that document should be signed at the same time as the advisory.

| Form | Source | When to Present | Who Signs |
|------|--------|----------------|-----------|
| Wire Fraud Advisory | SDREALTORS | At or before purchase agreement signing | Buyer and seller |
| Wire Instruction Verification | Title company | Before funds are wired | Buyer (and seller if applicable) |
| Brokerage Wire Fraud Acknowledgment | Individual brokerage | At first client meeting or contract execution | Client and agent |
| Closing Disclosure (HUD/TRID) | Lender/title | Three business days before closing | Buyer |

## South Dakota Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Liability and Enforcement

**Agents who skip the wire fraud advisory face more than a slap on the wrist.** The SDREC's disciplinary process under ARSD 20:69:04 allows the commission to impose fines, require additional education, place a license on probation, or suspend it entirely. In cases involving actual consumer loss, revocation is on the table.

Civil liability is the bigger financial risk. If a buyer wires $80,000 to a fraudulent account because no one warned them about email compromise schemes, the agent and brokerage become targets for a negligence lawsuit. South Dakota courts apply standard negligence analysis: duty, breach, causation, and damages. The existence of an industry-standard wire fraud advisory form that the agent failed to present becomes powerful evidence of breach.

Errors and omissions insurance may cover some of these claims, but many E&O policies exclude or limit coverage for failures to follow standard brokerage procedures. If your broker mandated the use of the Wire Fraud Advisory and you skipped it, expect your insurer to scrutinize the claim carefully.

Beyond individual liability, the SDREC can also hold the supervising broker responsible under SDCL 36-21A-24, which places affirmative supervision duties on designated brokers. A broker who knew or should have known that agents were omitting wire fraud disclosures faces independent disciplinary exposure, even if the broker personally did nothing wrong in the transaction.

## Common Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make

The first mistake is treating the wire fraud advisory as optional. Some agents assume the title company handles everything related to wire transfers. While title companies do verify wiring instructions on their end, the agent's duty to warn the client exists independently. Skipping the advisory because "the title company will handle it" leaves a gap in the paper trail that the SDREC will notice.

**The second mistake is delivering the wire fraud advisory too late in the process.** Presenting it at the closing table, minutes before the buyer is expected to sign, defeats the purpose. The buyer needs time to absorb the warning and establish a verification protocol with their bank and the title company. Best practice is to present the form at or before the purchase agreement signing.

The third mistake is relying on email to deliver wire instructions without a verbal confirmation step. This is the exact attack vector criminals exploit. Agents who forward wiring instructions by email, even if the instructions are legitimate, create an opportunity for interception. Always tell clients to call the title company at a phone number they obtained independently, not from the email containing the wiring instructions, to confirm the routing and account numbers.

The fourth mistake is failing to document that the advisory was delivered. A signed copy should be uploaded to the transaction file the same day it is signed. If a dispute arises six months later, an agent's memory of handing over the form will not carry the same weight as a date-stamped, countersigned document in the file. As we have covered in our overview of [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down), documentation gaps are one of the top reasons transactions collapse into disputes.

The fifth mistake is not updating clients when closing is delayed. A postponed closing date means a new window for fraud. If wiring instructions were provided two weeks ago and closing shifts by ten days, the buyer should be reminded to re-verify instructions before sending funds. A quick phone call can prevent a catastrophic loss.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in South Dakota carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-24. That means if an agent in your office fails to deliver the wire fraud advisory and a buyer gets defrauded, the commission can come after your license too. Auditing for compliance is not a suggestion; it is a requirement embedded in your statutory obligations.

**Start by reviewing every closed transaction file for the presence of a signed Wire Fraud Advisory.** If your brokerage uses a digital transaction management platform, set a checklist requirement that blocks the file from being marked "complete" until the advisory is uploaded. This kind of systematic enforcement is far more reliable than verbal reminders at team meetings, which is a point we explore further in our discussion of [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Brokers should also conduct quarterly spot audits. Pull five to ten random transaction files and verify that the advisory was signed before or at the time of contract execution, not at closing. Check that the document is legible, fully executed, and properly dated. If you find consistent gaps, it signals a training problem that needs to be addressed before the SDREC finds it first.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For | Action if Missing |
|------------|-----------|------------------|-------------------|
| Signed Wire Fraud Advisory | Every transaction | Signature of all parties, date on or before contract execution | Require agent to obtain retroactively and document in file with explanation |
| Wire instruction verification call log | Every transaction involving a wire | Agent or TC note confirming verbal verification occurred | Issue written warning to agent; flag file for follow-up |
| Brokerage acknowledgment form | Every transaction (if required by office policy) | Client and agent signatures, dated | Retrain agent on office procedures |
| Delayed closing re-verification | Any transaction with a postponed close | Note confirming client was reminded to re-verify wiring instructions | Add re-verification reminder to closing delay protocol |

Training is the other half of the equation. The SDREC's continuing education requirements under SDCL 36-21A-48 mandate 24 hours of CE every two years for salespersons and brokers. While wire fraud prevention is not a named CE course topic mandated by statute, brokers can and should require their agents to complete wire fraud training as part of their annual brokerage education. Several approved CE providers in South Dakota now offer courses that cover cyber fraud in real estate transactions.
