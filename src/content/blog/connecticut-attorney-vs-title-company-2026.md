## Why the Question of Connecticut Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026 Still Confuses Agents

Connecticut has operated as an attorney-closing state for decades, yet the question of Connecticut attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 continues to trip up agents who relocate from title-company states or who handle their first transaction across state lines. The confusion is understandable: the state has no single statute that reads "attorneys are required at closing" in plain black-letter terms. Instead, the mandate emerges from a web of Connecticut Supreme Court opinions, Practice Book rules, and the regulatory posture of the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection (DCP).

The practical effect is absolute. If you are a licensed agent or broker operating in Connecticut, you must ensure that a licensed Connecticut attorney conducts the closing — not merely reviews paperwork, not merely appears by phone, but physically or virtually presides over the execution of documents, disbursement of funds, and recording instructions.

## The Legal Foundation: Why Attorneys, Not Title Companies, Run Closings

**Connecticut's requirement is rooted in the judiciary's definition of the unauthorized practice of law (UPL), not in a single real estate statute.** The Connecticut Supreme Court's opinion in *Statewide Grievance Committee v. Patton* (1991) established that the preparation of deeds, mortgages, and closing documents constitutes the practice of law. The Connecticut Bar Association's Unauthorized Practice of Law Committee has repeatedly reinforced this position through advisory opinions, most recently reaffirming that non-attorney title closers cannot conduct settlements.

Connecticut General Statutes Section 51-88 prohibits any person from practicing law without bar admission. The DCP, which licenses real estate agents under Chapter 392 (C.G.S. 20-311 et seq.), does not grant agents authority to prepare legal instruments. When an agent facilitates a closing without attorney involvement, the agent is not just violating custom — they are potentially aiding UPL, which carries both regulatory and civil consequences.

Title companies in Connecticut function differently than in states like Wisconsin. They issue title insurance policies, perform title searches, and may hold escrow funds, but they do not "close" the transaction in the way a [title company operates in Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026). In Connecticut, the closing attorney frequently also serves as the title agent, issuing the policy on behalf of a national underwriter while simultaneously conducting the legal closing.

## Who Does What at the Connecticut Closing Table

Understanding roles prevents agents from overstepping or under-preparing. The table below clarifies responsibilities as they apply in 2026 practice.

| Function | Closing Attorney | Title Company (as insurer) | Real Estate Agent |
|----------|-----------------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Prepare deed and conveyance documents | Required | Not permitted | Not permitted |
| Conduct title search or order it | Yes (or delegates to title company) | Yes | No |
| Issue title insurance commitment | Yes (if also title agent) | Yes | No |
| Preside over closing execution | Required | Not permitted alone | Attends, does not conduct |
| Disburse funds from escrow | Yes | Only if attorney-supervised | No |
| Record documents on land records | Yes (or directs) | May record if authorized | No |
| Advise parties on contract terms | Yes (for own client) | No | Limited to brokerage duties |

This division means agents should never find themselves explaining deed language, calculating proration adjustments for tax purposes without attorney review, or instructing a title company to close without counsel present.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Connecticut Closings

Errors here do not merely embarrass you in front of clients — they create liability exposure, potential DCP complaints, and can unravel transactions weeks after recording.

**First, agents recommend or pressure buyers to skip hiring an attorney to save money.** Even when a buyer says they want to close without counsel, Connecticut custom and UPL rules make this functionally impossible for a standard residential sale. Agents who encourage this approach expose themselves to claims that they facilitated unauthorized practice. The buyer's attorney fee — typically $750 to $1,500 for a standard residential closing — is a non-negotiable cost of doing business in this state.

Second, agents prepare or modify the Residential Property Condition Disclosure Report (form available through the Connecticut Association of Realtors, CAR Form RPD) beyond checking factual boxes with the seller. The RPD itself is a statutory obligation under C.G.S. 20-327b, but agents who draft addenda, write legal disclaimers, or amend contract language based on disclosure findings are practicing law. The fix is simple: flag the issue for the attorney to draft appropriate language.

Third, agents confuse the role of the title company representative who appears at closing with someone who has authority to make legal determinations. When a title closer employed by a national underwriter attends to witness signatures or deliver the title policy, that person is not conducting the closing. If no attorney is present and the title closer executes the settlement, the closing may be voidable and the agent who arranged it becomes a target.

Fourth, agents fail to confirm attorney engagement early enough in the transaction timeline. Connecticut contracts — particularly the CAR/CBR Standard Form Real Estate Purchase and Sales Agreement — contain attorney approval contingency periods (typically five business days). If an agent does not ensure the buyer has retained counsel before this period lapses, the client loses a critical exit right without realizing it.

Fifth, agents from other states assume a mobile notary or settlement agent can substitute for an attorney. Connecticut does not recognize non-attorney settlement agents as valid closing officers for residential real estate transactions, period.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under C.G.S. 20-312 and the regulations promulgated by the DCP Real Estate Commission. A broker who permits agents to operate outside these boundaries risks license suspension, fines up to $25,000 per violation under certain DCP enforcement actions, and civil liability for client losses.

**Every brokerage operating in Connecticut should audit three things quarterly.** First, verify that every closed transaction file contains documentation of attorney involvement — the attorney's name, bar number, and engagement letter or closing statement. Second, review all contract addenda in pending files to confirm agents are not drafting legal language themselves. Third, confirm that [deadline tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) systems flag the attorney review period so no buyer accidentally waives this contingency through agent inattention.

Brokers should also maintain a written policy — included in their office procedures manual — stating that agents must never advise clients to proceed without counsel. This written policy becomes critical evidence if a DCP complaint is filed. The Connecticut Real Estate Commission has shown willingness to hold brokers accountable for systemic failures even when the individual agent made the error.

Training matters too. At minimum, brokers should dedicate one continuing education session per year to [common compliance failures that kill deals](/blog/where-deals-break-down), including the attorney requirement. New agents transferring licenses from title-company states need explicit onboarding about Connecticut's distinct approach.

## The Title Insurance Angle: How Policies Get Issued Without a Title-Company Closing

National title insurers — Fidelity, First American, Old Republic, Stewart — all operate in Connecticut. They issue policies through approved attorneys who serve as title agents. The attorney searches title (or orders a search from an abstractor), resolves defects, and certifies insurability. The underwriter then issues the policy based on the attorney's certification.

This structure means the title company is present in every transaction, just not as the entity conducting the closing. Agents should understand this distinction when explaining the process to out-of-state buyers or sellers who expect to walk into a title company branch office for signing. The closing happens at the attorney's office, or at a location the attorney designates, with the attorney presiding.

Some larger law firms in Connecticut maintain title company affiliations or subsidiaries. This is permissible provided the attorney maintains independent professional judgment and complies with Rules of Professional Conduct 1.7 and 1.8 regarding conflicts of interest. Agents who refer clients to attorney-title operations should disclose any affiliated business arrangements under RESPA Section 8 and Connecticut's own disclosure requirements.

## Liability Exposure When Things Go Wrong

If a Connecticut closing proceeds without proper attorney involvement and a defect surfaces later — a forged signature, an undisclosed lien, a deed with an incorrect legal description — the chain of liability extends beyond the parties. The agent who arranged the defective closing may face a DCP complaint under C.G.S. 20-320(a), which enumerates grounds for disciplinary action including "any conduct which constitutes or demonstrates dishonesty, incompetence, or untrustworthiness."

**The consequences scale from administrative to catastrophic.** A first offense might yield a consent order with continuing education requirements and a fine. Repeated violations can result in license revocation. Civil lawsuits for negligence or aiding UPL can produce damage awards that exceed standard errors-and-omissions insurance coverage, particularly if the policy contains a "professional services" exclusion that does not anticipate an agent acting beyond licensure scope.

Recording failures are another pain point. Connecticut's town clerk system requires documents to meet specific formatting and content standards. When non-attorneys prepare deeds that get rejected at recording, the gap between closing and actual recording can create priority problems — subsequent liens, lis pendens filings, or intervening judgments can attach to the property. The buyer's attorney is supposed to prevent this exact scenario.

## Practical Workflow for Agents in 2026

The smartest agents in Connecticut treat attorney involvement as a [checklist item that triggers early](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), not a closing-week scramble. Within 24 hours of a signed contract, confirm in writing that both buyer and seller have engaged counsel. Document this confirmation in your transaction file. If either party resists, explain the market reality rather than the legal mandate — most Connecticut attorneys will not permit their opposing counsel's client to appear unrepresented, and lenders will not fund without attorney-prepared documents.
