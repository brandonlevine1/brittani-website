## Why Utah's Fee Structure Catches Out-of-State Agents Off Guard

**If you moved to Utah from a state that collects transfer taxes at closing, reset your assumptions immediately.** Utah does not impose a state-level real estate transfer tax. That single fact shapes every closing cost estimate you hand to clients, every net sheet you build, and every REPC addendum you negotiate. Understanding Utah transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 is not optional — it is the difference between a clean closing and a dispute over who owes what at the settlement table.

The absence of a transfer tax does not mean agents can ignore fee disclosures. Utah's recording fees, set under Utah Code Title 17, Chapter 21a, still require precise calculations. Errors show up on the Closing Disclosure, frustrate title officers, and occasionally kill deals when a buyer's cash-to-close comes in short.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, and dollar amounts you need for 2026 transactions. It also flags the compliance failures that get agents into trouble with the Utah Division of Real Estate.

## Utah Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026: The Legal Framework

Utah eliminated any discussion of a state transfer tax decades ago. Unlike states such as Washington or Pennsylvania, Utah lawmakers have never enacted a documentary stamp tax or deed excise tax at the state or county level. The governing authority on what gets charged at recording is [Utah Code 17-21a-5](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title17/Chapter21A/17-21a-S5.html), which grants county recorders the power to set fees within statutory limits.

Under the 2026 schedule, county recorders may charge a base fee for the first page of any recorded document and a per-page fee for additional pages. The statute also authorizes a technology surcharge in counties that have opted in. Not every county charges the same amount — Salt Lake County, Utah County, and Washington County each post their own schedule, though most follow the statutory floor closely.

**The key takeaway is that agents must verify the exact fee schedule for the county in which the property is located, not assume a blanket statewide number.** This applies whether you are closing in Cache County or Iron County. A two-minute call to the recorder's office or a check of the county website eliminates guesswork.

Some counties assess a slightly higher base fee — Davis County, for instance, has historically charged $42 for the first page. Always confirm with the county recorder before preparing final estimates. Title companies pull current schedules automatically, but agents who generate preliminary net sheets bear responsibility for accuracy on those early estimates.

Recording fees are not prorated. The fee applies per document recorded, regardless of the property's sale price. A $250,000 condo closing and a $2.5 million estate sale pay the same recording fee for the same deed type.

## Who Pays What: Allocation Under the REPC

The Utah Association of Realtors (UAR) Real Estate Purchase Contract, commonly known as [UAR Form 25](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), governs how closing costs are allocated between buyer and seller. Section 8 of the REPC addresses settlement and closing costs.

By longstanding Utah custom, the buyer pays to record the warranty deed and the trust deed that secures their new mortgage. The seller pays to record any documents needed to clear title — reconveyances, lien releases, and payoff-related filings. These conventions are not mandatory by statute; they are negotiable terms within the contract.

**Agents who fail to clearly specify recording fee responsibility in the REPC create ambiguity that title companies cannot resolve without a contract amendment.** If Section 8 is silent or contradicts the addenda, expect a delay at closing while both sides argue over a $40 charge that should have been handled during negotiations.

| Cost Item | Typically Buyer | Typically Seller |
|---|---|---|
| Recording Warranty Deed | Yes | No |
| Recording Trust Deed | Yes | No |
| Recording Reconveyance | No | Yes |
| Recording Lien Releases | No | Yes |
| Title Insurance (Owner's Policy) | Negotiable | Often Seller |
| Title Insurance (Lender's Policy) | Yes | No |

These allocations can shift in competitive markets. During a seller's market, buyers sometimes agree to absorb all recording costs. The point is that the contract must state the allocation clearly.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Recording Fees

Five errors appear repeatedly in Utah transactions, and each one creates friction at the closing table or, worse, exposes agents to liability under the Utah Division of Real Estate's administrative rules (Utah Admin. Code R162-2f).

First, agents estimate recording fees based on another state's schedule. Transplants from California or Colorado sometimes quote clients $75 or $100 per document when Utah's statutory fee is $40 plus $2 per page. This overshoot erodes client trust when the Closing Disclosure shows a lower number and the client wonders what else was inflated.

Second, agents omit the per-page fee for multi-page deeds. A trust deed with a rider package can run six to eight pages. At $2 per additional page, that adds $10 to $14 — small, but enough to cause a discrepancy between the Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure, triggering a TRID tolerance violation for the lender.

Third, agents neglect to confirm whether the county has adopted its optional technology surcharge. Not every county charges it, but those that do add $3 to $5 per document. Missing this surcharge across multiple documents in a single transaction compounds the error.

Fourth, agents forget that each separate document requires its own recording fee. A transaction involving a warranty deed, a trust deed, and a subordination agreement means three separate fees, not one. Lumping them together on a net sheet misinforms the client.

Fifth, agents fail to update their templates annually. County recorders can adjust fees with notice, and the statutory cap has shifted in prior legislative sessions. Running 2024 numbers in a 2026 transaction is a credibility killer. ## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The Utah Division of Real Estate enforces licensee conduct under Utah Code 61-2f and the associated administrative rules. While a $4 recording fee miscalculation will not typically trigger a formal disciplinary action on its own, a pattern of inaccurate disclosures can constitute misrepresentation under R162-2f-401a.

If a client relies on your written estimate and is materially short at closing, the transaction can be delayed or canceled. A buyer who budgeted exactly to their approved cash-to-close figure and discovers an unexpected $200 in recording fees may not have the funds to close on time. In that scenario, the seller could invoke the contract's default provisions.

**Brokers can also face liability if they have not trained agents on accurate fee disclosures.** The Utah Division of Real Estate expects the principal broker to maintain supervisory systems that prevent recurring errors. Under R162-2f-401b, the broker's failure to supervise can result in fines or license restrictions independent of the individual agent's discipline.

Beyond regulatory exposure, agents face civil claims. A client who loses a property because the deal fell apart over a cash-to-close shortfall may pursue damages. The claim is straightforward: the agent's inaccurate estimate caused quantifiable harm.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Utah carry an affirmative duty under Utah Code 61-2f-401 to supervise all licensees operating under their license. Recording fee accuracy is one piece of that puzzle, but it touches multiple audit points.

Brokers should verify that every net sheet template used by their agents reflects the correct county's 2026 recording fee schedule. Templates should not be static PDFs that agents fill in once and never update. They should pull from a current source or, at minimum, carry a revision date that the broker can check during file review.

Transaction files should contain documentation showing the source of the recording fee estimate. This can be a printout from the county recorder's website, a title company's preliminary fee schedule, or a direct reference to Utah Code 17-21a-5. If the broker's file review reveals that agents are pulling numbers from memory, that is a training gap.

Brokers should also audit the REPC to confirm Section 8 is filled in completely. A blank or ambiguous cost allocation section signals that the agent either skipped the discussion with the client or assumed the title company would sort it out. Neither is acceptable under the Division's supervisory standards. Reviewing these details during a [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) prevents small oversights from compounding into deal-killing problems.

Finally, brokers should maintain a written policy on fee disclosure accuracy and include it in their office procedures manual. The Division expects documentation of supervisory systems during an audit — verbal instructions do not satisfy the standard.

## Keeping Your Fee Knowledge Current for 2026 and Beyond

Utah's legislature occasionally revisits Title 17 provisions, and county recorders have administrative authority to adjust fees within the statutory framework. Agents who want to stay ahead should bookmark their county recorder's fee page and check it at least once per quarter.

The UAR also publishes periodic updates to its forms, including revisions to the REPC that may affect closing cost language. Form 25 is not static — the 2026 version may carry different default language in Section 8 than the 2024 version. Agents using outdated forms risk contract enforceability issues and Division scrutiny.
