## Rhode Island Commission & Licensing Rules 2026: The Statutes That Govern Your Paycheck

The **Rhode Island commission & licensing rules 2026** landscape has shifted enough in the past eighteen months that agents who operated on autopilot through 2024 are now exposed to real enforcement risk. Between post-settlement buyer representation requirements, updated Department of Business Regulation (DBR) enforcement postures, and revised Rhode Island Association of Realtors (RIAR) forms, there is a lot of ground to cover. This article breaks down the statutory framework, specific forms, penalties for noncompliance, and the operational mistakes that put agents and brokers at risk.

Rhode Island real estate licensees operate under Title 5, Chapter 20.5 of the Rhode Island General Laws (RIGL 5-20.5), administered by the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation's Real Estate Division. The DBR has regulatory authority over licensing, discipline, and fee structures, while the Rhode Island Real Estate Commission advises on policy matters and hears disciplinary cases. Understanding where your obligations sit within this framework is not optional — it is the difference between collecting a commission and defending yourself before a disciplinary panel.

## Licensing Structure and Renewal Requirements

Rhode Island issues three categories of real estate licenses: salesperson, broker, and associate broker. Each carries distinct supervisory obligations and commission-handling rules. Salespersons must operate under a supervising broker and cannot receive compensation directly from a client or cooperating brokerage.

**License renewals occur biennially, and the 2026 renewal cycle requires 24 hours of approved continuing education (CE), including a mandatory 3-hour core course covering legislative and regulatory updates.** The DBR sets specific deadlines, and agents who fail to renew by the expiration date enter "inactive" status. An inactive licensee cannot legally perform any licensed activity, including receiving commission on transactions that close during the lapse period.

The renewal fee for salespersons is $100, and brokers pay $150. Late renewals within 30 days incur an additional penalty fee. Beyond 30 days, the license expires entirely, and the agent must reapply as a new licensee unless they petition for reinstatement under RIGL 5-20.5-9.

|---|---|---|---|

| Associate Broker | $150 | 24 hours (incl. 3-hr core) | Biennial |
| Broker | $150 | 24 hours (incl. 3-hr core) | Biennial |

## Commission Rules Under Rhode Island General Laws 5-20.5

RIGL 5-20.5-11 is the statute agents need to know by heart. It mandates that no licensee may bring a legal action to recover a commission unless a written agreement exists authorizing the commission. This is not a suggestion. Without a signed listing agreement, buyer representation agreement, or referral agreement, a Rhode Island court will not enforce your claim to compensation.

**Commission payments must flow through the supervising broker.** A salesperson or associate broker cannot accept commission directly from a seller, buyer, or cooperating brokerage. RIGL 5-20.5-16 explicitly prohibits this, and violations constitute grounds for disciplinary action including license suspension.

Post-NAR settlement rules have layered additional requirements onto this framework. Effective since August 2024, buyer agents must enter into a written buyer representation agreement before touring properties. The agreement must state the amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, and that compensation cannot be open-ended. Rhode Island agents use the RIAR Form 700 (Buyer Representation Agreement) to satisfy this requirement, and the form was updated in early 2025 to align with settlement terms.

> "No salesperson or broker shall be entitled to a commission or compensation for services rendered unless such services were performed under a written contract or agreement." — RIGL 5-20.5-11

## Forms That Keep You Compliant

Rhode Island agents work with a specific set of RIAR forms that establish commission, agency, and disclosure obligations. Getting the wrong form — or skipping one entirely — creates liability exposure that can unwind a deal or trigger a complaint.

The RIAR Form 100 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) governs the commission relationship between a seller and their listing broker. It specifies the commission rate, duration, and whether the seller authorizes offers of compensation to buyer agents. Under the 2025 revision, the cooperative compensation field is no longer pre-filled — it must be a deliberate, negotiated entry.

RIAR Form 700 (Buyer Representation Agreement) establishes the buyer-agent commission terms. RIAR Form 200 (Agency Disclosure) must be presented at the first substantive contact with a consumer, as required by RIGL 5-20.5-8. RIAR Form 300 (Dual/Designated Agency Consent) is required when a brokerage represents both sides. Failure to present the Agency Disclosure form before substantive contact violates state law and is one of the most common disciplinary triggers.

| Form | Purpose | When Required |
|---|---|---|
| RIAR Form 100 | Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement | Before marketing the property |
| RIAR Form 200 | Agency Disclosure | First substantive contact with consumer |
| RIAR Form 300 | Dual/Designated Agency Consent | Before dual agency representation begins |
| RIAR Form 700 | Buyer Representation Agreement | Before touring properties with buyer |
| RIAR Form 850 | Commission Disbursement Authorization | At closing, for split instructions |

The Commission Disbursement Authorization (Form 850) is often overlooked by newer agents. This form directs the closing attorney on how to split commission between cooperating brokerages and between a broker and their agents. Without it, closing attorneys may hold funds or require additional documentation that delays disbursement.

## Penalties for Noncompliance: Fines, Suspensions, and Deal Collapse

The Rhode Island Real Estate Commission has broad disciplinary authority under RIGL 5-20.5-18. Violations can result in license revocation, suspension, fines up to $1,000 per violation, mandatory education, and public reprimand. The Commission can also refer matters to the Rhode Island Attorney General for criminal prosecution in cases involving fraud or unlicensed activity.

**An agent operating without a valid license — even unknowingly due to a lapse — faces forfeiture of all commission earned during the inactive period.** Rhode Island courts have consistently upheld that an unlicensed person has no standing to recover compensation for brokerage services. The 2023 decision in *Marchetti v. Coastal Properties LLC* reinforced this, finding that a lapsed license voided the agent's right to a $28,000 commission.

Beyond individual penalties, brokers face vicarious liability for their agents' violations. RIGL 5-20.5-16(b) holds brokers responsible for ensuring their affiliated licensees comply with all statutory requirements. A single agent's failure to present the Agency Disclosure form can result in disciplinary action against the entire brokerage. This is why [broker-level auditing](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) is not just good practice — it is a legal necessity in Rhode Island.

Deal cancellation is another practical consequence. Buyers who discover they were not properly informed of agency relationships can void purchase agreements under the Rhode Island Residential Real Estate Sales Disclosure Act. Sellers who discover unauthorized dual agency can pursue contract rescission and damages.

## Common Mistakes Rhode Island Agents Make

**Mistake 1: Presenting the Agency Disclosure After Showing a Property.** RIGL 5-20.5-8 requires disclosure at "first substantive contact." Agents routinely interpret this as the first offer — it is not. If you discuss a buyer's financial situation, preferences, or motivation before presenting Form 200, you have already violated the statute. The DBR has issued formal guidance clarifying that a phone call discussing property criteria constitutes substantive contact.

**Mistake 2: Accepting a Commission Check Directly.** Newer agents occasionally receive a check from a cooperating broker made payable to them personally rather than their supervising broker. Depositing that check violates RIGL 5-20.5-16. The correct response is to return the check and request reissuance to the supervising broker's trust account.

**Mistake 3: Failing to Update the Buyer Representation Agreement When Compensation Changes.** If a buyer and agent initially agree to a 2.5% commission in the Form 700 but the seller offers 2% through a concession, the buyer owes the 0.5% difference. Agents who fail to communicate this before the buyer signs a purchase agreement face breach-of-fiduciary-duty claims and DBR complaints.

**Mistake 4: Operating on an Expired License During a Pending Transaction.** Agents who let their license lapse between contract execution and closing assume they can still collect commission because the deal was originated while licensed. Rhode Island law does not support this interpretation. You must hold a valid, active license at the time compensation is earned — which the DBR defines as closing.

**Mistake 5: Omitting the Commission Disbursement Authorization (Form 850) from the Closing Package.** This creates chaos at the settlement table. Closing attorneys in Rhode Island will not disburse commission without clear written direction, and the delay can cascade into problems with [deadline management](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) for related transactions.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry the supervisory burden in Rhode Island. Under RIGL 5-20.5-16, the DBR expects brokers to maintain systems that ensure compliance — not merely react when violations surface. The [Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation](https://dbr.ri.gov/divisions/real-estate) has increased audit activity since 2024, focusing specifically on buyer representation agreements and commission documentation.

**Every broker should audit the following on a transaction-by-transaction basis:** proper execution of Form 200 with a timestamp or notation confirming it was presented before substantive contact; a signed Form 700 for every buyer representation with compensation terms that match the closing statement; and a completed Form 850 in every closing file. Missing any of these creates a paper trail gap that exposes the brokerage during a DBR audit or a consumer complaint.

Brokers should also verify that every affiliated licensee's license is active and current. A simple calendar system that flags renewal deadlines 90 days out is insufficient. Brokers need to verify active status on the DBR's online lookup tool before every closing. The cost of one missed lapse — a forfeited commission plus a disciplinary proceeding — far exceeds the cost of a [systematic compliance check](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Commission accounting is another audit priority. Brokers must maintain records of all commission receipts and disbursements in their trust accounts for at least six years under RIGL 5-20.5-14. The DBR conducts random trust account audits, and discrepancies between closing statements and trust account records are treated as serious violations, potentially triggering a full forensic audit.
