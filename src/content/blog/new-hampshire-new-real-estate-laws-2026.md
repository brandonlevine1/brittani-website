## New Hampshire New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Changes That Hit Your Practice Hardest

The 2026 legislative session in Concord delivered a slate of changes that will reshape how New Hampshire agents handle disclosures, buyer representation, and transaction documentation. If you hold an active license under RSA 331-A, understanding these **New Hampshire new real estate laws 2026** updates is not optional—it is the difference between a clean closing and a Commission complaint. Several of these provisions carry effective dates staggered between July 1 and October 1, 2026, meaning some are already in force.

The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC) published updated guidance in its June 2026 bulletin, and the revised forms are now available through the Commission's portal. Agents who rely on outdated templates risk deal cancellation, disciplinary hearings, and personal liability exposure that errors-and-omissions insurance may not fully cover.

## Buyer Agency Agreements: The Written-First Mandate Under Amended RSA 331-A:16

**House Bill 482, signed into law on March 14, 2026, amends RSA 331-A:16 to require a written buyer agency agreement before any substantive real estate services are rendered.** The statute defines "substantive real estate services" broadly—it includes property tours, pricing discussions, and negotiation advice. This is the New Hampshire legislature's formal codification of obligations that mirror the NAR settlement terms adopted nationally in August 2024.

The NHREC released a model Buyer Agency Agreement template (NHREC Form 7.1) in May 2026. Agents are not required to use the Commission's exact form, but any alternative must contain the mandatory disclosures outlined in RSA 331-A:16(III), including a clear statement of compensation terms, duration of representation, and the buyer's right to terminate.

Failure to secure a signed agreement before substantive services now constitutes a violation of RSA 331-A:26(II)(e), which authorizes the Commission to impose fines up to $3,000 per occurrence, issue license suspensions, or mandate additional continuing education hours. Brokers should note that a supervising principal broker can also face discipline if the office lacks a compliance protocol.

## Updated Seller Disclosure Requirements Under RSA 477:4-c

Senate Bill 219 expanded the seller's property disclosure obligations effective July 1, 2026. The amended RSA 477:4-c now requires sellers to disclose known information about private well water testing results conducted within the prior three years, PFAS contamination awareness, and any history of flood damage not covered by FEMA-mapped zones. These additions reflect New Hampshire's ongoing groundwater concerns, particularly in the southern tier of the state.

The updated Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Form (NHREC Form 4.2) adds three new sections corresponding to these requirements. Agents listing properties must ensure their sellers complete the revised form—not the prior version—before marketing begins. The Commission's bulletin explicitly states that use of the superseded NHREC Form 4.1 after July 1 will not satisfy statutory requirements.

**If a seller refuses to complete the form, agents must document that refusal in writing using NHREC Form 4.2-R (Seller Disclosure Refusal Acknowledgment).** Simply noting the refusal in transaction notes is no longer sufficient. The buyer must receive a copy of Form 4.2-R within two business days of mutual contract execution.

| Disclosure Topic | Old Requirement (Pre-2026) | New Requirement (Effective 7/1/2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Private well testing | Disclose known contamination | Disclose all test results from prior 3 years |
| PFAS awareness | Not required | Must disclose if seller has knowledge of PFAS in area |
| Flood history | FEMA zone disclosure only | Any flooding event regardless of FEMA mapping |
| Seller refusal documentation | Written note in file | Formal Form 4.2-R delivered to buyer within 2 days |

## Agency Disclosure Timing: The "First Substantive Contact" Redefinition

The NHREC revised its administrative rules (Rea 303.02) in April 2026 to tighten the definition of "first substantive contact" for purposes of agency disclosure delivery. Under the prior interpretation, agents had until the first scheduled showing to deliver the Agency Disclosure Form (NHREC Form 2.1). The new rule moves that trigger earlier.

First substantive contact now includes any discussion—in person, by phone, via text, or through email—where the agent discusses pricing opinions, property-specific details, or the motivations of a buyer or seller. An open house sign-in does not trigger the requirement, but a follow-up conversation about a specific listing does.

This matters because many agents in New Hampshire previously treated the agency disclosure as a showing-day formality. That practice is now non-compliant. Agents who discuss a client's budget or timeline on a discovery call must deliver NHREC Form 2.1 before that conversation proceeds, or at minimum during that same interaction with written acknowledgment.

The consequences mirror those under the buyer agency mandate: fines, continuing education requirements, and potential license action through [RSA 331-A:26](https://www.gencourt.state.nh.gov/rsa/html/XXX/331-A/331-A-26.htm).

## Wire Fraud Advisory: A New Mandatory Disclosure Form

**Effective October 1, 2026, all New Hampshire transactions must include delivery of the Wire Fraud Advisory (NHREC Form 9.3) to both parties at or before contract execution.** This requirement stems from House Bill 617, which adds RSA 331-A:25-d to the licensee obligations chapter. New Hampshire joins a growing number of states mandating wire fraud warnings as a condition of licensee compliance rather than an optional best practice.

The form warns buyers and sellers about business email compromise schemes, instructs them to verify wire instructions by phone using independently sourced numbers, and requires signatures from both parties and both agents. Brokers who facilitate closings through title companies should confirm that the title company's own wire warnings do not substitute for NHREC Form 9.3—they do not.

Agents working transactions that close before October 1 are not retroactively required to provide the form, but the NHREC recommends voluntary adoption immediately. ## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

Based on patterns the Commission flagged in its June 2026 enforcement preview, several predictable errors will generate the bulk of early complaints.

**First, agents will use outdated disclosure forms.** The transition from NHREC Form 4.1 to Form 4.2 seems minor until a buyer's attorney spots the omission of PFAS and flood history sections. At that point, the buyer gains a statutory right to rescind under RSA 477:4-c(V), and the listing agent faces a Commission inquiry.

Second, agents will treat the buyer agency agreement as a formality to handle "before writing an offer" rather than before any substantive service. The statute is unambiguous—substantive services include touring properties. An agent who shows five homes before getting a signature has five potential violations, not one.

Third, agents will deliver the agency disclosure form at the wrong time. The reflex to hand it over at a showing appointment no longer aligns with the rule if prior phone conversations already crossed into substantive territory.

Fourth, agents will fail to obtain Form 4.2-R when sellers refuse disclosures. The old habit of noting "seller declined" in the MLS remarks or transaction file creates liability exposure that the new form requirement was designed to eliminate.

Fifth, agents will assume the wire fraud advisory is the title company's responsibility. It is not. The listing broker owns this obligation under RSA 331-A:25-d regardless of who handles closing.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce Internally

Principal brokers carry supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:12 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. The 2026 changes create specific audit obligations that brokers cannot delegate to individual agents without oversight systems.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Statutory Basis | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buyer agency agreements in file before first showing | Per transaction | RSA 331-A:16 | Fine up to $3,000 per agent per occurrence |
| Correct version of seller disclosure form (4.2) | Per listing | RSA 477:4-c | Buyer rescission right; broker discipline |
| Agency disclosure delivery timing documentation | Per client relationship | Rea 303.02 | Commission complaint; license action |
| Wire Fraud Advisory (Form 9.3) signed by both parties | Per transaction (after 10/1) | RSA 331-A:25-d | Fine; civil liability if loss occurs |
| Seller Refusal Form (4.2-R) when applicable | Per listing where seller declines | RSA 477:4-c(IV) | Buyer rescission; E&O claim |

Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint at three stages: listing activation, contract execution, and pre-closing. Waiting until closing to audit compliance means catching violations too late to cure them. The NHREC has indicated it will prioritize brokerage-level audits in Q4 2026, focusing on firms with more than ten active licensees.

## How These Changes Interact With Existing NH Commission Rules

The 2026 statutes do not exist in isolation. They layer on top of existing NHREC administrative rules governing advertising (Rea 305), trust accounts (Rea 306), and record retention (Rea 304.01, which requires brokers to maintain transaction files for three years). Agents must understand that the new forms increase the volume of required documentation per file, and the three-year retention clock applies to all of them.

One frequently overlooked interaction: the revised buyer agency agreement requirement under RSA 331-A:16 intersects with the existing dual agency consent rules under RSA 331-A:25-b. If a buyer client working under a signed buyer agency agreement becomes interested in the agent's own listing, the agent must obtain written dual agency consent on NHREC Form 3.1 before proceeding. The buyer agency agreement alone does not satisfy this requirement, even if it contains a dual agency clause.

Agents transitioning from other states should also review how New Hampshire's [approach to agency disclosure](/blog/wyoming-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) compares with frameworks in neighboring jurisdictions. The differences in timing and trigger definitions create traps for agents licensed in multiple states.

## The Cost of Non-Compliance: Real Numbers

The NHREC's disciplinary authority under RSA 331-A:28 provides a graduated enforcement framework. A first offense on a disclosure timing violation typically results in a letter of concern and mandatory continuing education (6-12 hours at the agent's expense). A second offense within 24 months escalates to fines ranging from $1,000 to $3,000. A pattern of violations—or a single violation that causes consumer harm—can trigger license suspension of 30 to 180 days.

Beyond Commission action, agents face civil liability. Under RSA 477:4-c(V), a buyer who does not receive required disclosures may rescind the purchase agreement and recover actual damages, including inspection costs, appraisal fees, and temporary housing expenses incurred in reliance on the contract. Sellers who lose deals due to their agent's disclosure failure have pursued E&O claims successfully in New Hampshire Superior Court.

The reputational cost compounds the financial exposure. NHREC publishes all formal disciplinary actions on its website, and a single public censure can undermine years of referral-based business development.
