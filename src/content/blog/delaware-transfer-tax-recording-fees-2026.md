# Delaware Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026: What Every Agent Needs to Know Before Closing

Getting the numbers wrong on a Delaware transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 transaction is one of the fastest ways to delay a closing, anger a client, or trigger a compliance review from your brokerage. Delaware's realty transfer tax framework is governed primarily by Title 30, Chapter 54 of the Delaware Code, and the rates are among the highest on the East Coast. Agents who treat these fees as an afterthought routinely underestimate closing costs on their settlement statements, creating last-minute scrambles that erode trust and, in some cases, kill deals entirely.

This post breaks down the exact rates, the forms involved, the most common agent errors, and what your broker should be auditing on every file.

## How Delaware's Transfer Tax Is Structured

Delaware imposes a realty transfer tax at both the state and county level each time real property changes hands for consideration. The state rate is set at 2.5% of the property's sale price or fair market value, whichever is greater, under 30 Del. C. Section 5401. Each of Delaware's three counties — New Castle, Kent, and Sussex — adds its own county transfer tax of 1.5%, bringing the standard combined rate to 4%.

Wilmington is the notable exception. The City of Wilmington levies an additional 1.5% city realty transfer tax under Chapter 22 of the Wilmington City Code, which means transactions within city limits carry a combined burden of 5.5%.

**The split between buyer and seller is presumed to be 50/50 under Delaware custom and standard contract language.** However, this is a negotiable term. Agents must confirm the allocation in the Agreement of Sale and ensure the settlement agent reflects it accurately on the closing disclosure.

| Jurisdiction | State Rate | County Rate | City Rate | Combined Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Castle County (outside Wilmington) | 2.5% | 1.5% | 0% | 4.0% |
| Wilmington | 2.5% | 1.5% | 1.5% | 5.5% |
| Kent County | 2.5% | 1.5% | 0% | 4.0% |
| Sussex County | 2.5% | 1.5% | 0% | 4.0% |

Exemptions exist but are narrow. Transfers between spouses, certain corporate reorganizations, and transfers to or from governmental entities may qualify under 30 Del. C. Section 5404. Agents should never assume an exemption applies without written confirmation from the settlement attorney.

## Recording Fees and Document Requirements

Recording fees in Delaware are separate from the transfer tax and are paid to the county Recorder of Deeds when documents are filed for public record. These fees cover deeds, mortgages, assignments, and any ancillary instruments that need to appear in the chain of title. Each county publishes its own fee schedule, and the amounts are typically modest compared to the transfer tax itself.

As of 2026, New Castle County charges a base recording fee for deeds that starts at approximately $60 for the first page, with additional per-page charges. Kent and Sussex counties follow a similar structure, though exact amounts differ and are updated periodically by county ordinance. Agents should verify the current schedule directly with the relevant Recorder of Deeds office before quoting fees to clients.

**Document formatting matters more than most agents realize.** The Recorder of Deeds in each county enforces minimum margin requirements, font size standards, and paper size rules. A deed submitted on non-standard paper or with insufficient margins will be rejected at the counter, which means the transaction is effectively unrecorded until corrected and resubmitted. The Delaware Association of Realtors standard forms generally comply, but custom-drafted deeds from out-of-state attorneys are a common source of rejection.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Delaware does not use a standalone state-mandated transfer tax return form the way some states do. Instead, the transfer tax is calculated and collected at settlement, and the payment is submitted to the Recorder of Deeds along with the deed. The deed itself — typically a [general warranty deed or special warranty deed](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) — must include the full consideration paid and the tax parcel identification number.

The Delaware Real Estate Commission requires agents to use or be familiar with several standard forms published by the Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR). The DAR Agreement of Sale form includes provisions for allocating transfer tax responsibility between buyer and seller. Agents listing or selling in Wilmington need to verify that the city transfer tax line item is included in the settlement statement, because many standard templates default to the 4% rate and omit the city surcharge.

For transactions involving an exemption from transfer tax, the settlement agent must attach a written affidavit to the deed at the time of recording, citing the specific statutory exemption under 30 Del. C. Section 5404. Without this affidavit, the Recorder of Deeds will assess the full tax.

Mortgage recording also requires attention. Delaware imposes no separate mortgage recording tax, but lenders must pay the standard recording fee, and the mortgage document must comply with county formatting rules. Agents working with buyers should confirm that the lender's closing instructions account for all recording fees, not just the transfer tax.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of getting transfer tax or recording fees wrong range from inconvenient to career-damaging. The most immediate risk is a rejected recording. If the deed cannot be recorded because the transfer tax was underpaid or the documents do not meet county standards, the buyer does not have a recorded interest in the property. That gap in the public record can expose the buyer to competing claims, lien priority issues, and title insurance complications.

**Underpayment of transfer tax triggers penalties and interest under 30 Del. C. Section 5410.** The state can assess additional tax plus a penalty of 5% per month on the unpaid balance, up to a maximum of 50% of the tax due. Interest accrues on top of the penalty. The Recorder of Deeds has the authority to refuse recording until the deficiency is cured, and in practice, that refusal is the enforcement mechanism that catches most errors.

From a licensing perspective, the Delaware Real Estate Commission can investigate agents who repeatedly cause settlement errors or misrepresent closing costs to buyers and sellers. Under 24 Del. C. Section 2912, the Commission has broad authority to discipline licensees for incompetence, negligence, or conduct that demonstrates a disregard for the interests of the public. Repeated miscalculation of transfer taxes on closing disclosures could support a negligence finding.

Deal cancellations are less common but do happen. When a buyer discovers at the settlement table that the actual transfer tax is thousands of dollars more than the agent quoted, the buyer may refuse to close. If the contract's financing or closing cost contingencies have already expired, the buyer may have no contractual exit, creating a dispute that benefits no one.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make

Five errors appear over and over in Delaware transaction files, and most of them are entirely preventable.

First, agents routinely quote the 4% combined rate for transactions inside Wilmington city limits, forgetting the additional 1.5% city tax. On a $350,000 sale, that oversight amounts to $5,250 in unaccounted closing costs. Buyers who discover this shortfall at the closing table rarely respond well.

Second, agents fail to confirm how the transfer tax split is documented in the Agreement of Sale. Delaware's 50/50 custom is strong, but it is not a statutory mandate. If the contract is silent or ambiguous, disputes arise at settlement over who owes what. The DAR Agreement of Sale form has a specific provision for this allocation — agents need to fill it in every time, without exception.

Third, many agents neglect to verify that the tax parcel identification number on the deed matches the county's records. A mismatched parcel number will cause the Recorder of Deeds to flag the document, delaying recording. This error is especially common in rural Sussex County, where parcels have been subdivided or consolidated in recent years.

Fourth, agents working with [transaction coordinators](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) sometimes assume the TC or settlement agent will catch every fee discrepancy. That assumption is dangerous. The agent's name is on the transaction, and the agent bears professional responsibility for the accuracy of cost estimates provided to clients.

Fifth, some agents overlook the fact that recording fees are due for every document filed, not just the deed. Mortgage documents, subordination agreements, and power-of-attorney instruments all carry separate recording fees. Omitting these from the estimated closing costs creates the same trust problem as misquoting the transfer tax.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers have a supervisory obligation under 24 Del. C. Section 2909 to ensure that their agents handle transactions competently. Transfer tax and recording fee compliance should be part of every brokerage's file review process.

**At minimum, brokers should verify three things on every closed file.** First, confirm that the transfer tax rate applied on the settlement statement matches the property's jurisdiction — 4% in most areas, 5.5% in Wilmington. Second, confirm that the buyer-seller split matches the allocation stated in the Agreement of Sale. Third, confirm that the deed was actually recorded by obtaining a copy of the recorded instrument with the Recorder of Deeds stamp.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer tax rate | Matches property jurisdiction | 4% applied to Wilmington transaction |
| Buyer-seller split | Matches Agreement of Sale | Contract silent on allocation |
| Deed recording | Recorded instrument on file | No stamped deed in closing package |
| Parcel ID | Matches county tax records | Parcel number mismatch or blank field |
| Recording fees | All documents accounted for | Only deed fees included, mortgage omitted |

Brokerages that skip this audit expose themselves to vicarious liability if a client suffers financial harm due to an agent's error. The Delaware Real Estate Commission's complaint process is public, and a pattern of transfer tax errors at a single brokerage will attract regulatory attention. Brokers should also maintain a current copy of each county's [recording fee schedule and closing procedures](/blog/where-deals-break-down) so agents have a reliable reference before quoting costs to clients.

Agents should check this resource at the start of each calendar year and whenever a new session of the General Assembly convenes.

County Recorder of Deeds offices in New Castle, Kent, and Sussex counties each maintain their own websites with current recording fee schedules. Bookmarking these pages and reviewing them quarterly takes less than fifteen minutes and prevents the kind of quoting errors that cost agents referrals and repeat business.
