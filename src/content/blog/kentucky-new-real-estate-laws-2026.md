## The Statutory Overhaul Agents Cannot Ignore

Kentucky new real estate laws 2026 represent the most significant regulatory update the Commonwealth has passed since the post-NAR settlement adjustments took effect in late 2024. The Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) and the Kentucky General Assembly moved aggressively during the 2026 legislative session, amending KRS Chapter 324 in ways that touch buyer representation, seller disclosures, commission transparency, and wire fraud prevention. Agents who treat these changes as optional will find themselves on the wrong end of license complaints before the year is over.

The effective dates are staggered. Some provisions kicked in January 1, 2026, while others phase in July 1 and October 1. That staggered rollout has already created confusion, and KREC's enforcement division has signaled it will not grant grace periods past the statutory deadlines.

This post breaks down the changes by category, identifies specific compliance errors already surfacing in the field, and outlines exactly what brokers need to audit in their offices.

## Buyer Representation Agreements: No More Informal Arrangements

**The single biggest operational shift for Kentucky agents in 2026 is the mandatory written buyer representation agreement requirement under the amended KRS 324.160.** Prior to July 1, 2026, Kentucky permitted agents to work with buyers under implied agency or oral agreements in many circumstances. That era is over.

The statute now requires execution of KREC Form 210-B (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) or KREC Form 211-B (Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) before the licensee provides any "substantive real estate services." KREC's interpretive guidance, published in Bulletin 2026-03, defines substantive services to include property tours, pricing analysis, and offer preparation.

Compensation terms must appear in a conspicuous section of the form and cannot reference MLS-published offers of compensation. The agreement must state a specific dollar amount or percentage that the buyer has agreed to pay the agent, consistent with the [Kentucky Real Estate Commission's published guidance](https://krec.ky.gov/).

## Seller Disclosure Revisions and Form 301 Updates

KREC Form 301 (Seller's Disclosure of Property Condition) underwent its first major revision in over a decade. The updated form, required for all listings entered into the MLS after July 1, 2026, adds 14 new disclosure questions covering flood history, prior insurance claims, known environmental hazards within a half-mile radius, and the presence of polybutylene plumbing.

**Agents who continue using the old Form 301 after the cutoff date are operating with a legally insufficient disclosure.** KREC has clarified that use of the superseded form does not satisfy the statutory requirement under KRS 324.360, meaning the buyer's rescission clock may never start running.

The delivery timeline also changed. Under the previous rule, sellers had to deliver the completed Form 301 before acceptance of an offer. The 2026 amendment requires delivery no later than three business days after listing activation, giving buyers access to disclosures before they write offers. This aligns Kentucky's approach with what [other states have implemented around seller disclosure timing](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026).

| Requirement | Pre-2026 Rule | 2026 Rule (Effective July 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form version | KREC Form 301 (2014) | KREC Form 301 (2026 revision) |
| Delivery deadline | Before offer acceptance | Within 3 business days of listing activation |
| Flood history questions | 2 questions | 5 questions |
| Environmental hazard disclosure | Not required | Required (half-mile radius) |
| Buyer rescission period | 5 days from receipt | 5 business days from receipt |

## Commission Transparency and the End of Blanket Offers

House Bill 247, signed by the Governor in March 2026, codified what many brokerages had already adopted voluntarily: the prohibition of blanket offers of buyer-agent compensation published through the MLS. The law amends KRS 324.111 to specify that any seller-offered concession to a buyer's agent must be negotiated within the purchase contract itself and disclosed on the settlement statement.

This means KREC Form 220 (Purchase Contract) has been updated to include Section 14(c), a dedicated field where the parties memorialize any seller concession toward buyer-agent compensation. Agents who bury this term elsewhere in the contract or attach it as a separate addendum risk having it deemed unenforceable under the statute.

For agents tracking their pipeline, this change adds a negotiation step that did not previously exist in many transactions. The days of checking a box on an MLS sheet are gone, and each deal now requires an explicit conversation about compensation structure with both the buyer and the listing agent. ## Wire Fraud Prevention: New Statutory Duties for Licensees

Kentucky joined a growing number of states imposing affirmative wire fraud prevention duties on licensees. The 2026 amendments to 201 KAR 11:250 require every agent coordinating a transaction to independently verify wire instructions through a secondary communication channel and document that verification in the transaction file.

The regulation specifies that email alone is insufficient as a verification method. Agents must confirm instructions via phone call, in-person meeting, or secure portal verification, and they must log the date, time, and method of confirmation. KREC Form 450 (Wire Verification Documentation) was created specifically for this purpose.

Failure to comply exposes agents to both regulatory discipline and civil liability. The statute creates a rebuttable presumption of negligence if an agent fails to complete verification and the buyer subsequently wires funds to a fraudulent account. This is not theoretical risk — Kentucky saw a 340% increase in real estate wire fraud attempts between 2023 and 2025 according to the Kentucky Department of Financial Institutions. ## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

KREC's enforcement division issued informal guidance in May 2026 identifying patterns of non-compliance it was already seeing in filed complaints. Five errors appeared repeatedly.

**First, agents are presenting properties to buyers before executing Form 210-B or 211-B.** Some agents mistakenly believe that an initial consultation or "meet and greet" showing does not constitute substantive services. KREC Bulletin 2026-03 explicitly states otherwise. Any in-person property tour triggers the requirement.

Second, listing agents are delivering the old version of Form 301 because their transaction management software has not been updated. The Commission has no exception for technology delays. The agent bears responsibility for using the correct form regardless of what their platform auto-generates.

Third, agents are omitting the specific dollar amount or percentage from buyer representation agreements, instead writing "to be determined" or "per MLS." Both phrases render the agreement non-compliant under the amended KRS 324.160, and KREC has stated it will treat vague compensation terms as a material violation.

Fourth, wire verification documentation is being completed after closing rather than at the time of verification. The regulation requires contemporaneous documentation. Backdated Form 450 entries discovered during an audit will be treated as falsified records under 201 KAR 11:121, which carries potential license suspension.

Fifth, agents are failing to provide copies of the signed buyer representation agreement to the buyer within 24 hours. The statute requires same-day delivery when signed in person, or next-business-day delivery for electronic signatures. Multiple complaints have already cited this specific timing failure.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokers carry supervisory liability under KRS 324.160(3) for every transaction file in their office.** The 2026 amendments expanded broker responsibility by requiring quarterly internal audits of at least 25% of active and closed transaction files. This is not best practice guidance — it is a regulatory mandate with specific audit criteria published in KREC Bulletin 2026-05.

The audit must verify the following elements in each reviewed file: presence of the correct-version buyer or seller representation agreement with compliant compensation terms, timely delivery of the updated Form 301, proper use of Section 14(c) in Form 220, and a completed Form 450 for any transaction involving wired funds.

| Audit Element | Required Documentation | Consequence of Absence |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer representation agreement | Form 210-B or 211-B (executed, dated) | Broker liability under KRS 324.160(3); possible fine of $1,000-$5,000 per violation |
| Seller disclosure delivery proof | Form 301 (2026 version) with delivery timestamp | Buyer rescission right; listing agent disciplinary action |
| Commission documentation | Form 220, Section 14(c) completed | Concession may be unenforceable; RESPA scrutiny |
| Wire verification | Form 450 completed contemporaneously | Presumption of negligence; potential civil liability |
| File delivery to buyer | Timestamped copy of agreement | $500 fine per occurrence under 201 KAR 11:121 |

Brokers who delegate this audit function to office managers or transaction coordinators should ensure those individuals are trained on the 2026-specific requirements. The Commission holds the broker personally accountable regardless of delegation.

## Penalties and Enforcement Timeline

KREC's enforcement posture for 2026 violations follows a tiered structure. First-time violations of the buyer representation agreement timing requirement carry a $1,000 fine and mandatory continuing education completion within 60 days. Second violations within a 24-month period trigger a $5,000 fine and a 30-day license suspension.

For disclosure form violations, the buyer's statutory rescission right is the immediate contractual consequence. Beyond deal cancellation, the listing agent faces a formal complaint, potential fine of $2,500, and a mandatory appearance before the Commission. Brokers whose offices show systemic non-compliance can face office-wide audits and fines up to $25,000.

Wire fraud verification failures carry the steepest penalties because of the consumer harm potential. A single documented failure resulting in buyer financial loss can trigger license revocation proceedings and personal civil liability exceeding the transaction value.
