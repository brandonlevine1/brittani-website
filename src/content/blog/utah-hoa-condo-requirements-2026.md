## Why Utah HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

Utah's residential real estate market has shifted heavily toward community-association-governed properties. Nearly 40 percent of homes sold along the Wasatch Front sit inside an HOA or condominium regime. That concentration makes the Utah HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026 a daily compliance issue, not a once-a-quarter specialty problem.

The legal framework spans multiple chapters of the Utah Code, ties directly to the standard Utah Association of Realtors (UAR) forms, and triggers consequences that range from deal cancellation to disciplinary action by the Utah Division of Real Estate. Agents who treat HOA disclosures as an afterthought are the same ones who end up on the wrong side of a complaint file.

This post breaks down the statutes, forms, timelines, common errors, and broker-level audit points you need to handle these transactions cleanly through the rest of 2026.

## The Statutory Framework: Utah Code Sections That Control HOA and Condo Sales

**Two primary statutes govern HOA and condominium disclosures in Utah, and confusing them is a frequent source of agent error.** Utah Code Title 57, Chapter 8 (the "Condominium Ownership Act") applies to condominium projects specifically. Utah Code Title 57, Chapter 8a (the "Community Association Act") covers planned communities governed by homeowners associations that are not condominiums.

Under Section 57-8-6.3, a seller of a condominium unit must provide or cause to be provided a resale certificate containing financial statements, reserve fund balances, pending special assessments, insurance coverage details, and any legal actions pending against the association. The parallel requirement for non-condo HOA communities lives in Section 57-8a-401, which mandates delivery of a similar association information packet to the buyer.

A third statutory layer comes from Utah Code 57-1-37, the general seller disclosure act. This section requires that sellers disclose material defects and conditions—including those arising from association governance—regardless of property type. Agents who rely solely on the HOA-specific chapters while ignoring 57-1-37 leave a gap that can later become a liability claim.

You can read the full text of the [Community Association Act on the Utah Legislature website](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title57/Chapter8A/57-8a.html).

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

The standard UAR Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC) addresses HOA-governed properties through built-in provisions and dedicated addenda. The most relevant forms for 2026 transactions include the following:

| Form | Purpose | When It Applies |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| UAR REPC (current edition) | Master contract with HOA provisions in Section 7 | Every residential transaction |
| UAR HOA Addendum | Specifies resale packet delivery deadline, cost allocation, buyer review rights | Any property inside an HOA or condo association |
| Resale Certificate (association-prepared) | Financial and legal status of the association | Required by statute for condos; standard practice for HOAs |
| UAR Seller Disclosure Form | Material facts including association disputes, special assessments, rule violations | Every sale under 57-1-37 |

**The HOA Addendum is not optional when the property is association-governed.** Omitting it from a transaction file doesn't eliminate the statutory obligation—it just removes the contractual mechanism that protects both parties and creates a clear audit trail.

Agents should confirm that the resale certificate is ordered immediately upon mutual acceptance. Most management companies need five to ten business days to compile it. Waiting until week two of a 14-day due diligence period leaves zero margin for review.

## Deadlines and Delivery: How Timing Works Under the REPC

Utah's approach differs from states that impose a fixed statutory review period. Instead, the buyer's right to review HOA documents is tied to the contractual due diligence deadline established in the REPC. If the parties agree to a 14-day due diligence window and the resale packet arrives on day 12, the buyer has two days to digest budgets, reserves, litigation, and rules before the deadline lapses.

This structure places an operational burden on listing agents. You must order the resale certificate early, confirm its delivery to the buyer's side, and track whether any extension is needed if the management company is slow. Missing this window doesn't just frustrate buyers—it can give them grounds to cancel with earnest money returned, because the contract language typically conditions the buyer's obligation to proceed on receipt and satisfactory review of HOA documents.

Buyer's agents carry responsibility too. If your client receives the packet and you fail to flag the review deadline, you may face a negligence claim if the buyer later discovers an undisclosed special assessment or pending lawsuit. The [importance of tracking deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) cannot be overstated in these transactions.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Liability, Cancellation, and Disciplinary Action

**Failing to deliver required HOA disclosures in Utah triggers a cascade of consequences that escalate quickly.** At the transaction level, the buyer can cancel the REPC and demand return of earnest money. This right is explicit in the contract language when the HOA Addendum is properly attached.

Beyond the deal itself, agents face exposure under the Utah Real Estate Licensing and Practices Act (Title 61, Chapter 2f). The Utah Division of Real Estate can investigate complaints for failure to disclose material facts, incompetence, or conduct that demonstrates negligence. Penalties include fines, mandatory education, license suspension, or revocation.

Civil liability adds another layer. A buyer who closes without receiving the resale certificate and later discovers a $15,000 special assessment or a structural deficiency lawsuit against the association has standing to sue both the seller and the agents involved. Utah courts have recognized that agents owe a duty of competent performance that includes ensuring statutory disclosures are completed.

| Consequence | Trigger | Who Bears Risk |
|-------------|---------|----------------|
| Earnest money refund / deal cancellation | Failure to deliver resale packet by deadline | Seller (and listing agent by association) |
| Division of Real Estate complaint | Negligence, failure to disclose material HOA facts | Listing agent, buyer's agent, or both |
| Civil lawsuit for damages | Buyer harmed by undisclosed assessment or litigation | Seller, agents, potentially the brokerage |
| E&O insurance claim | Malpractice allegation tied to missed disclosure | Agent's and brokerage's insurer |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Utah HOA Disclosures

Three to five errors account for the vast majority of compliance failures in HOA and condo transactions across Utah. Recognizing them is the first step toward eliminating them from your practice.

**First, agents frequently fail to order the resale certificate at contract acceptance.** They assume the seller or the management company will handle it unprompted. The result is a document that arrives days before or even after the due diligence deadline, compressing the buyer's review window and creating cancellation risk.

Second, listing agents rely on stale HOA documents from a prior listing period or from the seller's original purchase. Resale certificates have a limited shelf life—financial figures, pending litigation, and reserve balances change quarterly. Using outdated information is functionally the same as non-disclosure.

Third, agents confuse the seller disclosure form obligations with the HOA resale certificate requirement. These are separate statutory duties. Completing the UAR Seller Disclosure Form does not satisfy the resale certificate requirement under 57-8-6.3 or 57-8a-401. Both must be delivered.

Fourth, buyer's agents accept a partial packet—perhaps the CC&Rs and bylaws without the financial statements—and call it complete. The statute requires specific categories of information. A packet missing reserve study data or pending assessment details is not compliant.

Fifth, agents in dual agency or limited agency roles assume one disclosure satisfies both sides. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Utah Code 61-2f-401 for the acts of their affiliated agents. When an agent mishandles HOA disclosures, the brokerage's name appears on the complaint alongside the individual licensee. Proactive audit systems reduce that exposure significantly.

**Every brokerage operating in Utah should verify that transaction files include the HOA Addendum whenever the MLS data or public records indicate the property is association-governed.** This is a binary check—either the addendum is present or it is not. There is no gray area.

Beyond form presence, brokers should confirm that the resale certificate delivery date is documented in the file. A dated email, a signed receipt, or a timestamped upload to the transaction management system satisfies this requirement. Verbal confirmations do not.

Brokerages should also audit whether the due diligence deadline was extended in cases where the resale packet was delivered late. If a buyer proceeded past the deadline without ever receiving the packet, the file has a gap that could become a liability event months or years later. Implementing a [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) helps brokers catch these gaps before closing rather than after a complaint lands.

Finally, brokers should track which management companies in their market consistently deliver late or incomplete packets. Sharing that intelligence with listing agents at the time of listing—not at the time of crisis—prevents downstream problems.

## Handling Disputes Over HOA Document Content

Occasionally the resale certificate reveals information that surprises the buyer—a large upcoming special assessment, a lawsuit against the association, or restrictive rules the buyer finds unacceptable. When this happens, the buyer's options depend entirely on where the transaction sits relative to the due diligence deadline.

If the deadline has not passed, the buyer can cancel under the REPC's due diligence provision. This is clean and straightforward. If the deadline has passed because the agent failed to flag the issue in time, the buyer's recourse shifts from contract cancellation to potential claims against the agent for negligent performance.

Agents should never advise buyers on whether HOA financials are healthy or whether pending litigation is material. That analysis belongs to attorneys and CPAs. Your role is to ensure the documents are delivered, the timeline is respected, and the buyer is explicitly told to seek professional advice if they have concerns.
