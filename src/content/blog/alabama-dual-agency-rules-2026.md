## Understanding Alabama Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Alabama treats agency relationships with a specificity that catches agents off guard, particularly those who have transferred licenses from states with simpler disclosure frameworks. The **Alabama dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** carry real consequences: disciplinary hearings, transaction rescission, and malpractice exposure that your E&O policy may not fully cover. The Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) has enforced these provisions aggressively over the past three years, and 2026 shows no signs of a softer stance.

The governing law sits primarily in the Alabama Real Estate Consumers' Agency and Disclosure Act, codified at Alabama Code Title 34, Chapter 27, Sections 34-27-80 through 34-27-88. AREC also publishes mandatory forms that licensees must use verbatim — you cannot substitute brokerage-created alternatives for the state-prescribed documents. Agents who assume their MLS boilerplate covers agency disclosure obligations are the ones who end up in front of the Commission.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: The Statutory Distinction

**Dual agency in Alabama means a single licensee represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction, with the informed written consent of both parties.** The agent owes fiduciary duties to both sides simultaneously, which creates an inherent conflict. Alabama law acknowledges this conflict but permits the arrangement when proper disclosure and consent protocols are followed.

Designated agency operates differently. Under Section 34-27-83, a qualifying broker may designate one licensee to represent the seller and a different licensee within the same brokerage to represent the buyer. Each designated agent then owes full fiduciary duties exclusively to their assigned client. The qualifying broker, however, becomes a dual agent by operation of law and must remain neutral.

| Feature | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Number of agents involved | One licensee serves both parties | Two licensees, one per party |
| Fiduciary duty scope | Limited; cannot advocate for either side over the other | Full fiduciary duty to assigned client |
| Broker's role | Supervises the single dual agent | Broker becomes neutral dual agent |
| Confidentiality of pricing info | Cannot disclose either party's bottom line | Must protect each client's confidential information |
| Required form | AREC Form 5-5 (Dual Agency Consent) | AREC Form 5-4 (Designated Agency Agreement) |

This distinction matters because many agents in larger brokerages default to designated agency without formally documenting the arrangement. If the broker has not signed a designation in writing before substantive negotiations begin, the brokerage is operating in undisclosed dual agency — a violation that AREC treats seriously.

## The Forms You Must Use and When to Present Them

Alabama mandates a sequential disclosure process. At first substantive contact with a consumer, you must present AREC Form 5-2, the Agency Disclosure and Election form. This document explains the types of agency relationships available in Alabama and allows the consumer to acknowledge receipt. It does not itself create an agency relationship.

If dual agency becomes a possibility — say your listing client wants to write an offer on another listing within your book — you must then obtain signed consent using AREC Form 5-5 before proceeding with any negotiations on behalf of both parties. The timing here is non-negotiable. You cannot present this form at the closing table or even after an offer has been submitted. Consent must precede the dual representation.

For designated agency, AREC Form 5-4 must be executed by the qualifying broker, both designated agents, and both clients. The broker's signature confirms the designation and acknowledges the broker's neutral role. Missing any signature on this form creates an incomplete designation, which AREC interprets as non-compliance.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

**AREC does not treat agency disclosure violations as technicalities.** The Commission has the authority under Section 34-27-36 to impose fines up to $2,500 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional education hours. In recent years, AREC has published consent orders showing agents fined for presenting Form 5-5 after negotiations had already begun, even when both parties ultimately signed willingly.

Beyond regulatory penalties, the legal exposure is substantial. Either party to a transaction can seek rescission of the contract if they were not given proper dual agency disclosure before the representation began. Alabama courts have consistently held that undisclosed dual agency renders the agent's actions voidable at the client's election. This means a deal can unravel weeks or months after closing if a buyer or seller later learns they were in an undisclosed dual agency arrangement.

Malpractice claims arising from dual agency failures also tend to fall outside standard E&O coverage limitations. Many policies contain exclusions for intentional violations of licensing law, and AREC disciplinary findings can serve as evidence that the violation was knowing rather than inadvertent. Agents should review their E&O policy language specifically around regulatory non-compliance exclusions.

The [Alabama Real Estate Commission's website](https://arec.alabama.gov) publishes disciplinary actions quarterly — reading those summaries is the fastest way to understand what triggers enforcement.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Alabama

The first and most frequent error is conflating disclosure with consent. Presenting AREC Form 5-2 does not authorize dual agency. Agents who believe that initial disclosure form covers all scenarios end up acting as undisclosed dual agents the moment a buyer client expresses interest in their own listing. Two separate events must occur: disclosure of available relationship types, then specific consent to dual representation.

The second mistake involves timing. Agents wait until the offer is drafted or, worse, accepted before obtaining dual agency consent. The statute requires consent before the dual representation begins, not before closing. If you show your listing to a buyer you represent and begin discussing pricing strategy for an offer, you are already functioning as a dual agent. The form should be signed before that conversation.

Third, agents in designated agency arrangements share confidential client information with their in-office counterpart. The entire point of designated agency is that each agent maintains independent fiduciary duties. Discussing the seller's bottom-line number with the buyer's designated agent in the break room defeats the legal protection designated agency is supposed to provide. If that information sharing is later discovered, both agents and the broker face liability.

Fourth, qualifying brokers fail to execute the designation in writing. A verbal statement like "you handle the buyer, I'll handle the seller" does not satisfy Section 34-27-83. The written designation must exist, must be signed by the broker, and must be provided to both clients.

Fifth, agents operating as transaction licensees (a limited service role under Alabama law) assume they are exempt from dual agency rules. Transaction licensees still owe duties of honesty and disclosure. If a transaction licensee begins providing advice or advocacy to both parties, they have functionally created a dual agency that requires proper consent.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Qualifying brokers bear ultimate responsibility for agency compliance within their brokerage.** AREC holds brokers accountable even when an individual agent makes the disclosure error, because the broker's supervisory duty under Section 34-27-36(a)(19) requires reasonable oversight of all licensee activities.

Brokers should audit three things quarterly at minimum. First, verify that every transaction file contains a signed AREC Form 5-2 with a date that precedes the first offer or substantive negotiation. Second, for any in-house transaction where one brokerage represents both sides, confirm that either Form 5-4 (designated agency) or Form 5-5 (dual agency consent) is signed and dated before the first offer submission. Third, review email and text communication logs in designated agency transactions to ensure the two designated agents are not sharing confidential pricing or motivation information.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For | Risk if Missing |
|------------|-----------|------------------|-----------------|
| AREC Form 5-2 in file | Every transaction | Signed before first offer | AREC fine, rescission risk |
| Form 5-4 or 5-5 for in-house deals | Every in-house transaction | Dated before negotiations | Undisclosed dual agency violation |
| Confidentiality walls in designated agency | Quarterly spot-check | No cross-communication of pricing/motivation | Breach of fiduciary duty, malpractice claim |
| Broker signature on Form 5-4 | Every designated agency file | Broker personally signed | Incomplete designation, regulatory exposure |
| Agent training documentation | Annually | CE completion, internal training logs | Failure to supervise defense unavailable |

Brokers who manage teams or large offices know that [memory-based compliance fails at scale](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory). Systematic file review is the only defensible approach when AREC audits your office.
