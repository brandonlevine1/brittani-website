## Why Louisiana HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

Louisiana agents handling properties governed by homeowners associations or condominium regimes face a disclosure framework that blends state statute, LREC form requirements, and local association bylaws into a single compliance obligation. Understanding Louisiana HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026 is not optional — it directly affects your license, your client's right to rescind, and your broker's exposure to lawsuits. The stakes increased in 2025 when the Louisiana Real Estate Commission tightened its enforcement posture on disclosure deficiencies cited during audits.

More than 1.2 million Louisiana residents live in HOA or condo-governed communities, according to the Community Associations Institute. That ratio translates into a significant percentage of residential transactions touching association governance documents every single month. Agents who treat these disclosures as afterthoughts are the same agents who watch deals unravel three days before closing.

## The Statutory Framework: What Louisiana Law Actually Requires

Two primary bodies of law control HOA and condo disclosures in Louisiana. The Louisiana Condominium Act, codified at [Louisiana Revised Statutes Title 9, Sections 1121.101 through 1124.115](https://www.legis.la.gov/legis/Laws_Tit_Disp.aspx?lawType=RS), governs condominium sales specifically. The Louisiana Homeowners Association Act, found at La. R.S. 9:1141.1 et seq., covers planned communities and subdivisions operating under recorded covenants.

**Under La. R.S. 9:1124.111, a seller of a condominium unit must furnish the buyer with a resale certificate containing specified financial and governance information before the sale can close.** This certificate must include current regular and special assessments, the association's most recent financial statement, the declaration of condominium, bylaws, and any pending litigation involving the association. The statute also mandates disclosure of any capital expenditures anticipated within the current or following fiscal year.

For HOA properties, La. R.S. 9:1141.8 imposes a parallel obligation. Sellers must disclose the existence of the association, provide governing documents, and reveal any outstanding assessments or violations tied to the property. The distinction matters: condo disclosures carry more prescriptive statutory detail, while HOA disclosures rely more heavily on the purchase agreement's terms and LREC forms.

## LREC Forms and Document Numbers Agents Must Know

The Louisiana Real Estate Commission publishes standardized forms that agents are expected to use unless their brokerage has approved alternatives reviewed by legal counsel. For HOA and condo transactions, the following forms are directly relevant in 2026:

| Form Number | Form Name | Purpose |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| LREC Form 2-0 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Contains HOA/condo disclosure and document delivery provisions in Sections 11-12 |
| LREC Form 2-1 | Property Disclosure Document | Requires seller to identify HOA/condo affiliation, fees, and known violations |
| LREC Form 6-0 | Condominium/PUD Addendum | Specifies document delivery deadlines, buyer review period, and cancellation rights |
| LREC Form 9-0 | Amendment to Purchase Agreement | Used to extend deadlines when association documents arrive late |

**Form 6-0, the Condominium/PUD Addendum, is where most compliance failures originate.** Agents who fail to attach this addendum to transactions involving governed properties leave their buyers without a contractually defined review period. Without it, disputes about what was delivered and when become impossible to resolve cleanly.

The LREC also expects agents to verify that sellers have requested the resale certificate from the association management company early enough to meet contractual deadlines. Most Louisiana management companies require 7 to 14 business days to produce a resale packet, and some charge rush fees exceeding $300 for expedited delivery.

## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of noncompliance fall into three categories: regulatory, contractual, and civil. Each carries its own timeline and severity, and all three can stack.

From a regulatory standpoint, the Louisiana Real Estate Commission has authority under La. R.S. 37:1455 to suspend, revoke, or fine licensees who fail to disclose material facts or who neglect required documentation. A disclosure failure involving an HOA or condo can be characterized as a violation of Louisiana's License Law, specifically La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(1), which prohibits "incompetency or unworthiness." Fines can reach $5,000 per violation, and repeat offenses trigger mandatory suspension hearings.

Contractually, the buyer's right to cancel is the most immediate risk. If the resale certificate is never delivered or is delivered after the contractual review period lapses, the buyer may cancel and recover earnest money deposits in full. Agents representing sellers who refused to order the resale packet in a timely manner often find themselves explaining this failure to an angry client who just lost a $400,000 deal.

Civil liability runs deeper. Louisiana courts have held that agents who fail to disclose known association problems — pending special assessments, construction defects subject to litigation, or restrictive covenants that materially affect use — can be held personally liable for damages under La. C.C. art. 2545 (redhibition) and general negligence theories. The damages are not capped at the commission earned; they can include the full cost of undisclosed assessments, lost equity, and attorney fees.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA and Condo Disclosures

Five errors appear repeatedly in LREC complaint files and closing attorney malpractice cases involving Louisiana HOA and condo transactions.

**First, agents fail to attach Form 6-0 to the purchase agreement at all.** This is the most common and most easily preventable mistake. If the property is governed by an association and the agent uses a standard LREC Form 2-0 without the condominium/PUD addendum, the contract lacks the buyer's document review period. The deal proceeds without a safety valve, and liability shifts squarely onto the listing agent and their broker.

Second, agents confuse the seller's Property Disclosure Document (Form 2-1) with the resale certificate required under the Condominium Act. These are two different obligations. Form 2-1 captures what the seller personally knows. The resale certificate is an official document produced by the association or its management company and contains financial data the seller may not have. Both are required. One does not substitute for the other.

Third, agents fail to calendar the document delivery deadline. Louisiana purchase agreements typically specify that association documents must be delivered within a set number of days after acceptance. Missing this deadline gives buyers leverage to renegotiate or walk, and it exposes the listing agent to a claim of negligent transaction management. Agents who rely on memory instead of systems will eventually [miss a deadline that kills a deal](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Fourth, agents accept verbal assurances from sellers that "there's no HOA" without verifying the title or subdivision plat. Many older Louisiana neighborhoods have dormant covenants or deed restrictions that technically constitute an association obligation. A 1972 covenant recorded in Jefferson Parish does not stop being enforceable because the current seller is unaware of it.

Fifth, agents do not review the resale certificate themselves before passing it to the buyer. This means they miss red flags — depleted reserve funds, pending litigation, or special assessments already approved but not yet invoiced — that a buyer's agent should be flagging. Your [role as an agent involves more than forwarding PDFs](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory); it requires substantive review and client advisement.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(25) for the acts of their licensees. A broker who fails to implement systems ensuring HOA and condo compliance is not shielded by claiming ignorance of an agent's omission. The LREC has sanctioned brokers directly for pattern failures in disclosure compliance, even when the individual agent received the primary fine.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Risk If Missing |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Form 6-0 attached | Every HOA/condo file must include the addendum | Buyer cancellation, LREC complaint |
| Resale certificate in file | Must be the actual association-produced document, not seller's summary | Redhibition claim, license action |
| Delivery date documented | Written proof of when buyer received documents | Earnest money dispute, litigation |
| Review period expiration tracked | Calendar entry or system alert showing deadline | Missed objection window, liability transfer |
| Fee allocation in contract | Clear notation of who pays for resale packet | Commission dispute, closing delay |

Brokers should run quarterly file audits specifically targeting HOA and condo transactions. Pull every file flagged as a governed property and verify that Form 6-0 is present, the resale certificate is in the documents tab, and the delivery date is memorialized in writing. If your brokerage handles more than a handful of these transactions monthly, manual tracking is a recipe for missed items. This is exactly where [pipeline management systems](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) prove their value.

Brokers should also mandate that agents request the resale certificate within 48 hours of contract execution. Waiting until week two of a 30-day closing is how packets arrive late and buyers exercise cancellation rights at the worst possible moment.
