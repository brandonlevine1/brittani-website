## The Framework Behind Rhode Island Teams & Supervision Rules 2026

Rhode Island's real estate industry has seen a steady rise in team-based production models over the past five years, and the regulatory framework has tightened in response. Understanding **Rhode Island teams & supervision rules 2026** is no longer optional for agents who want to avoid fines, license actions, or collapsed deals. The Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation (DBR), which oversees the Real Estate Section, has made supervisory compliance a top enforcement priority heading into this year.

The statutory backbone sits in Rhode Island General Laws Title 5, Chapter 20.5 — the Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons Act. This chapter defines the broker-salesperson relationship, sets advertising standards, and establishes penalties for non-compliance. Every team arrangement in the state must fit within this statutory framework, regardless of what the team calls itself internally.

Rhode Island does not issue a separate "team license." A team is simply a marketing arrangement within an existing brokerage. That distinction matters because it means the designated broker retains full supervisory authority and liability for every transaction a team member touches.

## How Rhode Island Defines Teams and Supervision Authority

**The state treats a "team" as two or more licensees who market collectively under a shared identity within a single brokerage.** There is no separate registration process for teams at the DBR level, but the broker must maintain records showing which licensees belong to which team and how supervision is delegated.

Under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-10, the designated broker is responsible for the supervision of all licensees affiliated with the brokerage. This responsibility cannot be delegated to an unlicensed team leader or administrative assistant. If a team leader holds only a salesperson license, they cannot exercise supervisory authority over other salespersons — only a broker or associate broker can perform that function.

The DBR has clarified through administrative guidance that team leaders who are salespersons may coordinate transactions, assign tasks, and manage marketing, but they cannot review files for regulatory compliance on behalf of the broker. That duty stays with the broker or a designated associate broker.

## Advertising and Team Name Requirements

Rhode Island's advertising rules for teams are among the more prescriptive in New England. R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-17 requires that all advertising — print, digital, signage, and social media — prominently display the licensed brokerage name. The team name cannot appear more prominently than the brokerage name, and it cannot create the impression that the team operates as an independent entity.

| Advertising Element | Requirement | Common Violation |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name | Must appear at least as large as team name | Team name in headline, brokerage in fine print |
| Individual agent name | Must include license status (salesperson or broker) | Omitting "salesperson" or using unlicensed title |
| Team phone/email | Must route to brokerage-monitored system | Using personal Gmail without broker access |
| Social media profiles | Must include brokerage name in bio/about section | Team-only branding on Instagram or Facebook page |
| Yard signs | Brokerage name equal or greater prominence | Small brokerage logo at bottom of sign |

This table reflects the most frequent advertising deficiencies cited in DBR enforcement actions. Agents who operate under a team name without proper brokerage attribution risk a formal complaint, corrective action orders, and fines up to $1,000 per violation under the statute.

The DBR also watches for the use of terms like "Realty," "Real Estate," or "Properties" in team names. If a team name could be confused with a separate brokerage, the DBR will flag it during routine audits or upon consumer complaint. Teams should clear their proposed name with the broker and confirm it does not conflict with any registered brokerage in the state.

## Agency Disclosure and Transaction Forms for Teams

Rhode Island mandates the use of specific agency disclosure forms at the outset of a brokerage relationship. The primary form is the **Agency Disclosure (Form 200)**, which must be presented to buyers and sellers before any substantive real estate discussions occur. Team members often stumble here because they assume the team leader's initial disclosure covers all subsequent team interactions — it does not.

Each licensee who engages in substantive discussion with a client must confirm that the client received the agency disclosure. If a buyer initially meets with one team member and is later handed off to another, the second agent must verify the disclosure is still accurate and that the client understands who represents them. The [Rhode Island Real Estate Commission's regulations](https://dbr.ri.gov/divisions/real-estate/) make clear that the agency relationship runs between the client and the brokerage, not the individual agent or team.

**When teams handle both sides of a transaction — representing the buyer and the seller — dual agency consent must be documented using the prescribed disclosure language.** Rhode Island permits disclosed dual agency, but only with informed written consent from all parties. Teams that inadvertently create undisclosed dual agency face some of the most severe penalties the DBR imposes, including license revocation.

The state also requires a **Seller's Disclosure Form** and the use of the **Listing Agreement (Form 100)** for residential transactions. These forms must bear the designated broker's name and the brokerage information, not merely the team name. Filing forms under a team name without brokerage identification is a violation.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Under Rhode Island Team Rules

**Mistake one: assuming the team leader can sign off on compliance reviews.** Only the designated broker or an authorized associate broker can fulfill the supervisory review obligation. A salesperson team leader who reviews files and signs off on disclosures is acting outside their license scope, which exposes the entire brokerage to liability.

Mistake two: failing to update agency disclosures when team members change mid-transaction. Rhode Island's consumer protection framework requires clients to know who is working on their behalf. When a team member goes on vacation, leaves the brokerage, or is reassigned, the client must receive updated information and re-confirm their agency relationship. Skipping this step has led to disciplinary actions and deal rescissions.

Mistake three: using unlicensed assistants for activities that require a license. Rhode Island law is clear that anyone who negotiates, shows property for the purpose of inducing a sale, or provides pricing opinions must hold an active license. Teams frequently push administrative boundaries by having unlicensed staff conduct open houses or respond to buyer inquiries about pricing — both violations under Section 5-20.5-3.

Mistake four: maintaining separate trust accounts or escrow arrangements under the team name. All client funds must flow through the brokerage's designated escrow account. Teams cannot operate shadow accounts, even for earnest money convenience. This is a serious trust fund violation that can result in immediate license suspension.

Mistake five: inadequate record retention. The DBR requires brokerages to maintain transaction records for a minimum of six years. Teams that keep files on personal drives, in team-only cloud folders without broker access, or in formats the broker cannot readily audit are creating a compliance gap that surfaces during DBR investigations.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Rhode Island carry the heaviest regulatory burden when it comes to teams. The DBR holds the designated broker personally accountable for supervisory failures, even when the broker was unaware of the violation. Ignorance is not a defense under the statute.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | Monthly | Team social media, websites, print materials, signage |
| Agency disclosures (Form 200) | Every transaction | Proper delivery, client signatures, correct brokerage name |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | All funds in brokerage escrow, no team-held funds |
| Licensee status verification | Quarterly | All team members hold active, current licenses |
| Record retention and access | Quarterly | Broker can access all team files within 24 hours |
| Dual agency documentation | Every applicable transaction | Written consent from both parties before substantive work |

Brokers should also confirm that team compensation structures do not create undisclosed referral arrangements. If one team member receives a fee for sending a client to another team member or an outside vendor, that arrangement must comply with RESPA and Rhode Island's own anti-kickback provisions in the licensing statute.

The process of managing these audits across multiple teams is where most brokerages fall behind — especially those running lean operations. Systematic tracking of deadlines, form submissions, and advertising changes requires either dedicated compliance staff or technology that flags gaps before they become violations. ## Penalties for Non-Compliance in Rhode Island

The DBR has a graduated enforcement approach, but even first offenses can carry meaningful consequences. Fines under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-24 range from warnings and corrective orders for minor advertising violations up to $1,000 per offense for substantive breaches. Repeated violations or trust fund mishandling can trigger license suspension or revocation proceedings.

Beyond regulatory penalties, non-compliance creates civil liability. A buyer or seller who discovers undisclosed dual agency, unlicensed activity, or misrepresentation by a team member can pursue contract rescission and damages. Rhode Island courts have permitted rescission of purchase agreements where the agency disclosure was deficient, which means the deal itself can unravel months after closing.

**Brokers also face personal exposure.** If the DBR determines that a broker failed to supervise a team member who committed a violation, the broker's license is at risk independently of whatever happens to the offending agent. This creates a strong incentive structure for brokers to implement auditable supervision protocols rather than relying on trust alone.

For agents working within teams, the individual risk is real too. License suspension means lost income, and Rhode Island's continuing education reinstatement requirements add cost and delay.
