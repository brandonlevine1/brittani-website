The pace of regulatory change in Wisconsin real estate has accelerated sharply heading into the second half of 2026. Between revised disclosure timelines, tightened buyer agency rules, and new earnest money deposit windows, the Wisconsin new real estate laws 2026 cycle represents the most significant overhaul since the state adopted its current Offer to Purchase forms in 2011. Agents who treat these updates as minor tweaks risk deal cancellations, Department of Safety and Professional Services (DSPS) complaints, and personal liability exposure.

This post breaks down the specific statutory and administrative code changes, identifies the forms that have been revised, and flags the exact compliance failures that are already generating enforcement activity.

## Wisconsin New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Core Statutory Changes

Three primary legislative and administrative actions drive this year's changes. Senate Bill 247, signed into law in March 2026, amends Wis. Stat. Chapter 709 (Disclosures) and Wis. Stat. 452.133 (Buyer Agency). Simultaneously, the Wisconsin Real Estate Examining Board (REEB) finalized revisions to Wis. Admin. Code Chapters REEB 18 and REEB 24, effective June 1, 2026.

The Wisconsin REALTORS Association (WRA) responded by releasing updated versions of several WB-series forms. The most consequential are the revised WB-7 Real Estate Condition Report, the new WB-36 Buyer Agency/Tenant Representation Agreement, and amendments to the WB-11 Residential Offer to Purchase. Each carries new mandatory language that older form versions lack entirely.

**Agents still using pre-June 2026 form versions are operating outside compliance.** DSPS has already issued guidance confirming that use of outdated WB-series forms after June 1 constitutes a violation of Wis. Admin. Code REEB 24.07, which governs the use of approved forms.

## Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements Under WB-36

The most visible change for buyer agents is the mandatory pre-showing agreement. Under revised Wis. Admin. Code REEB 24.055, a signed WB-36 Buyer Agency Agreement must be executed before an agent conducts any in-person showing for a prospective buyer. The rule mirrors post-NAR settlement practices but goes further than the national standard by specifying exact triggering events.

Open houses do not trigger the requirement, provided the agent hosting is the listing agent or a member of the listing team. However, if a buyer's agent accompanies their client to an open house, the WB-36 must already be in place. Virtual tours shared via MLS link are similarly exempt, but agent-narrated video walkthroughs conducted over Zoom or FaceTime do trigger the rule.

The WB-36 itself now contains a mandatory compensation disclosure section in Lines 47-63 that specifies the buyer's maximum financial obligation, any seller-paid offsets, and the agent's agreement to credit cooperative compensation if offered. Leaving these lines blank is not an option—REEB's June 2026 guidance memo explicitly states that incomplete WB-36 forms do not satisfy the pre-showing requirement.

## Revised Disclosure Timelines and the Updated WB-7

Wisconsin's Real Estate Condition Report has always been a seller obligation under Wis. Stat. 709.01-709.08. The 2026 amendments tighten the delivery window and expand the scope of required disclosures. Sellers must now deliver the updated WB-7 to buyers no later than the time of offer presentation—not merely before acceptance, as previously interpreted.

The updated WB-7 adds three new disclosure categories: known flooding history (even if outside FEMA zones), prior insurance claims related to mold or water intrusion, and awareness of any pending special assessments. These additions appear in Section 8 of the revised form, Lines 142-168.

| Disclosure Requirement | Old Rule (Pre-June 2026) | New Rule (June 2026+) |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery Timing | Before acceptance | At or before offer presentation |
| Flooding History | Only FEMA zone disclosure | Any known flooding, regardless of zone |
| Insurance Claims | Not required | Mold/water claims in past 5 years |
| Special Assessments | Only if levied | Pending or proposed assessments |
| Buyer Rescission Window | 2 business days from receipt | 2 business days from receipt (unchanged) |

**Listing agents bear responsibility for ensuring the updated WB-7 is completed and delivered on time.** If a buyer receives an outdated form version, the rescission clock under Wis. Stat. 709.02(1) does not begin running, effectively giving the buyer an indefinite right to cancel.

## Earnest Money Deposit Changes Under REEB 18.09

The revised Wis. Admin. Code REEB 18.09 shortens the earnest money deposit window from three business days to two business days following binding acceptance. This change alone has already caused problems for agents operating in rural markets where physical check delivery adds logistical friction.

The rule applies to the broker holding the trust account—meaning the listing broker's office in most Wisconsin transactions. If the earnest money is not deposited within the two-business-day window, the broker must notify both parties in writing within 24 hours and document the reason for delay. Failure to do so constitutes a trust account violation subject to DSPS disciplinary action.

Electronic payment platforms that deposit funds directly into the broker's trust account satisfy the timing requirement as of the timestamp on the transfer confirmation. Wire transfers count from the time funds are received and confirmed by the bank, not the time the buyer initiates the wire. For agents managing [earnest money compliance in Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026), this distinction matters enormously when deadlines fall on a Friday afternoon.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance vary by violation type, but none are trivial. DSPS enforcement actions under Wis. Stat. 452.17 range from formal reprimands to license suspension, with fines up to $1,000 per violation for individual agents and $5,000 per violation for brokerages.

Beyond regulatory penalties, the transactional consequences hit faster. A buyer who never received a compliant WB-7 retains rescission rights indefinitely under Wis. Stat. 709.02(1). An unsigned or incomplete WB-36 means the buyer agent has no enforceable compensation agreement—creating personal liability if the agent later claims a commission. Earnest money deposit violations can trigger automatic release of funds to the buyer under the WB-11 default provisions.

| Violation | Regulatory Consequence | Transactional Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Outdated WB-7 used | DSPS investigation, fine up to $1,000 | Buyer retains indefinite rescission right |
| No WB-36 before showing | Formal reprimand or fine | No enforceable compensation agreement |
| Late earnest money deposit | Trust account violation, potential suspension | Buyer may demand return of funds |
| Incomplete WB-36 Lines 47-63 | Does not satisfy REEB 24.055 | Agent cannot enforce compensation terms |
| Failure to disclose dual agency timing | Wis. Stat. 452.135 violation | Transaction voidable by either party |

Civil liability compounds the regulatory risk. Wisconsin courts have consistently held that statutory disclosure failures create a presumption of damages in buyer lawsuits, shifting the burden to the agent to prove the buyer suffered no harm.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

Five specific errors have emerged in the first weeks since the June 2026 effective date, based on early DSPS complaint data and WRA member advisories.

**First, agents are using the old WB-36 form from 2024 that lacks the compensation disclosure section.** The 2024 version does not contain Lines 47-63 and therefore cannot satisfy the pre-showing requirement under REEB 24.055. Agents must verify they are pulling the June 2026 revision from their form provider.

Second, listing agents are delivering the WB-7 at acceptance rather than at offer presentation. The distinction seems minor until a buyer's attorney argues that the rescission clock never started. This single timing error has already generated at least two reported deal collapses in the Milwaukee metro area.

Third, buyer agents are treating open house attendance as a showing when the listing agent is present, unnecessarily requiring WB-36 execution before the buyer walks through the door. While conservative compliance is rarely penalized, this practice is creating friction with unrepresented buyers who feel pressured to sign agreements before they have chosen an agent.

Fourth, brokers are failing to update their trust account procedures to reflect the two-business-day deposit window. Offices that previously batched deposits on Tuesdays and Thursdays now risk falling outside the deadline for Friday acceptances.

Fifth, agents are not documenting the exact time of offer presentation, which matters because the WB-7 delivery obligation now attaches to that moment. Without a timestamped record, disputes about whether the disclosure was timely become he-said-she-said arguments that DSPS resolves against the agent.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers bear heightened responsibility under Wis. Stat. 452.14 for ensuring their agents operate within current law. The 2026 changes demand specific operational audits that many offices have not yet implemented.

**Every brokerage should audit its form library immediately to confirm that all WB-series templates reflect the June 2026 revisions.** Transaction management software that auto-populates forms must be updated at the platform level—individual agents cannot be trusted to manually verify form versions on every transaction.

Brokers must also revise their trust account deposit procedures to confirm two-business-day compliance. This means examining the actual deposit timestamps, not just the date the check was received at the office. DSPS auditors will request bank-stamped deposit receipts showing the date and time funds cleared into the trust account.

Training documentation matters. Under REEB 17.08, brokers must maintain records showing that agents received instruction on regulatory changes. A signed acknowledgment form for each agent—dated before their next transaction—creates an affirmative defense if a single agent's error triggers a DSPS investigation of the brokerage.

A single compromised wire instruction exposes the brokerage to both regulatory action and civil damages.
