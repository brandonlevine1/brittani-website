## Understanding West Virginia Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on a closing statement is one of the fastest ways to lose credibility with clients and title companies alike. The **West Virginia transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026** dictate exactly how much money changes hands at the recording window, and agents who misquote these figures create last-minute scrambles that delay closings or, worse, kill deals outright. This guide breaks down the statutory framework, the forms you must file, and the consequences of getting it wrong.

West Virginia imposes a real estate transfer excise tax under [West Virginia Code Chapter 11, Article 22](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/wvcode/code.cfm?chap=11&art=22). The tax applies to every deed, contract, or instrument that conveys title to real property for consideration. County clerks act as the collection point—no payment, no recording.

## The Rate Structure: State and County Excise Taxes

West Virginia's transfer tax is actually two separate levies that stack on top of each other. Understanding each component prevents calculation errors that surface at the closing table.

| Component | Rate | Authority | Collected By |
|-----------|------|-----------|--------------|
| State excise tax | $1.10 per $500 of consideration | W.Va. Code §11-22-2 | County clerk |
| County excise tax | $1.10 per $500 of consideration | W.Va. Code §11-22-2a | County clerk |
| Combined total | $2.20 per $500 (effective $4.40 per $1,000) | — | County clerk |

The tax applies to each $500 increment or fraction thereof. A sale price of $250,001 triggers tax on 501 units of $500, not 500 units. This rounding-up rule catches agents off guard regularly. On a $300,000 transaction, the combined excise tax totals $1,320—not an insignificant line item for sellers budgeting their net proceeds.

## Recording Fees and Document Standards

Beyond the excise tax, county clerks charge recording fees governed by W.Va. Code §59-1-2. These fees are separate from the transfer tax and apply to the physical act of indexing the document in the public record. As of 2026, the base recording fee for a deed is $20.00 for the first page and $5.00 for each additional page.

**Additional surcharges apply depending on the county and document type.** The County Records Management and Preservation Fund adds $5.00 per instrument under §59-1-2(a)(5). Some counties also collect a Courthouse Facilities Improvement Fund surcharge. Agents should confirm the exact schedule with the specific county clerk's office weeks before closing, not the morning of.

Documents must meet formatting standards or the clerk can reject them outright. W.Va. Code §39-1-2a requires minimum one-inch margins on all sides, a three-inch blank space at the top of the first page for clerk indexing stamps, and black ink on white paper. Non-conforming documents get returned, and your closing stalls.

## Forms Agents Must Know and Use

The primary form for documenting the excise tax is the **West Virginia Excise Tax Return (STC 13:20)**. This form accompanies every deed presented for recording and declares the consideration paid. The seller (or the agent handling the transaction paperwork) must complete it accurately, and the county clerk stamps it upon tax payment.

For transactions exempt from the excise tax—transfers between spouses during divorce, deeds to governmental entities, transfers for no consideration—the **Exemption Certificate (STC 13:20E)** replaces the standard return. Filing the wrong form or omitting it entirely means the clerk either rejects the deed or assesses full tax on a transaction that should have been exempt.

The West Virginia State Tax Department oversees these forms and periodically updates them. Agents should verify they are using the current revision before every transaction. Using an outdated form version is a rejection risk that experienced title companies catch, but agents handling FSBO transactions or working without a title company sometimes miss.

## Who Pays What: Allocation Between Buyer and Seller

**W.Va. Code §11-22-2 places the excise tax squarely on the grantor (seller) by default.** However, this statutory default is not absolute. The purchase agreement can allocate all or part of the tax to the buyer, and in competitive markets, buyers sometimes agree to absorb transfer costs to strengthen their offer.

| Fee Type | Default Responsibility | Negotiable? |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|
| State excise tax ($1.10/$500) | Seller | Yes, via contract |
| County excise tax ($1.10/$500) | Seller | Yes, via contract |
| Recording fee (deed) | Buyer | Yes, via contract |
| Recording fee (mortgage/deed of trust) | Buyer | Yes, via contract |
| Lien release recording | Seller | Rarely negotiated |

Recording fees for the deed itself traditionally fall to the buyer, since the buyer is the party who benefits from having the deed of record. Recording fees for the deed of trust or mortgage also land on the buyer in standard practice. Lien releases—the recordings that clear the seller's old mortgage from the record—are the seller's expense.

Agents should spell out these allocations explicitly in the contract rather than relying on "customary" splits. Custom varies by county in West Virginia, and assumptions cause disputes. The [agency disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) in the state already demand transparency about financial obligations—transfer tax allocation is no exception.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

**Mistake 1: Calculating excise tax on the rounded-down sale price.** The statute taxes each $500 or fraction thereof. A sale at $185,250 means 371 units of $500, not 370.5 rounded down. The correct tax is 371 multiplied by $2.20, equaling $816.20. Agents who round down shortchange the county clerk and get the deed kicked back.

**Mistake 2: Forgetting the county excise tax exists.** Many agents quote only the $1.10 state rate to sellers, failing to mention the additional $1.10 county rate. This under-estimates the seller's costs by exactly half and creates an unpleasant surprise at the closing table. When a seller's net proceeds drop by $660 on a $300,000 sale because the agent forgot the county portion, trust evaporates instantly.

**Mistake 3: Filing the standard STC 13:20 for exempt transfers.** When a parent deeds property to a child for no consideration, or when property transfers pursuant to a divorce decree, the Exemption Certificate (STC 13:20E) is required. Filing the wrong form either results in unnecessary tax payment or a rejected recording.

**Mistake 4: Missing county-specific page fees and surcharges.** West Virginia has 55 counties, and while the base recording fees are set by statute, additional local surcharges vary. An agent closing in Monongalia County may face different total fees than one closing in Raleigh County. Failing to call ahead and confirm the exact amount means the check or wire to the clerk's office comes up short.

**Mistake 5: Not accounting for multiple parcels on a single deed.** When a transaction involves two or more tax parcels conveyed by one deed, the excise tax still applies to the total consideration. But some clerks require separate page fees or additional indexing fees for multi-parcel deeds. This trips up agents handling rural land transactions where a single sale may encompass three or four parcels.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under W.Va. Code §30-40-19 for the acts of their affiliated agents. When an agent miscalculates transfer taxes on a closing statement, the resulting financial discrepancy can trigger a complaint to the West Virginia Real Estate Commission. Brokers should build three specific audit checkpoints into their transaction review process.

First, verify that every file contains either a completed STC 13:20 or STC 13:20E before closing day. The form should match the transaction type—taxable or exempt—and the consideration figure on the form must match the contract price. Second, confirm that the agent's estimated closing costs provided to the seller include both the state and county excise tax at the combined $2.20 per $500 rate. A quick formula check against the contract price catches errors before they reach the title company.

Third, review how agents are communicating recording fee estimates to buyers. If your brokerage covers multiple counties, create a reference sheet with county-specific recording fee schedules updated quarterly. Agents working in [transactions where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) over cost disagreements will benefit from having exact numbers at their fingertips rather than estimates.

Brokers who discover repeated errors should treat them as training deficiencies, not just clerical mistakes. The West Virginia Real Estate Commission can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under §30-40-25 and can suspend or revoke licenses for patterns of negligence or misrepresentation regarding transaction costs.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Liability, Fines, and Deal Failure

The immediate consequence of unpaid excise tax is straightforward: the county clerk refuses to record the deed. Under W.Va. Code §11-22-6, no deed can be recorded without full payment of the excise tax or presentation of a valid exemption certificate. An unrecorded deed means the buyer has no constructive notice of ownership—other creditors or claimants could record liens or competing interests against the property.

Beyond recording refusal, the State Tax Commissioner can pursue the unpaid tax plus interest and penalties against the grantor. W.Va. Code §11-22-5 authorizes penalties of up to 25% of the unpaid tax for fraudulent intent to evade, plus interest accruing from the date the tax was due. For agents who advised their clients to understate consideration on the STC 13:20 form—a practice that occasionally surfaces in the industry—criminal penalties under §11-22-5(b) include fines up to $10,000 or imprisonment up to one year.

From a civil liability perspective, an agent who miscalculates the transfer tax and causes a delayed closing may face breach-of-duty claims. If the buyer's rate lock expires during the delay, or if the seller misses a contractual deadline on their subsequent purchase, the resulting damages could far exceed the original tax miscalculation. This is not theoretical—it happens in transactions across the state every quarter.
