## Understanding Utah Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Utah who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not merely academic. It determines how you draft Section 6 of the Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC), how you advise clients on net proceeds or cash-to-close, and whether your transaction survives the settlement table without a dispute. Getting this wrong exposes you to Division of Real Estate complaints, E&O claims, and deals that crater at the eleventh hour.

Utah does not have a statute that assigns most closing costs to a specific party. Outside the documentary transfer tax under Utah Code 59-2-102 and the recording requirements under Utah Code 57-3-106, nearly every fee is negotiable. That flexibility is a gift and a trap — because if the REPC is silent or ambiguous, both parties will claim the other owes the cost.

## The Default Split: What Custom Dictates in 2026

**Custom in Utah's Wasatch Front markets has calcified into a pattern most agents treat as law, even though it is not.** The seller typically pays for the owner's title insurance policy, the real estate commission, one-half of the escrow/closing fee, and the documentary transfer fee. The buyer typically pays for the lender's title insurance policy, the loan origination fee, the appraisal, prepaid interest, hazard insurance, and the other half of the escrow/closing fee.

These allocations are market norms, not legal mandates. In rural Utah counties like San Juan or Garfield, you will find different customs — sometimes the buyer pays the full escrow fee, sometimes the seller covers it entirely. The only allocation that approaches statutory requirement is the real property transfer tax, which Utah Code 59-2-102 places on the grantor (seller) unless the parties agree otherwise in writing.

| Cost Item | Typical Payer (Wasatch Front) | Governed By |
|-----------|-------------------------------|-------------|
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | REPC Section 6 |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | REPC Section 6 |
| Documentary transfer fee | Seller | Utah Code 59-2-102 |
| Escrow/closing fee | Split 50/50 | REPC Section 6 |
| Recording fees | Buyer | Utah Code 57-3-106 |
| Real estate commission | Seller (historically) | Listing agreement / REPC |

Agents who rely on "that's how it's always done" without documenting the allocation in the REPC are setting up a liability event. The [Utah Division of Real Estate](https://realestate.utah.gov/) has issued guidance reminding licensees that unwritten custom does not substitute for contractual clarity.

## Forms That Control the Allocation

The Utah Association of Realtors (UAR) REPC — Form No. 25 (2025 revision, still current for 2026 transactions) — addresses closing costs primarily in Section 6: Settlement and Closing. This section contains blanks where agents must specify who pays for specific items, including title insurance, escrow fees, and any seller concessions toward buyer closing costs.

**If you leave Section 6 blanks empty, you have not created an enforceable allocation.** The title company will follow local custom, and if either party objects at the settlement statement review, you have a dispute with no contractual anchor. Addendum No. 1 (Seller Financing) and Addendum No. 4 (FHA/VA Loan Requirements) also contain closing cost provisions that can override or supplement Section 6.

For FHA transactions, Addendum No. 4 specifies that certain costs cannot be charged to the buyer under HUD guidelines, which effectively shifts them to the seller or requires a seller concession. VA transactions carry similar restrictions under 38 CFR 36.4313. Agents representing VA buyers must be precise about which costs the seller absorbs — mistakes here can void the loan approval days before closing.

The UAR Settlement Deadline Addendum (Form No. 9) governs timing. If closing costs are disputed and resolution pushes past the settlement deadline, either party may issue a Notice to Cure Default (Form No. 20) and, if unresolved within the cure period, terminate. ## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

Liability flows from several directions. The Utah Division of Real Estate enforces Utah Administrative Code R162-2f-401a, which requires licensees to deal honestly and in good faith and to exercise reasonable skill and care. Failing to properly document closing cost allocation can constitute a violation of this standard.

A buyer's agent who verbally promises "the seller will pay your closing costs" without memorializing that in the REPC or an addendum has created an agency liability problem. If the seller refuses at closing, the buyer may sue the agent for negligent misrepresentation. The Division can impose sanctions including fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension, or mandatory education under Utah Code 61-2f-404.

Deal cancellation is the most common consequence. When a settlement statement arrives showing unexpected charges, the party bearing the surprise cost may refuse to close. Without a contractual mandate in Section 6 or an addendum, neither the title company nor a court can force allocation. The transaction dies, earnest money becomes disputed, and you are the common denominator in both parties' complaints.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Cost Allocation

**First, agents routinely fail to specify the escrow fee split.** Leaving it blank or writing "per custom" invites title companies to apply their own default, which may differ from what you told your client. Write the exact percentage or dollar amount each party pays.

Second, agents miscalculate seller concession limits for government-backed loans. FHA allows up to 6% of the sale price in seller concessions for most transactions, while VA caps interested party contributions at 4% of the sale price. Writing a 5% seller concession on a VA deal creates a loan compliance failure that surfaces during underwriting — often too late to restructure without delaying closing. Understanding [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) often traces back to these late-stage corrections.

Third, agents neglect to update closing cost terms when price changes occur through negotiation or appraisal adjustments. A $10,000 price reduction may eliminate the need for a seller concession, but if the addendum granting 3% toward closing costs remains unchanged, the seller is now paying twice — once through the price cut and again through the concession. Amendment No. 1 (Form No. 12) must be executed to reconcile.

Fourth, dual agents in limited agency arrangements fail to advise both parties on the closing cost implications of their negotiation positions, violating the balanced duty under Utah Code 61-2f-401(4). The Division treats this seriously because the dual agent is the only person at the table with full information.

Fifth, agents forget that transfer tax exemptions exist under Utah Code 59-2-102.5 for certain transfers between family members, trusts, or entities. Assuming the seller always pays transfer tax without checking for exemptions costs clients real money and demonstrates a lack of reasonable skill and care.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Utah Code 61-2f-401(3) and Utah Administrative Code R162-2f-401b. If an agent on your team consistently leaves Section 6 incomplete, your brokerage bears responsibility for the pattern.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Red Flag |
|-------------|---------------|----------|
| REPC Section 6 | All blanks filled, dollar amounts or percentages specified | Blank fields, "per custom" language |
| Addendum No. 4 (FHA/VA) | Concession percentage within program limits | Concession exceeding 4% on VA, 6% on FHA |
| Amendment forms after price change | Closing cost terms updated to reflect new price | Original concession amount unchanged after reduction |
| Settlement statement review | Agent-documented allocation matches HUD-1/CD | Discrepancy between REPC and closing disclosure |
| Dual agency files | Both parties advised on cost allocation in writing | No documentation of balanced disclosure |

## Negotiation Dynamics Agents Should Understand

The post-NAR settlement environment has intensified closing cost negotiations. Buyer agents can no longer assume commission will be offered through the MLS, and buyers are increasingly asking sellers to cover not just traditional concessions but also buyer-agent compensation through closing cost credits. This makes Section 6 drafting more complex than in prior years.

When a buyer requests that the seller pay 3% toward closing costs and that amount includes a credit for buyer-agent compensation, the REPC must be explicit about the allocation within that 3%. Lenders will scrutinize the Closing Disclosure to ensure seller credits are going toward legitimate closing costs, not being rebated to the buyer as cash. Ambiguity here triggers loan-level compliance flags that delay closings by weeks.

Utah's market conditions in 2026 — particularly along the Wasatch Front where inventory remains tight — mean sellers often reject concession requests outright. Agents representing buyers in competitive situations should calculate the total cash-to-close impact of absorbing all buyer-side costs without seller help, and present that number to clients before submitting offers. Surprises at closing are an agent's failure, not the market's.
