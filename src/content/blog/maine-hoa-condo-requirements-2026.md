## Understanding Maine HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Maine's condominium and homeowners association disclosure framework sits primarily within Title 33 of the Maine Revised Statutes, specifically the Maine Condominium Act (33 M.R.S. §§ 1601-101 through 1604-118). Agents listing or selling units governed by an association need a working command of **Maine hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** to avoid contract cancellations, delayed closings, and personal liability exposure. The rules have not undergone dramatic legislative overhaul for 2026, but enforcement attention from the Maine Real Estate Commission has intensified following a string of consumer complaints tied to incomplete resale packages.

The statute distinguishes between new-unit sales by a declarant (developer) and resales by unit owners. Most practicing agents encounter the resale pathway, which triggers the resale certificate requirement codified at [33 M.R.S. § 1604-108](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/33/title33sec1604-108.html). This section mandates that specific financial, governance, and physical-condition information reach the buyer within a defined timeframe. Failure to deliver opens the door to deal cancellation at the buyer's sole discretion, no matter how far along the transaction has progressed.

## The Resale Certificate: Maine's Core Disclosure Vehicle

**The resale certificate is the single most important document in any Maine condominium resale transaction.** It functions as a snapshot of the association's financial health, pending litigation, insurance coverage, and operating rules. The seller is responsible for obtaining it from the association or its management company, though agents frequently coordinate the request on the seller's behalf.

Under 33 M.R.S. § 1604-108, the certificate must include: the declaration, bylaws, and rules of the association; a statement of monthly common-expense assessments; a disclosure of any special assessments approved or anticipated; a statement of any capital expenditure reserves; current association financial statements; and information about pending lawsuits involving the association. The association has ten days to furnish the certificate once requested. If it fails to do so, the seller may provide a sworn affidavit in its place — but this carries its own risk profile that most agents underestimate.

The buyer then has five calendar days after receiving the resale certificate to cancel the purchase agreement. This cancellation right is absolute and does not require the buyer to cite a specific deficiency. Once those five days lapse without written cancellation, the right expires.

## Timing Requirements and the Five-Day Window

| Event | Deadline | Statutory Basis |
|-------|----------|-----------------|
| Seller requests resale certificate from association | Before or at listing (best practice) | Agent duty of care |
| Association delivers resale certificate | Within 10 days of request | 33 M.R.S. § 1604-108(b) |
| Buyer receives resale certificate | Before or concurrent with contract execution | 33 M.R.S. § 1604-108(d) |
| Buyer's cancellation window | 5 days after receipt | 33 M.R.S. § 1604-108(d) |
| Seller's fallback if association fails to deliver | After 10-day period expires | 33 M.R.S. § 1604-108(b) |

**Agents who wait until after mutual acceptance to order the resale certificate are gambling with their closing date.** The five-day cancellation clock does not start until the buyer actually receives the document, so a late delivery pushes the entire timeline backward. On a 30-day close, losing two weeks to a tardy management company can blow past the financing contingency deadline and create cascading problems.

Maine does not prescribe a specific state-issued form number for the resale certificate itself — the content requirements are embedded in statute, and most management companies use their own template. However, agents should verify completeness against the statutory checklist in § 1604-108(a) before forwarding to the buyer's side. An incomplete certificate does not satisfy the delivery requirement and will not trigger the five-day window.

## HOA Communities vs. Condominiums: A Critical Distinction

Not every community with an association is a condominium. Maine treats these differently. The Maine Condominium Act applies only to condominiums created under Title 33, Chapter 31. Planned communities governed by homeowners associations that are not organized as condominiums fall outside the statutory resale-certificate mandate.

| Feature | Condominium (Title 33 Ch. 31) | Non-Condo HOA Community |
|---------|-------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Resale certificate required by statute | Yes | No |
| Buyer cancellation right | 5 days | Only if contractual |
| Association must respond in 10 days | Yes | No statutory obligation |
| Seller must disclose assessments | Statutory mandate | Best practice / contract-dependent |
| Agent liability for non-delivery | Statutory + common law | Common law / MREC rules |

This does not mean agents can ignore HOA disclosures in non-condo communities. The Maine Real Estate Commission's licensing rules and the duty of honest dealing still require agents to disclose material facts, including HOA fees, restrictions, and pending assessments. The practical difference is that non-condo HOA disclosures are governed by contract terms and general agency law rather than the specific statutory machinery of § 1604-108. Many listing agents use the Maine Association of Realtors form addenda to address HOA information in non-condo contexts, and brokers should confirm their teams are doing so consistently.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Maine Condo and HOA Disclosures

**Three to five errors account for the majority of MREC complaints and closing-table disputes involving association disclosures.**

First, agents frequently conflate the property condition disclosure (the MAR Property Disclosure Form) with the resale certificate. These are separate obligations. The seller's property disclosure addresses the physical unit; the resale certificate addresses the association. Delivering one does not excuse the other.

Second, listing agents sometimes accept an outdated resale certificate from the seller — one generated for a prior failed transaction months earlier. The statute requires the certificate to reflect current conditions. An association's financial picture can shift materially between quarters, and a stale certificate exposes the agent to claims of inadequate disclosure. Best practice: order a fresh certificate for each new listing agreement.

Third, agents misunderstand who bears the cost. The seller is responsible for the expense of obtaining the resale certificate unless the purchase and sale agreement allocates it differently. Agents who fail to address this in the listing agreement sometimes end up eating the cost themselves or creating last-minute disputes at the settlement table.

Fourth, buyer's agents occasionally fail to calendar the five-day cancellation deadline, allowing it to lapse without their client making a deliberate decision. This is a missed advisory opportunity that can generate complaints if the buyer later discovers unfavorable association information they would have acted on.

Fifth, agents in dual-agency transactions sometimes deliver the resale certificate without documenting the date and method of delivery. Without a written acknowledgment of receipt, disputes over whether the five-day window has expired become he-said-she-said exercises that favor the buyer's position.

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

An agent who fails to ensure proper delivery of the resale certificate faces three distinct categories of risk. The most immediate is deal cancellation: because the buyer's cancellation right persists until five days after actual receipt, a buyer can walk away from a transaction at any point prior to that window closing — including days before a scheduled closing — if the certificate was never formally delivered.

The second category is regulatory. The Maine Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under 32 M.R.S. Chapter 114, can investigate complaints arising from disclosure failures. Sanctions range from a letter of guidance to license suspension. The Commission has historically treated repeated disclosure failures as evidence of incompetence or untrustworthiness, both grounds for discipline under 32 M.R.S. § 13197.

The third category is civil liability. A buyer who closes without receiving proper disclosures and later discovers material deficiencies — undisclosed special assessments, inadequate reserves, pending litigation — may pursue damages against the seller and the listing agent. Maine courts have recognized that agents owe duties of care that extend beyond simply passing along whatever the seller provides. If you knew or should have known the resale certificate was incomplete, you carry exposure. Agents working within [teams or supervisory structures](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) should confirm that their broker's compliance protocols cover association disclosure verification.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory liability for their agents' disclosure compliance under MREC rules. A broker's internal audit should address five areas.

First, confirm that every condo listing file contains either a current resale certificate or documented evidence that one has been ordered. The order date matters — if the listing sat for months, the original certificate may be stale by the time an offer arrives. Second, verify that the file includes a written buyer acknowledgment of receipt with a date. Email delivery with a read receipt is acceptable; a text message saying "sent the HOA docs" is not sufficient documentation.

Third, review whether listing agreements in your office address the cost allocation for the resale certificate. If your agents are using the MAR standard listing form, confirm the addendum or special provisions section specifies responsibility. Fourth, audit dual-agency files with heightened scrutiny — these generate a disproportionate share of complaints because the single agent is responsible for both delivery and advisory functions. Fifth, compare your [deadline-tracking systems](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) against the statutory timelines. The ten-day association response period plus five-day buyer cancellation period creates a fifteen-day minimum buffer that must be reflected in your transaction calendars.

Brokers who rely on memory-based compliance rather than [systematic checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) will eventually face a situation where an agent's oversight becomes the brokerage's problem. Documenting your supervisory protocols — and actually enforcing them — is the best defense against vicarious liability claims.
