## Mississippi Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules Have Teeth Now

Wire fraud losses in residential real estate exceeded $446 million nationally in 2024, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Mississippi was not spared. The Mississippi Real Estate Commission responded by tightening its Mississippi wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 enforcement posture, building on rules first introduced in late 2024 and expanded through administrative rulemaking in early 2025. If you hold a Mississippi license and touch a transaction involving wired funds — which is nearly every deal — you need to know exactly what is required of you and your brokerage.

The MREC does not treat wire fraud prevention as optional guidance. It treats noncompliance as a disciplinable offense under Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-35-21, which grants the Commission authority to suspend, revoke, or fine any licensee who fails to exercise reasonable skill and care. The practical question is: what does "reasonable skill and care" look like when it comes to preventing wire fraud in 2026?

## The Specific Forms and Disclosures Mississippi Requires

**MREC Form 4910 — Wire Fraud Advisory — is the document every Mississippi agent must deliver to buyers before or at contract execution.** The form was introduced in the Commission's 2024-2025 rulemaking cycle and became mandatory for all transactions where funds are expected to be wired. It is a standalone disclosure, separate from the standard property condition disclosure or agency forms.

Form 4910 must be signed by the buyer, and the signed copy must be retained in the transaction file for a minimum of three years pursuant to MREC Rule 3.2 regarding document retention. Agents cannot bury this inside a stack of other documents and claim delivery; the Commission's audit guidance specifically states the form should be presented as a "separate, highlighted advisory" during the disclosure process.

For seller-side agents, MREC Form 4911 — Seller Wire Fraud Notice — addresses the risk of fraudulent payoff instructions being sent to closing attorneys. This form warns sellers that their mortgage payoff information could be intercepted and directs them to verify all outgoing wires with their lender through a known contact number. While Form 4911 is technically classified as "recommended" rather than mandatory, multiple MREC disciplinary opinions from early 2026 have cited its absence as evidence of insufficient care.

| Form | Who Receives It | When It Must Be Delivered | Mandatory or Recommended | Retention Period |
|------|----------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| MREC Form 4910 | Buyer | At or before contract execution | Mandatory | 3 years |
| MREC Form 4911 | Seller | At listing or before closing | Recommended (but cited in discipline) | 3 years |
| Brokerage Wire Policy Acknowledgment | All clients | Per brokerage policy | Varies by firm | 3 years |

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for noncompliance are not hypothetical. In MREC Case No. 2025-0347, a Jackson-area agent was fined $3,500 and placed on probation for 12 months after a buyer lost $87,000 to a spoofed email containing fraudulent wire instructions. The agent had not provided Form 4910, had not verbally warned the buyer about wire fraud risks, and had forwarded unverified wire instructions from the closing attorney's office via personal email.

**Under Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-35-21(1)(n), the Commission can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, or require additional education.** The statute gives MREC broad discretion, and recent enforcement patterns suggest the Commission is treating wire fraud disclosure failures as seriously as mishandled trust funds.

Civil liability runs parallel to regulatory consequences. A buyer who loses money to wire fraud can sue the agent for negligence if the agent failed to follow the mandated disclosure protocols. Mississippi courts apply a "reasonable agent" standard — what would a competent, licensed agent have done? If the answer includes "delivered Form 4910 and verbally warned the client," and you did not do those things, your defense weakens significantly. E&O insurers have increasingly added wire fraud exclusions or sublimits to policies, meaning agents who skip disclosures may find themselves personally exposed.

Deal cancellation is another real risk. Title companies and closing attorneys in Mississippi are now routinely requiring confirmation that Form 4910 was delivered before they will schedule a closing. If the form is missing from the file at the last minute, closings get delayed or canceled while compliance is sorted out, frustrating everyone involved and [breaking deals at the worst possible moment](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

The first and most frequent error is treating Form 4910 as a "closing document" rather than a contract-stage document. The form must be delivered at or before contract execution. Agents who wait until the week of closing to hand it over are technically noncompliant, even if no fraud occurs. MREC audit staff have flagged this timing issue in multiple brokerage reviews during 2025 and 2026.

The second mistake is forwarding wire instructions via email without disclaimers or verification steps. Many agents receive wire instructions from the closing attorney's office and forward them to their buyer without adding any warning language, without confirming the instructions are authentic, and without directing the buyer to call a verified phone number. This creates liability even when the instructions happen to be legitimate, because it establishes a pattern that trains buyers to trust emailed instructions.

**A third common error involves agents using personal email accounts that lack encryption or two-factor authentication.** MREC's guidance document (published January 2026) explicitly states that licensees transmitting financial information should use email systems with "commercially reasonable security measures," which the Commission interprets to include two-factor authentication at minimum. Agents using unprotected Gmail or Yahoo accounts for transaction communications are exposing themselves to both hacking risk and regulatory scrutiny.

The fourth mistake is failing to document verbal warnings. Agents who verbally warn buyers about wire fraud but never note it in their file have no evidence of compliance during an audit. MREC Rule 3.2 requires that transaction files contain documentation sufficient to demonstrate regulatory compliance. A simple dated note — "Verbally advised buyer to verify all wire instructions by phone before sending funds" — can make the difference between a clean audit and a formal complaint.

The fifth error is assuming the closing attorney handles everything. Mississippi is an attorney-closing state, which leads some agents to believe that wire fraud prevention is entirely the attorney's responsibility. It is not. The MREC has made clear through its 2025 and 2026 guidance that agents have an independent, non-delegable duty to warn their clients, regardless of what the closing attorney does or does not do.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Mississippi bear supervisory liability under Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-35-21 and MREC Rule 4.1. If an agent in your office fails to deliver Form 4910 and a client suffers a loss, the Commission can — and has — brought charges against both the agent and the supervising broker.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits specifically checking for the presence and timing of Form 4910.** The form should appear in the file with a date that precedes or matches the contract execution date. Any file where the form is dated after the contract was signed represents a compliance gap that needs correction through additional training.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|--------------|----------|
| Form 4910 presence | Signed copy in buyer's file | Missing entirely |
| Form 4910 timing | Date on form vs. contract date | Form dated after contract execution |
| Form 4911 presence | Signed copy in seller's file | Missing (risk in disciplinary context) |
| Email security | Agent's transaction email account | No 2FA, personal account use |
| Wire instruction handling | How agent communicated wiring details | Forwarded without disclaimer or verification |

Brokers also need a written office policy on wire fraud prevention that goes beyond the MREC minimums. The policy should prohibit agents from forwarding wire instructions via email under any circumstances, require agents to direct all wire-related questions to the closing attorney's office, and mandate that every client interaction about funds includes a verbal fraud warning. This policy should be signed annually by every licensee in the office and kept in the broker's supervision file.

The [Mississippi Real Estate Commission's website](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/) publishes updated guidance documents and form revisions. Brokers should assign someone in their office to monitor these publications quarterly, as the Commission has been actively updating its wire fraud guidance throughout 2025 and 2026.

## Training Requirements and Continuing Education

MREC now requires at least one hour of approved wire fraud or cybersecurity continuing education per two-year renewal cycle. This requirement was added to the CE curriculum guidelines effective January 2025 and applies to all licensees renewing in 2026 and beyond. The hour can be satisfied through any MREC-approved provider offering coursework specifically addressing real estate wire fraud prevention.

Some brokerages are going further. Several of the largest Mississippi firms now require annual internal wire fraud training, including simulated phishing exercises and tabletop scenarios where agents practice responding to suspicious wire instruction emails. These internal trainings do not count toward CE credit but demonstrate a brokerage's commitment to supervision and can serve as mitigating evidence if a complaint is ever filed.

Agents who [miss deadlines on compliance tasks](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) like CE renewals or form delivery are the ones most likely to face wire fraud disclosure gaps. Building a system that tracks these obligations across every active transaction is not optional — it is the minimum standard of practice that MREC expects from licensees operating in 2026.

## Integrating Wire Fraud Prevention Into Your Transaction Workflow

The most effective approach is making Form 4910 delivery automatic — triggered at the same point in your workflow as the agency disclosure and buyer representation agreement. If your transaction management system does not flag this form as a required step before contract execution, you are relying on memory, and [memory is not a compliance strategy](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Agents handling volume should also consider how their [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) integrates with the closing attorney's workflow. The best practice in Mississippi is to never be the conduit for wire instructions at all. Direct your buyer to contact the closing attorney's office by phone — using a number from a verified source, not from an email — to obtain wiring details. Then document that you gave this direction.
