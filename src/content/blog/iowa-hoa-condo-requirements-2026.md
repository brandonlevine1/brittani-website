## Iowa HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: The Statute-Level Breakdown

Agents listing or selling condominiums in Iowa face a specific set of disclosure obligations that differ from standard residential transactions. Understanding the Iowa HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026 is not optional—it is a matter of statutory compliance tied directly to deal viability and personal liability. Missing a single document delivery can hand a buyer a free exit from an otherwise binding contract.

Iowa's condominium disclosure framework is anchored in Iowa Code Chapter 499B, the Iowa Uniform Condominium Act. This chapter governs the creation, management, sale, and resale of condominium units across the state. The Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) enforces agent conduct standards that intersect with these disclosure duties, meaning a compliance failure can trigger both civil liability and licensing consequences.

## What Iowa Code Chapter 499B Requires for Resales

**Section 499B.11 is the provision that agents must memorize.** It mandates that a seller of a condominium unit provide a resale certificate to a prospective buyer before the purchase agreement becomes binding—or, if already under contract, within 10 business days of the buyer's written request. The certificate must be prepared by the unit owners' association, not the seller individually.

The resale certificate must contain specific data points: the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments approved or pending, the association's current budget, a statement of reserves, any capital expenditure plans, outstanding litigation involving the association, and whether the association holds first-refusal rights. If any of this information is omitted, the certificate is incomplete and does not satisfy the statutory requirement.

Buyers who do not receive a compliant resale certificate retain the right to rescind the purchase agreement until it is delivered. There is no expiration date on this rescission right if the certificate never arrives—a fact that keeps deals vulnerable indefinitely.

## The IAR Form Stack: What Agents Actually Hand Over

The Iowa Association of Realtors (IAR) publishes standard forms that agents use in day-to-day practice. For HOA and condo transactions, the relevant documents include the IAR Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 101), which contains provisions addressing association disclosures, and addenda specific to condominium purchases.

| Document | Source | Who Prepares It | Delivery Deadline |
|----------|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Resale Certificate | Iowa Code 499B.11 | Unit Owners' Association | 10 business days after buyer request |
| Declaration & Bylaws | Iowa Code 499B.11 | Association / Management Co. | Before or at contract execution |
| Association Financial Statement | Iowa Code 499B.11 | Association | Included with resale certificate |
| IAR Condominium Addendum | IAR Forms Library | Listing or Buyer Agent | Attached to purchase agreement |
| Seller Disclosure (Form 107) | Iowa Code 558A | Seller | Before acceptance of offer |

The IAR Form 107 (Seller Disclosure Statement) does include a section where sellers must indicate whether the property is subject to an HOA or condominium association. Checking "yes" triggers the expectation that association-specific documents will follow. Agents who skip this checkbox—or whose sellers check "no" incorrectly—create immediate liability exposure.

Iowa's Real Property Seller Disclosure Act (Iowa Code Chapter 558A) operates alongside Chapter 499B. They are not interchangeable. The seller disclosure covers property condition; the resale certificate covers association governance and financials. Both must be delivered.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Iowa Condo Transactions

**The first and most frequent error is treating HOA disclosures as optional when a property is in a "small" association.** Iowa Code Chapter 499B applies to all condominiums regardless of the number of units. A four-unit converted Victorian in Iowa City carries the same statutory obligations as a 200-unit tower in Des Moines. Size does not create an exemption.

The second mistake is conflating planned development HOAs with condominiums. Iowa does not have a single, unified statute covering all homeowners associations. Chapter 499B covers condominiums specifically. Planned communities governed only by recorded covenants (CC&Rs) and not organized as condominiums under 499B do not trigger the resale certificate requirement—but agents often assume they do, or worse, assume neither type requires any disclosure. The distinction matters because [knowing where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often comes back to an agent's failure to identify the correct legal structure early.

Third, agents routinely let the 10-business-day clock run without tracking it. The buyer requests the resale certificate, the management company is slow to respond, and the agent never follows up. Meanwhile, the buyer's rescission right remains alive. If market conditions shift during that limbo, the buyer can walk.

Fourth, some listing agents accept a letter from the association president in place of a formal resale certificate. A letter summarizing dues and rules does not satisfy 499B.11. The statute specifies the content that must appear; an informal summary leaves gaps that preserve rescission rights.

Fifth, agents fail to confirm whether special assessments have been voted on but not yet billed. A resale certificate that omits a pending assessment approved at the last board meeting is materially incomplete. The buyer agent should always request meeting minutes from the prior 12 months as supplemental due diligence.

## Liability Consequences When Agents Miss the Mark

An agent who fails to deliver required condominium disclosures faces three layers of exposure. The first is contractual: the buyer retains rescission rights, which can crater a deal days before closing when title work reveals the gap. The second is regulatory: IREC can investigate complaints, issue citations, and impose sanctions including fines or license suspension under Iowa Administrative Code 193E.

The third layer is civil litigation. If a buyer closes without receiving proper disclosures and later discovers undisclosed assessments or litigation, they can sue both the seller and the agents involved. Iowa courts have recognized that agents owe a duty to facilitate statutory compliance, and ignorance of Chapter 499B is not a defense for a licensed professional.

> "A unit's purchaser may cancel the contract of purchase before conveyance... if the resale certificate required under this section is not provided." — Iowa Code § 499B.11(4)

The financial stakes are real. Special assessments in Iowa condo communities frequently range from $2,000 to $25,000 per unit for roof replacements, parking structure repairs, or reserve fund recapitalization. An undisclosed $15,000 assessment is enough to fund a lawsuit that costs more than the commission earned.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Brokers carry supervisory liability under Iowa Administrative Code 193E-12.3, which requires reasonable oversight of affiliated licensees' transactions.** A broker who never reviews condo files for resale certificate delivery is exposed to the same complaints filed against the agent.

Broker audit protocols should verify three things in every condo transaction file: first, that the resale certificate was requested in writing with a date-stamped record; second, that the certificate was received and delivered to the buyer before closing; and third, that the IAR Condominium Addendum is executed and attached to the purchase agreement.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Look For | Risk If Missing |
|-----------------|------------------|-----------------|
| Written request for resale certificate | Dated email or letter from agent to association | Rescission right remains open |
| Resale certificate in file | Complete document per 499B.11 requirements | Buyer can cancel; regulatory complaint |
| IAR Condominium Addendum signed | Fully executed, attached to purchase agreement | Ambiguity on association terms; E&O claim |
| Seller Disclosure Form 107 HOA checkbox | Marked "yes" with association details | Misrepresentation exposure |
| Special assessment verification | Board minutes or management letter confirming status | Undisclosed financial obligation |

Brokers should also track [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in their transaction management workflows. The 10-business-day resale certificate window is easy to lose in a busy pipeline, and many Iowa management companies do not prioritize agent requests without follow-up.

Training sessions for agents should cover the difference between Chapter 499B condominiums and Chapter 499A horizontal property regimes, since the disclosure mechanics differ slightly. Brokers operating in markets like West Des Moines, Cedar Rapids, and the Quad Cities—where condo inventory is concentrated—should treat this as annual continuing education content for their teams.

## How Iowa Compares to Neighboring States

Iowa's approach is less prescriptive than some neighboring states but still carries teeth. Wisconsin, for example, has its own set of [HOA and condo requirements](/blog/wisconsin-hoa-condo-requirements-2026) that include specific timeframes and forms shaped by different statutes. Agents licensed in multiple states or working border markets near the Quad Cities must track which state's rules apply to each transaction—the property's physical location controls.

The Iowa Real Estate Commission posts [guidance and disciplinary actions](https://professionallicensing.iowa.gov/boards-commissions/real-estate-commission) publicly, and agents can review past cases where disclosure failures resulted in sanctions. Reviewing even two or three of these cases reveals the pattern: agents who assumed someone else would handle the disclosure are the ones facing discipline.
