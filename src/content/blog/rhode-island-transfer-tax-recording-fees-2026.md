## Understanding the Rhode Island Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on a closing disclosure is one of the fastest ways to delay a deal, frustrate a client, or invite regulatory scrutiny. The Rhode Island transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 carry specific statutory obligations that fall on agents, closing attorneys, and title companies alike. Miscalculating even a single line item can cascade into recording rejections, penalty interest, and uncomfortable conversations with your broker.

Rhode Island is an attorney-close state, which means a licensed attorney typically handles the recording mechanics. But agents remain the first line of communication with buyers and sellers about expected costs. If you are quoting net sheets, preparing listing presentations, or advising clients on offers, you need to know these numbers cold.

The state's transfer tax framework is governed primarily by Rhode Island General Laws Title 44, Chapter 25, while recording fees are set at the municipal level under R.I. Gen. Laws 34-13. Both sources saw minor adjustments heading into 2026, and this post covers exactly what changed, what stayed the same, and where agents routinely get tripped up.

## Current Tax Rates and Who Pays Them

**Rhode Island imposes a real estate conveyance tax of $4.60 per $500 of consideration, which equates to an effective rate of 0.92% of the sale price.** This rate applies statewide and is codified under R.I. Gen. Laws 44-25-2. The statute assigns primary liability to the grantor (seller), though contractual allocation to the buyer is permissible and common in competitive market conditions.

There is no separate county-level transfer tax in Rhode Island. However, some municipalities have explored local recording surcharges. As of 2026, the $2 per-document municipal surcharge remains the only local add-on that most agents will encounter.

For transactions involving commercial property exceeding $700,000 in consideration, an additional surtax discussion sometimes arises. Rhode Island does not currently impose a mansion tax or tiered rate structure, so the $4.60 per $500 rate applies uniformly regardless of sale price. This is a common point of confusion for agents who also operate in neighboring Connecticut or Massachusetts, where progressive rates apply.

## Recording Fees: A Municipality-by-Municipality Reality

Recording fees in Rhode Island are not uniform across all 39 cities and towns, although most follow the baseline established by R.I. Gen. Laws 34-13-7. The standard fee structure looks like this:

| Document Type | First Page Fee | Additional Pages | Municipal Surcharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deed | $40 | $3/page | $2 |
| Mortgage | $40 | $3/page | $2 |
| Discharge/Release | $40 | $3/page | $2 |
| Assignment | $40 | $3/page | $2 |
| Lis Pendens | $40 | $3/page | $2 |

Some municipalities add their own recording technology or document preservation fees. Providence, for example, charges an additional $5 document technology fee. Agents should verify current fee schedules directly with the city or town clerk's office before quoting final numbers on a closing disclosure estimate.

**Every document submitted for recording must include the exact recording fee in the form of a check payable to the city or town clerk.** Personal checks from buyers or sellers are generally not accepted; certified funds or attorney escrow checks are standard. A document returned for insufficient fees adds days to the recording timeline and exposes the transaction to gap coverage issues.

## Form Numbers and Filing Requirements

The primary tax form agents should know is the **Rhode Island Real Estate Conveyance Tax Return, Form T-76**, filed with the Rhode Island Division of Taxation. This form must accompany the deed at the time of recording, and the town clerk will not accept a deed without a stamped or receipted T-76 attached.

Form T-76 requires disclosure of the full consideration paid, the names of grantor and grantee, the property address, and the plat and lot numbers from the municipal assessor's records. The tax must be paid in full before the form is accepted. Partial payments result in rejection.

For exempt transactions—such as transfers between spouses during divorce, transfers to trusts where the grantor retains beneficial interest, or transfers by operation of law—you still need to file Form T-76 but mark the appropriate exemption code. The [Division of Taxation's website](https://tax.ri.gov/) lists all valid exemption categories under Section 44-25-1. Failing to file even for an exempt transfer is a common oversight that holds up recording.

## What Happens When Compliance Fails

The penalties for non-compliance are not abstract. Under R.I. Gen. Laws 44-25-6, failure to pay the conveyance tax at the time of recording triggers a penalty of 10% of the unpaid tax plus interest at 18% per annum. For a $400,000 transaction, the base tax is $3,680. A missed payment means an immediate $368 penalty plus roughly $1.81 per day in interest.

Beyond financial penalties, the deed simply will not be recorded. An unrecorded deed means the buyer does not have constructive notice of ownership against subsequent purchasers or creditors. Title insurance companies will not issue a final policy on an unrecorded deed, which means the lender's requirements remain unsatisfied and the loan will not fund.

**Agents do not directly record documents in Rhode Island, but they can be held responsible for misleading clients about costs or timelines.** If a seller's net sheet underestimates the transfer tax and the seller arrives at closing unable to cover the shortfall, the deal stalls. The listing agent who prepared that net sheet may face a complaint to the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation, Real Estate Section, for misrepresentation of material facts.

In extreme cases, pattern misrepresentation of closing costs has resulted in license discipline including fines up to $1,000 per violation and mandatory continuing education requirements imposed by the Real Estate Commission.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

**First, agents routinely forget to account for multi-page documents.** A standard warranty deed in Rhode Island runs two to three pages. Agents who quote a flat $40 recording fee are shortchanging the estimate by $6 to $9 per document. When you multiply this across multiple recorded instruments—deed, mortgage, assignment of mortgage, affidavit of title—the shortfall adds up fast.

Second, many agents confuse the transfer tax rate with rates from neighboring states. Massachusetts charges $4.56 per $1,000 (not per $500), and Connecticut has tiered rates. An agent who crosses state lines regularly and quotes the wrong formula will produce a closing disclosure that is off by thousands of dollars. Always reference the Rhode Island-specific rate of $4.60 per $500.

Third, some agents fail to inform sellers that the transfer tax is their obligation absent a written agreement to the contrary. Purchase and sale agreements in Rhode Island should explicitly address who pays the conveyance tax. If the contract is silent, the statutory default places it on the seller. Surprising a seller with a $5,000+ line item at the closing table is the kind of mistake that generates complaints and kills referrals.

Fourth, agents occasionally forget that certain exemptions require documentation beyond just checking a box on Form T-76. For instance, a transfer to a revocable living trust may require a copy of the trust instrument to satisfy the town clerk. Agents who tell clients "don't worry, it's exempt" without confirming the paperwork create last-minute recording delays.

Fifth, some agents confuse recording fees with the conveyance tax and quote them as a single number. These are separate obligations paid to separate entities—the recording fee goes to the town clerk, and the conveyance tax goes to the Division of Taxation (collected at recording). Conflating them on a net sheet or buyer cost estimate is technically inaccurate and can trigger compliance questions during a brokerage audit.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-10 for the conduct of their affiliated agents. When it comes to transfer taxes and recording fees, there are specific audit points every broker should enforce at the office level.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Net sheet accuracy | Verify transfer tax calculation uses $4.60 per $500 | Every listing |
| Contract language | Confirm purchase agreement allocates transfer tax responsibility | Every transaction |
| Form T-76 preparation | Ensure closing attorney receives accurate consideration amount from agent | Pre-closing review |
| Recording fee estimates | Validate page counts and municipal surcharges are included | Every transaction |
| Exemption documentation | Confirm supporting documents are assembled for exempt transfers | As applicable |

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy specifying that agents may not provide recording fee or transfer tax estimates without referencing the current statutory rates. A simple policy memo updated annually satisfies the duty of reasonable supervision and provides a defense if an individual agent makes an error.

Transaction files should contain a copy of the final settlement statement showing the transfer tax paid and recording fees disbursed. If your office conducts quarterly file audits—and it should—these line items are easy to verify against the statutory rates. Discrepancies between the estimated net sheet and the final settlement statement warrant a conversation with the agent to identify whether the error was informational or systemic.
