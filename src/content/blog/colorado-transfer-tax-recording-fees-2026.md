## Understanding Colorado Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Colorado handles property transfer costs differently than most states, and agents who treat these fees as an afterthought risk delayed closings and regulatory complaints. The Colorado transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 reflect a structure that has remained relatively stable at the state level but continues to shift in certain mountain and resort municipalities. Knowing exactly what is owed, by whom, and to which entity separates competent agents from those scrambling at the settlement table.

The state's primary authority on documentary fees lives in Colorado Revised Statutes Title 39, Article 13, specifically C.R.S. 39-13-102. The Colorado Division of Real Estate, housed within the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA), enforces agent conduct around accurate fee disclosures. Agents working transactions in resort communities face an additional layer of local transfer tax ordinances that can dramatically change the cost picture for buyers and sellers.

## The State Documentary Fee: Structure and Calculation

**Colorado does not impose a traditional "transfer tax" in the way states like New York or Pennsylvania do.** Instead, it charges a documentary fee at the rate of one cent per one hundred dollars of consideration. On a $600,000 sale, that comes to $60—a rounding error compared to the closing costs in most neighboring states.

This fee is collected by the county clerk and recorder at the time the deed is presented for recording. The statutory authority is straightforward: C.R.S. 39-13-102(1) mandates the fee on "each deed or other instrument conveying lands, tenements, or other realty." Exemptions exist for government conveyances, gifts without consideration, and deeds correcting legal descriptions.

The fee applies to the full consideration amount stated in the deed. When agents assist in preparing the Real Estate Transfer Declaration (Form TD-1000), they must ensure the consideration figure matches what appears on the settlement statement and the deed itself.

## Local Transfer Taxes That Catch Agents Off Guard

Here is where Colorado gets complicated. Multiple municipalities impose their own real estate transfer taxes independent of the state documentary fee. The rates and structures vary significantly.

| Jurisdiction | Transfer Tax Rate | Who Typically Pays | Statutory Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Town of Breckenridge | 1.0% of sale price | Split 50/50 by custom | Breckenridge Municipal Code 3-6 |
| Town of Telluride | 3.0% of sale price | Seller by custom | Telluride Town Charter Art. XIV |
| Town of Crested Butte | 1.5% of sale price | Negotiable | CB Municipal Code 17-51 |
| City of Aspen | Exempt (housing TDR fees apply) | Varies | Aspen Land Use Code 26.540 |
| Mountain Village | 1.0% of sale price | Seller by custom | MV Municipal Code 3.16 |

Telluride's 3% rate routinely shocks buyers and sellers unfamiliar with resort-market transactions. Agents listing property in these jurisdictions must disclose the local transfer tax obligation in marketing materials and during initial consultations. Failing to mention a $45,000 transfer tax on a $1.5 million Telluride property is the kind of error that ends in an ethics complaint.

## Recording Fees: County-by-County Breakdown

Recording fees in Colorado are governed by C.R.S. 30-1-103, which sets the base fee structure for county clerks and recorders. The standard recording fee for a deed is $13.00 for the first page and $5.00 for each additional page. However, counties may add surcharges authorized by specific legislation.

**Most Colorado counties now charge between $13 and $30 per document for basic deed recording, with additional per-page fees stacking quickly on complex conveyances.** A deed of trust with riders, a warranty deed, and any corrective instruments each incur separate recording charges. On a typical residential transaction, total recording fees land between $50 and $150.

The [Colorado Secretary of State's office](https://www.sos.state.co.us/) maintains filing guidelines, but agents should reference each county clerk's posted fee schedule directly, as surcharges for affordable housing funds and conservation programs vary. El Paso County, Denver County, and Boulder County each publish updated fee tables annually.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Get Right

The Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC) publishes standard contract forms through its partnership with the Colorado Association of Realtors. The forms most relevant to transfer tax and recording fee compliance include the CBS (Contract to Buy and Sell) series.

For residential transactions, agents work primarily with CREC Form CBS1-6-25 (Contract to Buy and Sell - Residential). Section 12 of this form addresses closing costs, and agents must correctly allocate documentary fees and recording charges in the appropriate blanks. The Settlement Statement, whether using the standard ALTA form or a local title company worksheet, must reconcile with the CBS allocations.

Form TD-1000 (Real Estate Transfer Declaration) is filed with the county assessor's office at the time of recording. This form captures the sale price, property identification, and parties to the transaction. Title companies typically prepare it, but the agent's responsibility is to verify accuracy before closing, particularly the stated consideration amount.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

**The errors that generate complaints and delayed closings tend to cluster around five recurring patterns.** Each one is preventable with basic diligence.

First, agents routinely forget to account for local transfer taxes in net sheet calculations. When a listing agent in Summit County provides a seller net sheet that omits the 1% Breckenridge transfer tax on a $900,000 property, that is a $9,000 surprise at closing. Sellers lose trust immediately, and some refuse to close without renegotiation.

Second, agents misidentify the responsible party for the documentary fee. While custom in most Colorado counties assigns the state documentary fee to the seller, the contract controls. If an agent marks the wrong party in Section 12 of the CBS form and no one catches it until the closing disclosure, the correction creates friction and potential delay. The custom is not law—it is negotiable, and agents must confirm the allocation in writing.

Third, agents fail to verify that the deed's stated consideration matches the contract price. This discrepancy triggers rejection by the county clerk's office when the TD-1000 form contradicts the deed. A rejected deed means title does not transfer on the expected date, which can cascade into rate lock expirations and possession disputes.

Fourth, agents in resort markets neglect to disclose local transfer tax obligations during the listing presentation or buyer consultation. CREC Rule E-35 requires licensees to disclose material facts that affect the transaction. A five-figure local transfer tax qualifies as material. Omitting it from early discussions—even if the title company catches it later—exposes the agent to a complaint under C.R.S. 12-10-217(1)(n) for failing to disclose facts the agent knew or should have known.

Fifth, agents assume recording fees are identical across all 64 Colorado counties. They are not. An agent who estimates recording costs based on Denver County fees will understate costs in counties that have adopted additional surcharges. Always pull the specific county's current schedule before finalizing the settlement statement.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers carry supervisory liability under C.R.S. 12-10-201 and CREC Rule E-2. When an agent under their supervision miscalculates transfer taxes or misallocates recording fees, the broker faces potential discipline alongside the agent.

Brokers should require that every transaction file include documentation of the applicable transfer tax rate for the property's jurisdiction. This is especially critical for brokerages operating across multiple Colorado counties and municipalities. A simple checklist item—"local transfer tax verified: Y/N"—prevents the most common oversight.

| Audit Item | What Broker Should Verify | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| CBS Section 12 allocations | Correct party assigned for each fee | Contract dispute, delayed closing |
| TD-1000 consistency | Consideration matches deed and contract | Deed rejection by county clerk |
| Local transfer tax disclosure | Disclosed in writing before contract | CREC complaint, E&O claim |
| Recording fee estimates | Match actual county schedule | Settlement statement correction, delay |
| Form version currency | Current CREC-approved form used | Voidable contract provisions |

Brokers who run offices in resort communities should conduct quarterly training on local transfer tax changes. Municipalities like Breckenridge and Crested Butte occasionally adjust rates or modify exemption thresholds through ballot measures, and agents may not catch the change without prompting. Similar to how firms handle [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process), transfer tax compliance deserves a documented, repeatable verification step.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The Division of Real Estate investigates complaints filed under C.R.S. 12-10-217, which outlines prohibited conduct for licensees. Misrepresenting costs, failing to disclose material facts, and demonstrating incompetence in handling transaction documents all fall within the statute's reach.

Penalties range from a letter of admonition to license suspension or revocation, depending on severity and history. Monetary fines up to $2,500 per violation are authorized under the statute. Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face civil liability when clients suffer financial harm from inaccurate fee estimates—particularly when the error changes the economics of a deal enough that a party walks away.

Deal cancellation is a real outcome. If a seller discovers at the closing table that they owe an unexpected $15,000 in local transfer taxes, they may exercise any available contractual remedy to terminate. The agent who failed to disclose this cost may then face a claim for the buyer's lost inspection fees, appraisal costs, and other reliance damages.
