## Understanding Mississippi HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Mississippi real estate transactions involving homeowners associations or condominium regimes carry disclosure obligations that trip up even experienced agents. The Mississippi hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 framework pulls from multiple statutes, and noncompliance can unwind a deal or expose licensees to disciplinary action. This post breaks down the statutory requirements, relevant forms, liability exposure, and broker-level audit responsibilities every Mississippi agent and managing broker should have committed to memory this year.

Mississippi does not have a single, unified HOA disclosure statute the way some neighboring states do. Instead, agents must piece together obligations from the Mississippi Condominium Act (Miss. Code Ann. 89-9-1 et seq.), the Mississippi Property Condition Disclosure Act (Miss. Code Ann. 89-1-501 et seq.), and the rules promulgated by the [Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC)](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/). Each body of law imposes distinct timing, content, and delivery requirements.

**Failure to identify which statute applies to your transaction is the single fastest route to a compliance violation.** A property governed by a recorded declaration of covenants but not organized as a condominium falls under different rules than a unit in a registered condominium regime. Agents must make this determination before drafting the purchase agreement.

## The Mississippi Condominium Act: Resale Disclosure Requirements

The Mississippi Condominium Act, codified at Miss. Code Ann. 89-9-1 through 89-9-83, governs the creation, management, and sale of condominium units. Section 89-9-25 is the provision most relevant to resale transactions. It requires that a purchaser receive a resale certificate containing specific financial and governance information before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed.

The resale certificate must include the current monthly assessment amount, any special assessments approved or pending, the association's current budget, the most recent audited or reviewed financial statement, and a statement of any pending litigation involving the association. It must also disclose the amount of reserves held by the association and any known capital expenditure obligations for the coming fiscal year.

Mississippi does not publish a state-mandated resale certificate form with a specific form number the way MREC publishes the Property Condition Disclosure Statement. The certificate is typically prepared by the association's management company or board of directors. However, agents must confirm that every element required under Section 89-9-25 is included—relying on the management company's template alone is insufficient.

## Property Condition Disclosure and the HOA Connection

The Mississippi Property Condition Disclosure Act (Miss. Code Ann. 89-1-501 through 89-1-523) requires sellers to complete the MREC-prescribed Property Condition Disclosure Statement. The current version of this form—referenced as the "Seller's Disclosure Statement" in MREC administrative rules—contains a specific section addressing homeowners association or condominium association membership. Sellers must disclose whether the property is subject to a mandatory HOA, the amount of regular assessments, any known special assessments, and whether the seller is current on all amounts owed.

**Agents who treat the Property Condition Disclosure Statement as a standalone obligation miss the fact that it creates an affirmative trigger.** If the seller checks "yes" for HOA membership, the listing agent must then ensure that governing documents, current financials, and the resale certificate (for condominiums) are assembled and made available to the buyer's side. The disclosure statement alone does not satisfy the full obligation—it merely opens the door.

The MREC Property Condition Disclosure Statement must be delivered to the buyer before or at the time the purchase agreement is signed. For transactions handled through the Mississippi Association of Realtors (MAR) standard forms, the relevant contract addendum is the "HOA/POA Addendum" attached to the residential purchase agreement. This addendum specifies the delivery deadline, the buyer's review period, and the consequences of noncompliance, including how agents should handle [common deadline failures](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in their transaction workflow.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Timing

Mississippi law grants condominium purchasers a 15-day rescission window after receipt of the public offering statement under Miss. Code Ann. 89-9-25. This right applies regardless of what the purchase agreement says. Contractual language attempting to waive or shorten this period is void under the statute.

For non-condominium HOA properties, the cancellation right is governed by the purchase agreement itself rather than by statute. The MAR standard residential contract and its HOA/POA Addendum typically provide a review period of 3 to 7 calendar days after document delivery. If the buyer finds something objectionable in the governing documents or financials during that window, cancellation is without forfeiture of earnest money.

| Scenario | Governing Law | Review Period | Cancellation Penalty |
|----------|--------------|---------------|---------------------|
| Condominium resale | Miss. Code Ann. 89-9-25 | 15 days from receipt | None (statutory right) |
| HOA property (non-condo) | Purchase agreement / MAR Addendum | 3-7 days (negotiable) | None if within period |
| No documents delivered | Miss. Code Ann. 89-9-25 / contract terms | Period never starts | None; buyer may cancel anytime |
| Late delivery of documents | Depends on property type | Restarts from new delivery date | None during review period |

Agents on both sides need to track these deadlines precisely. A listing agent who delays document delivery thinking it protects the seller actually hands the buyer an indefinite cancellation right. ## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

MREC has broad authority under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-21 to discipline licensees who violate statutory disclosure requirements. Sanctions range from a formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation, with fines up to $5,000 per violation. The Commission considers willfulness, harm to consumers, and whether the agent cooperated with the investigation.

Beyond regulatory discipline, agents face civil liability. A buyer who closes without receiving required disclosures can bring a claim for rescission or damages. Mississippi courts have held that a licensee's failure to ensure statutory disclosures were delivered constitutes a breach of the agent's fiduciary duty and, depending on the facts, may amount to negligent misrepresentation.

**Broker liability compounds the risk.** Under MREC Rule 1.4, the qualifying broker is responsible for ensuring that all agents under their supervision comply with disclosure statutes. If an agent fails to deliver required HOA or condo documents, the broker faces the same range of sanctions. Errors and omissions insurance may cover damages but will not cover MREC-imposed fines or the reputational cost of a license action.

Sellers who fail to disclose known HOA deficiencies—unpaid assessments, pending litigation, upcoming special assessments—can be held liable for fraud. When the listing agent knew or should have known about these issues and failed to ensure proper disclosure, the agent shares exposure. Mississippi does not cap damages in fraud actions, meaning the financial downside is theoretically unlimited.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make

The first recurring error is assuming that HOA disclosure rules only apply to condominiums. Mississippi has thousands of planned unit developments governed by mandatory HOAs that are not condominiums. The Property Condition Disclosure Act applies to any property subject to a mandatory association, and the MAR HOA/POA Addendum should be attached whenever assessments exist—regardless of whether the regime is a condominium.

The second mistake is relying on the HOA management company's timeline without building in a buffer. Management companies in Mississippi routinely take 10 to 14 business days to produce a resale certificate or estoppel letter. Agents who wait until the contract is fully executed to order documents often blow past the buyer's review period, creating confusion about whether the period has even begun.

The third error is conflating the estoppel letter with the resale certificate. The estoppel letter confirms what the seller owes the association. The resale certificate (for condominiums) discloses the association's financial health, governance structure, and pending obligations. Both are needed, and one does not substitute for the other.

A fourth common pitfall involves agents failing to review documents themselves before forwarding them to the buyer. If governing documents reveal a right of first refusal, rental restrictions, or an upcoming special assessment that could affect the buyer's intended use, the buyer's agent has a duty to flag it. Simply passing a PDF along without review does not satisfy the agent's advisory obligation under MREC Rule 3.2.

The fifth mistake is omitting disclosure of transfer fees. Many Mississippi HOAs charge a transfer or capital contribution fee at closing, sometimes exceeding $1,000. If this fee is not disclosed in the purchase agreement and accounted for on the closing statement, it surfaces as a last-minute surprise that can delay or kill the transaction.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers should build a compliance checkpoint into every transaction file that involves an HOA or condominium. At minimum, the audit should confirm that the Property Condition Disclosure Statement was delivered on time, that the HOA/POA Addendum is attached and fully completed, and that governing documents and financials are present in the file before the review period expires.

| Audit Item | Document to Verify | Timing Standard |
|------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| HOA existence confirmed | Property Condition Disclosure Statement, Section on associations | Before contract execution |
| HOA/POA Addendum attached | Signed addendum in file | At contract execution |
| Resale certificate (condo) | Certificate from association | Within 10 days of contract |
| Estoppel letter | Letter from management company | At least 7 days before closing |
| Transfer fee disclosed | Addendum or contract provision | Before contract execution |
| Buyer acknowledgment of receipt | Signed receipt or email confirmation with timestamp | Within 24 hours of delivery |

Brokers should also conduct quarterly spot audits of closed files to identify patterns. If a particular agent consistently lacks estoppel letters or resale certificates, that signals a training gap that must be addressed before it becomes a Commission complaint. MREC increasingly uses consumer complaints as the trigger for broader file audits, meaning one sloppy transaction can open the door to review of an agent's entire book of business.

Implementing checklists rather than relying on agent memory is a proven safeguard—especially for teams handling volume. The difference between an agent who tracks these items manually and one who uses [systematic checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) often shows up most clearly in HOA transactions, where a single missing document can restart the buyer's cancellation clock.
