# Vermont Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Get Right

The Vermont buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 represent one of the most significant compliance shifts Vermont agents have faced in a decade. Following the national settlement changes that reshaped buyer representation rules across the country, the Vermont Real Estate Commission has codified specific mandates around written buyer agreements that carry real penalties for noncompliance. If your brokerage has not audited its processes since these rules took effect, you are operating on borrowed time.

Vermont has historically been a state where agency relationships were governed more by custom than by rigid statutory procedure. That era is over. The written buyer agency agreement is now a hard prerequisite before you open a lockbox, pull up a listing on your phone in the car, or send a buyer a CMA. Understanding exactly what the state demands, which forms satisfy those demands, and where agents keep tripping up is no longer optional reading.

## The Statutory Framework Behind Vermont's Buyer Agency Rules

Vermont real estate licensing and agency law is governed primarily by [26 V.S.A. Chapter 41](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/26/041) and the administrative rules promulgated by the Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC), which operates under the Office of Professional Regulation (OPR). The Commission's administrative rules, found in VREC Rule 13, spell out agency disclosure obligations and the requirement for written agreements before representing a buyer in any transaction.

**VREC Rule 13.3, as amended to align with the 2024-2025 NAR settlement provisions, now requires that a licensee obtain a signed, written buyer agency agreement before engaging in any real estate brokerage services on behalf of a prospective buyer.** This includes showing properties, preparing offers, advising on pricing strategy, or arranging access to MLS data for a specific buyer's benefit. The rule does not carve out exceptions for open houses when the agent intends to represent the buyer going forward.

The statute at 26 V.S.A. Section 2296 also addresses mandatory agency disclosure, requiring that agents present the Vermont Mandatory Agency Disclosure form at the first substantive contact with any party. The buyer agency agreement is a separate obligation that layers on top of this disclosure requirement, not a replacement for it.

## Which Forms Satisfy the Requirement

The Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) publishes the standard forms that most brokerages across the state rely on. For buyer agency agreements, the primary form is the VAR-400, the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement. There are also variant forms for limited-service and non-exclusive arrangements.

Each form must contain several elements to satisfy VREC's requirements. The agreement must state a definite duration, identify the scope of services the agent will provide, disclose how and by whom the agent will be compensated, and include a clear description of the buyer's obligations. Any agreement that omits a termination date or leaves compensation terms ambiguous will not pass muster under VREC audit.

| Form Number | Form Name | Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| VAR-400 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Standard exclusive buyer representation |
| VAR-401 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Buyer working with multiple agents |
| VAR-402 | Limited-Service Buyer Agency Agreement | Reduced-scope representation |
| VAR Mandatory Disclosure | Agency Disclosure Form | Required at first substantive contact with any party |

Agents should note that brokerages may supplement the VAR forms with their own addenda, but any brokerage-specific form must still meet every minimum element required by VREC Rule 13.3. If your brokerage has developed its own buyer agreement template, it should have been reviewed by legal counsel against the current rule text.

## Vermont Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: Key Provisions Agents Must Understand

Several provisions inside the buyer agency agreement carry consequences that agents routinely underestimate. Compensation disclosure is the one drawing the most confusion. Under the updated rules, the buyer agreement must specify the amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, stated as a fixed dollar figure, a percentage, or a combination. It cannot contain open-ended language like "as customary" or "per MLS offering."

**The agreement must also disclose whether the agent's compensation may be paid by the seller, the listing broker, or the buyer directly, and what happens if the seller's offered compensation is less than the amount stated in the agreement.** This gap-fill provision is where many disputes arise post-closing, and agents who leave this section vague are inviting arbitration claims.

Duration matters too. VREC has not set a statutory maximum, but the Commission's guidance letters strongly recommend terms no longer than six months unless the buyer's circumstances justify a longer period. A 12-month exclusive agreement with a first-time buyer purchasing a primary residence will raise red flags during any compliance review. Most Vermont brokerages have settled on 90-to-180-day terms as the defensible default.

Termination provisions are another area of focus. The agreement must explain the conditions under which either party can terminate the relationship and what obligations survive termination, particularly around compensation for properties already shown or offers already submitted during the agreement period.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make

Even experienced agents are making errors that expose them and their brokerages to liability. Here are the five most frequent mistakes VREC complaints and brokerage audits are surfacing.

First, agents are presenting the buyer agency agreement at the wrong time. The rules require a signed agreement before substantive services begin. Sending a buyer three listings via email and then asking them to sign the agreement at the first showing is too late. The email with curated listings already constitutes brokerage services under VREC's interpretation. Similar missteps are common in [other states with strict agency requirements](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026), but Vermont's enforcement posture makes the timing issue particularly risky here.

Second, agents are using outdated forms. VAR updated its buyer agency forms in late 2025 to align with the settlement-driven rule changes. Any agent still using a pre-2025 VAR-400 is submitting a form that does not contain the required compensation disclosure language. This is an easy audit failure.

Third, compensation terms are vague or contradictory. Agents write "3%" in the agreement but then submit an offer where the seller is covering buyer agent compensation at 2.5%, without addressing who covers the 0.5% gap. When the buyer gets to closing and discovers a line-item charge they did not expect, complaints follow.

Fourth, agents are neglecting to provide a copy of the signed agreement to the buyer. VREC Rule 13.3(d) requires the agent to deliver a fully executed copy to the buyer within 24 hours of signing. Agents who collect the signature on a tablet at a coffee shop and forget to email the PDF are technically in violation.

Fifth, agents are modifying VAR form language without broker approval. Striking through clauses, adding handwritten terms in the margins, or attaching informal addenda without having the managing broker review the changes creates enforceability problems and exposes the brokerage to vicarious liability.

## Consequences of Noncompliance

Failing to secure a compliant buyer agency agreement is not a technicality the Commission overlooks. VREC has the authority under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299 to impose disciplinary action ranging from a formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation. Fines of up to $1,000 per violation are authorized, and each transaction in which the agent failed to obtain a proper agreement can be treated as a separate violation.

**Beyond regulatory penalties, the practical consequences hit agents in the wallet.** An agent who cannot produce a valid buyer agency agreement may lose any legal claim to compensation in a disputed transaction. Vermont courts have held that absent a written agreement, a buyer's agent has no enforceable right to a commission, even if the agent invested months of work showing properties and negotiating terms. The agreement is the agent's proof of entitlement.

| Violation | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|
| No written agreement before services | VREC fine up to $1,000; loss of commission entitlement |
| Outdated or non-compliant form used | Formal reprimand; required corrective education |
| Failure to deliver copy to buyer within 24 hours | VREC citation; grounds for buyer complaint |
| Vague compensation terms | Arbitration exposure; buyer claim for refund at closing |
| Unauthorized form modifications without broker review | Brokerage vicarious liability; potential E&O claim |

There is also the E&O insurance dimension. Many errors-and-omissions policies exclude coverage for claims arising from an agent's failure to follow mandatory regulatory procedures. If your buyer agency agreement was deficient and a claim results, do not assume your insurance will cover it. Check your policy language now, not after a claim is filed.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry supervisory liability under Vermont law. If an agent in your office is showing property without a signed buyer agency agreement, VREC can hold the broker responsible under 26 V.S.A. Section 2297's supervisory duty provisions. Passive oversight is not a defense.

Brokers should be auditing three things on a recurring basis. First, verify that every active buyer file contains a signed, current-version buyer agency agreement dated before the first substantive service entry in the file. Second, confirm that copies were delivered to buyers within the 24-hour window and that delivery is documented, whether by email timestamp, transaction management system log, or signed receipt. Third, review any agent-modified agreements for unauthorized changes that could undermine enforceability.

Establishing a standard file checklist is one of the most effective defenses against compliance drift. Agents who rely on memory alone tend to [miss critical deadlines and documentation steps](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), and the buyer agency agreement is exactly the type of early-stage requirement that gets skipped when an eager buyer wants to see a property immediately. Building the agreement into a mandatory first step in your transaction management system removes the possibility of forgetting it.

Brokers should also run quarterly training sessions specifically on buyer agency compliance. This is not something you cover once at orientation and forget. The rules are still settling into practice across Vermont, and agents need repeated reinforcement until the new workflow becomes automatic. Review [why agents commonly miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and build accountability structures that prevent compliance lapses before they happen.

## Protecting Your Practice Going Forward

The written buyer agency agreement is the foundational document of every buyer-side transaction in Vermont. Treat it with the same care you give purchase contracts and disclosure forms. Store executed copies in your transaction file immediately, reference them when submitting offers, and never assume a verbal understanding with a buyer substitutes for the signed document.
