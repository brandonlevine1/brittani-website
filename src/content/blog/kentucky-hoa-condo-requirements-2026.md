## Understanding Kentucky HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Kentucky handles homeowner association and condominium disclosures through two separate statutory frameworks, and confusing them is one of the fastest ways to expose your brokerage to liability. The Kentucky hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 have not fundamentally changed from recent years, but enforcement expectations from the Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) continue to tighten as more buyers challenge transactions that skipped disclosure steps. If you list or sell property in a governed community, the statutes below control your obligations.

The Kentucky Condominium Act (KRS Chapter 381.8105 through 381.890) governs condominium resales exclusively. Non-condo HOA properties fall under the Kentucky Homeowners' Association Act (KRS 381.9601 through 381.9992). These are not interchangeable, and the forms, deadlines, and cancellation rights differ between them.

## The Two Statutes You Must Know

**KRS 381.8325 is the resale certificate statute for condominiums.** It requires the seller—through the association—to furnish a resale certificate containing specific financial and governance information before the buyer is bound. The certificate must include current assessments, any pending special assessments, the association's insurance coverage, and whether the unit is compliant with the declaration and bylaws.

KRS 381.9685 covers non-condominium HOA communities. It mandates a similar but distinct resale disclosure package that includes annual assessment amounts, any outstanding violations attributed to the property, the association's reserve fund balance, and pending litigation involving the association. The two statutes impose different buyer cancellation windows—fifteen days for condos, five days for HOA properties—which creates real scheduling pressure during a transaction.

| Requirement | Condo (KRS 381.8325) | HOA (KRS 381.9685) |
|---|---|---|
| Cancellation period | 15 days from receipt | 5 days from receipt |
| Who prepares certificate | Association or management company | Association or management company |
| Default cost responsibility | Seller | Seller |
| Must include reserve fund info | Yes | Yes |
| Must include pending litigation | Yes | Yes |
| Must include insurance summary | Yes | No explicit requirement |

Agents working in markets like Louisville, Lexington, and Northern Kentucky encounter both property types regularly. Misidentifying a condominium as a simple HOA property—or vice versa—changes the buyer's legal cancellation rights, which is a material error.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

Kentucky does not prescribe a single state-created form for HOA or condo resale certificates the way some states do. Instead, the association itself (or its management company) generates the certificate based on the statutory requirements. However, agents must connect this process to the Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) transaction forms they already use.

The KAR Form 205 (Seller's Disclosure of Property Condition) includes a section requiring the seller to disclose whether the property is subject to an HOA or condominium regime. This is your first trigger point. If the seller checks "yes," you must initiate the resale certificate request immediately—not after inspection, not at the title commitment stage.

**The KAR Form 380 (Condominium Resale Certificate Request) is the standard mechanism for requesting the condo packet from the association.** For non-condo HOAs, agents typically use a brokerage-drafted request letter or the management company's own intake form. Either way, the request should be documented in writing and time-stamped in your file.

The purchase contract itself—typically KAR Form 200 or 210—should reference the disclosure packet delivery as a condition, and many agents add a contingency addendum specifying the exact delivery timeline. Without this contractual hook, disputes over late delivery become harder to resolve.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Timing

Timing is where these transactions get dangerous. Under KRS 381.8325, a condominium buyer who receives the resale certificate has fifteen calendar days to cancel the purchase contract without stating a reason and without forfeiting earnest money. Under KRS 381.9685, the HOA equivalent window is five calendar days.

If the certificate is never delivered, the buyer's cancellation right never expires. This means a buyer could theoretically cancel weeks into the transaction—even after appraisal and financing contingencies have been released—if the resale certificate was never properly provided. Agents who treat the certificate as optional paperwork are handing buyers an indefinite exit ramp.

The clock starts on actual receipt, not on the date of mailing or the date the seller requested the certificate. Documenting delivery with a signed acknowledgment or tracked email timestamp protects everyone. ## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

KREC has the authority to discipline licensees who fail to ensure material disclosures are delivered. Under [KRS 324.160](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/statutes/statute.aspx?id=54012), the Commission can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional continuing education. A missing resale certificate that leads to buyer harm is exactly the kind of violation KREC investigates.

Beyond regulatory consequences, civil liability is real. A buyer who closes without receiving the resale certificate and later discovers undisclosed special assessments or litigation has a colorable claim against both the seller and the listing agent. Kentucky courts have held that agents owe a duty of competent service that includes facilitating required disclosures.

**Deal cancellations are the most common immediate consequence.** When a buyer's attorney discovers the resale certificate was never delivered mid-transaction, the standard advice is to invoke the statutory cancellation right—blowing up the deal days before closing and leaving your seller without a contract.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Kentucky HOA Transactions

**First, agents frequently confuse the condo and HOA statutes.** A property in a condominium regime governed by KRS Chapter 381.8105 has a fifteen-day cancellation window. A townhome in a planned community governed by KRS 381.9601 has a five-day window. Applying the wrong timeline to a contract creates either an artificially shortened buyer right (exposing you to liability) or an unnecessarily long contingency period (frustrating sellers).

Second, agents assume the title company will handle the resale certificate. Title companies in Kentucky do order HOA estoppel letters for payoff purposes, but an estoppel letter is not the same document as the statutory resale certificate. The estoppel covers outstanding balances owed; the resale certificate covers governance, litigation, reserves, and assessments. You need both, and the resale certificate must reach the buyer within the contractual timeframe—not just appear in the closing package.

Third, listing agents delay requesting the certificate until the property goes under contract. Many Kentucky associations and management companies take seven to fourteen business days to produce a resale certificate. If you wait until contract execution, you may burn half the buyer's cancellation window before the document even exists. Best practice is to order the certificate at listing or immediately upon receiving an offer.

Fourth, agents fail to document delivery. Handing a stack of HOA papers to a buyer at a showing or emailing an attachment without confirmation creates an evidentiary gap. If the buyer later claims they never received the resale certificate, and you have no signed receipt or read-confirmation, the cancellation right remains open. Every delivery should be tracked and filed.

Fifth, agents omit the resale certificate contingency from the purchase contract entirely. While the statutory cancellation right exists regardless of contract language, including an explicit contingency with a defined delivery deadline prevents ambiguity and gives both parties a clear framework for performance. Skipping this step invites disputes over whether the buyer waived their right or whether delivery was timely.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under KRS 324.160 for their agents' disclosure failures. A single missed resale certificate that results in litigation or a KREC complaint reflects directly on the brokerage's compliance posture. Proactive auditing is not optional—it is a cost-of-doing-business requirement.

| Audit Point | What to Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Seller disclosure (Form 205) | HOA/condo box checked correctly | Every listing file |
| Resale certificate request | Written request with date stamp in file | Within 48 hours of contract |
| Buyer receipt confirmation | Signed acknowledgment or email timestamp | Before cancellation window closes |
| Correct statute applied | Condo vs. HOA classification matches deed/declaration | At listing intake |
| Contract contingency language | Resale certificate delivery referenced in contract | Every HOA/condo transaction |

Brokers should build a transaction checklist that flags HOA/condo properties at intake and auto-assigns the resale certificate task with a hard deadline. Manual tracking across spreadsheets breaks down as transaction volume increases. This is an area where [structured checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) every time.

Quarterly file audits should specifically pull a sample of closed HOA/condo transactions and verify that the resale certificate, buyer acknowledgment, and contract contingency language all appear in the file. Missing any one of these documents is a compliance gap that a KREC auditor or opposing counsel will find.

## How This Intersects With Seller Disclosure Obligations

The resale certificate is an association-generated document, but the seller still has independent disclosure duties under KRS 324.360 (the Kentucky seller disclosure statute). Agents sometimes assume the resale certificate replaces the seller's duty to disclose known HOA issues—it does not.

If a seller knows about upcoming special assessments that the association has discussed but not yet formally approved, that knowledge must appear on the seller's disclosure form even if the resale certificate omits it. The certificate reflects the association's current records; the seller's disclosure reflects the seller's personal knowledge. These are complementary obligations.
