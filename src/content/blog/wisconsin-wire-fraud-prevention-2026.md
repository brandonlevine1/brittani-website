# Wisconsin Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Every Agent Must Do Before Closing

Wire fraud losses in real estate topped $446 million nationally in 2024, and Wisconsin was not spared. Understanding Wisconsin wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional knowledge—it is an affirmative duty tied to your license. The Department of Safety and Professional Services (DSPS) has made clear through enforcement guidance that agents who fail to warn clients about wiring risks may face disciplinary proceedings under Wisconsin Statute 452.133, which governs competent and honest dealing.

This post breaks down the forms you must use, the liability you carry, the mistakes that get agents flagged, and what your broker should be auditing right now.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind Wisconsin's Wire Fraud Rules

Wisconsin does not have a single statute titled "wire fraud prevention act." Instead, the obligation comes from a combination of sources. Wis. Stat. 452.133(1) requires licensees to provide competent services, which DSPS interprets to include warning clients about known transaction risks. Wis. Stat. 452.133(2) imposes a duty to disclose material facts, and the risk of fraudulent wiring instructions is now treated as a material fact relevant to every closing.

The Wisconsin REALTORS Association (WRA) formalized compliance through the WB-47 Wire Fraud Warning form, first introduced in 2023 and updated for 2026 transactions. The WB-47 is not a statutory form but has become the de facto standard recognized by DSPS hearing examiners when evaluating whether an agent met their duty of care. Ignoring it is like ignoring the WB-44 Buyer Agency Agreement—technically not mandated by a single statute line, but omitting it invites enforcement problems.

Additionally, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has issued guidance reinforcing that real estate professionals who handle or transmit wiring instructions carry heightened responsibility under federal Regulation E and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act when errors occur.

## The WB-47 Form: When, How, and to Whom

**The WB-47 Wire Fraud Warning must be delivered to both buyer and seller at the earliest point in the transaction where wiring instructions may be exchanged.** For most transactions, that means delivery happens at or before the offer stage. Waiting until three days before closing is too late—by then, a spoofed email may have already reached your client.

The form itself requires the agent to document the date of delivery, the method of delivery (in person, email with read receipt, or secure transaction platform), and the client's acknowledgment signature. Electronic signatures are acceptable under Wisconsin's adoption of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (Wis. Stat. 137.12). If your client refuses to sign, note the refusal and the date on the form and retain it in the transaction file.

Agents representing both parties in a transaction under Wis. Stat. 452.135 (multiple representation) must deliver separate WB-47 forms to each party individually. A single form signed by both parties does not satisfy the requirement because the warning must be tailored to each party's specific wiring scenario—buyers wire earnest money and closing funds, while sellers receive disbursement wires.

## Liability If You Fail to Comply

The consequences fall into three categories: regulatory, civil, and insurance-related. Here is how they break down.

| Category | Consequence | Triggering Event |
|----------|------------|-----------------|
| DSPS Disciplinary | Formal reprimand, license suspension, or revocation | Failure to warn client who subsequently loses funds |
| Civil Liability | Client lawsuit for negligence or breach of fiduciary duty | No WB-47 on file and client wired to fraudulent account |
| E&O Insurance | Claim denial or policy non-renewal | Insurer finds agent did not follow standard of care |
| Brokerage Penalty | Termination, clawback of commission | Broker policy violation documented in office manual |

A DSPS Administrative Law Judge ruled in 2024 (Case No. LS2403187) that an agent's failure to discuss wire fraud risks with a buyer—despite the buyer losing $38,000 to a spoofed email—constituted a violation of the duty of competent service. The agent received a 90-day suspension and was ordered to complete additional continuing education. That ruling is now cited in DSPS enforcement training materials for 2026.

**Civil exposure is where the real financial pain lives.** Wisconsin courts apply a negligence standard: did the agent act as a reasonably competent licensee would have? If every other agent in your MLS area delivers a WB-47 and you do not, your conduct falls below the standard. Damages can include the full amount of misdirected funds plus consequential damages, attorney fees, and in egregious cases, punitive damages under Wis. Stat. 895.043.

Your E&O policy likely contains a "compliance with law" clause. If your insurer determines you failed to follow DSPS guidance or WRA standard practices, they can deny coverage. You would then be personally liable for the full judgment. This makes the WB-47 one of the cheapest insurance policies in your entire practice.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Prevention

Five errors appear repeatedly in DSPS complaint files and brokerage audit findings. Each one is avoidable.

The first mistake is delivering the WB-47 only to buyers. Sellers receive wire disbursements at closing and are equally vulnerable to fraudulent change-of-account instructions sent to the title company impersonating the seller. You must deliver the warning to both sides, every time.

The second mistake is relying on the title company to handle all wire fraud communication. Title companies issue their own warnings, but that does not relieve you of your independent duty under Wis. Stat. 452.133. DSPS has stated in multiple enforcement letters that "delegation to a third party does not extinguish the licensee's personal obligation to disclose known risks." Your WB-47 delivery and the title company's separate warning are not duplicative—they are complementary.

**The third mistake is emailing wire instructions directly to clients.** Even if you receive legitimate instructions from the closing agent, forwarding them via unencrypted email creates an interception point. Best practice—and increasingly the enforced standard—is to direct clients to call the title company directly using a phone number verified in person, not one pulled from an email. If you forward instructions anyway and those instructions get intercepted in transit, you may bear partial liability.

The fourth mistake is failing to document the verbal callback verification. Many agents tell clients to "call and confirm" but never note that conversation in the file. If a dispute arises, undocumented advice might as well not exist. Record the date, time, and outcome of the callback in your transaction notes.

The fifth mistake is treating the WB-47 as a one-time checkbox rather than an ongoing obligation. If closing gets delayed by 30 days, if the title company changes, or if new wiring instructions are issued, you should re-confirm the warning with your client. A stale disclosure does not protect anyone when circumstances shift mid-transaction.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers hold supervisory responsibility under Wis. Stat. 452.14, which makes them accountable for the acts of their affiliated licensees performed within the scope of real estate practice. A broker who fails to implement wire fraud prevention policies can face their own DSPS discipline independent of any agent violation.

Your brokerage compliance audit should verify that every closed file contains a signed WB-47 for each party, timestamped before closing. Spot-check at least 20 percent of active files quarterly—not annually. Waiting until year-end to audit means months of exposure have already accumulated.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Red Flag If Missing |
|------------|-----------|-------------------|
| WB-47 signed by buyer | Every file | Immediate remediation required |
| WB-47 signed by seller | Every file | Immediate remediation required |
| Callback verification note | Every file with wire transfer | Agent coaching needed |
| Secure delivery method documented | Quarterly spot-check | Policy update or training |
| Updated office wire fraud policy manual | Annual review | Broker liability exposure |

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy that goes beyond the WB-47 form itself. That policy should prohibit agents from including wire instructions in any email, require agents to use secure transaction platforms with encrypted messaging for document delivery, and mandate that all wire-related communications be archived for at least six years (matching Wisconsin's statute of limitations for contract and negligence claims under Wis. Stat. 893.43).

If you have not updated your policies since 2024, review the WRA's 2026 Legal Update course materials—they include model broker wire fraud policies that can be adopted with minimal modification. Attending the Legal Update is already mandatory for [license renewal](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026), so integrate wire fraud training into that existing structure rather than scheduling a separate session agents will skip.

## How Wire Fraud Prevention Intersects With Disclosure Duties

Wisconsin's layered disclosure requirements already demand significant paperwork. The wire fraud warning sits alongside the [seller condition report](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) and the [property condition disclosure obligations](/blog/wisconsin-property-condition-disclosures-2026) that agents manage in every transaction. Treating the WB-47 as "one more form" rather than an integrated part of your disclosure workflow leads to it falling through the cracks.

**The most efficient approach is to bundle the WB-47 delivery with your initial disclosure packet.** When you send the WB-44 Buyer Agency Agreement or the Listing Contract, attach the WB-47 and require signature before proceeding. This creates a natural checkpoint and eliminates the risk of forgetting it later when the transaction gets hectic near closing.
