## Why South Carolina Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026 Demand Precision

Missing a single deadline in a South Carolina residential transaction can cost your client the deal—or cost you your license. South Carolina buyer contingency deadlines 2026 are governed by a combination of statutory requirements under the South Carolina Code of Laws, SC REALTORS (SCR) standard forms, and the regulatory oversight of the South Carolina Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation (SCLLR). Understanding how these deadlines interact is not optional; it is the baseline expectation for any licensed agent operating in this state.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission, housed within SCLLR, has repeatedly emphasized deadline compliance in disciplinary actions over the past two years. Agents who treat contingency periods as approximate rather than absolute invite formal complaints, E&O claims, and deal collapse. This post breaks down the specific forms, timelines, and enforcement mechanisms you need to know heading into the second half of 2026.

## The Forms That Govern Contingency Timelines

**Every contingency deadline in a standard South Carolina residential transaction traces back to SCR Form 310 (Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate).** This is the primary purchase contract form published by SC REALTORS for use in residential sales. Paragraph-by-paragraph, it establishes the framework for inspection, financing, appraisal, and due diligence contingencies.

Here are the key forms agents must track:

| Form Number | Form Name | Contingency Function |
|---|---|---|
| SCR 310 | Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate | Master contract; all primary contingency deadlines |
| SCR 311 | Addendum to Agreement to Buy and Sell | Additional or modified contingency terms |
| SCR 320 | Repair Request | Buyer's response within inspection window |
| SCR 325 | Seller's Response to Repair Request | Seller's counter within stated timeline |
| SCR 350 | Termination of Contract | Formal cancellation within contingency period |

The deadlines themselves are not pre-printed. They are negotiated between the parties and written into the blanks on Form 310. This means the buyer's agent is responsible for ensuring the dates entered are realistic, that the client understands them, and that all required notices are delivered before expiration.

When parties use SCR Form 311 to add contingencies—such as a sale-of-home contingency or a septic inspection—the same deadline logic applies. If the addendum lacks a specific date, courts and the Commission will look to the "reasonable time" standard under South Carolina contract law, which creates unpredictable outcomes agents should avoid.

## Inspection Contingency: The Most Commonly Botched Deadline

The inspection contingency under Paragraph 5 of SCR Form 310 gives the buyer a specified number of calendar days to conduct inspections and deliver one of three responses: acceptance of the property as-is, a written repair request via SCR Form 320, or termination via SCR Form 350. The clock starts ticking from the binding agreement date—not from when the agent "gets around to" scheduling the inspector.

**Failure to deliver written notice before the inspection deadline expires results in automatic waiver of the contingency.** This is not a gray area. The form language is explicit: if the buyer does not respond in writing by the deadline, the buyer accepts the property in its current condition and forfeits the right to terminate under that contingency.

Agents routinely [miss deadlines due to poor tracking habits](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) rather than ignorance of the rules. A 10-day inspection window on a contract executed on a Monday means the deadline falls on the following Thursday—including weekends. The only exception is if the final day lands on a Saturday, Sunday, or South Carolina state holiday, in which case the deadline slides to the next business day.

One critical nuance: delivery of notices under SCR Form 310 must comply with Paragraph 16, which specifies acceptable methods including personal delivery, email to the address in the contract, or fax. Texting your repair request to the listing agent does not constitute valid delivery unless the contract explicitly permits it.

## Financing and Appraisal Contingencies

Paragraph 7 of SCR Form 310 addresses the financing contingency. The buyer must make a good-faith loan application within the number of days stated in the contract and must obtain a commitment letter by the financing deadline. If the buyer cannot secure financing by that date, they must notify the seller in writing to invoke the contingency and receive a refund of earnest money.

The appraisal contingency, addressed in Paragraph 6, operates independently. If the property appraises below the purchase price, the buyer has until the appraisal contingency deadline to either terminate, negotiate, or waive the shortfall. These two contingencies run concurrently in most transactions, which means agents must track them as separate obligations with separate deadlines—not as a single "loan stuff" bucket.

| Contingency | Typical Timeframe | Trigger for Waiver | Form for Termination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inspection | 10-15 calendar days | No written response by deadline | SCR 350 |
| Financing | 21-30 calendar days | No written notice of inability to obtain loan | SCR 350 |
| Appraisal | Same as financing or separate date | No written objection after low appraisal | SCR 350 |
| Sale of Home | Varies by negotiation | Failure to deliver contract on existing home | SCR 350 |

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission's [disciplinary actions database](https://llr.sc.gov/re/) shows that financing-related deadline disputes are the second most common basis for formal complaints against buyer's agents, trailing only trust account violations.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Contingency Deadlines

Three to five errors appear with alarming regularity in SCLLR disciplinary files and E&O claims. Understanding them is the fastest way to protect your license.

**First, agents confuse "delivery" with "sending."** Under Paragraph 16 of SCR Form 310, notice is effective upon receipt, not upon transmission. An email sent at 11:58 PM on the deadline day means nothing if it bounces back or lands in a spam folder. Agents must confirm actual receipt and document that confirmation.

Second, agents fail to account for the earnest money deposit deadline as its own contingency-adjacent obligation. Paragraph 3 of Form 310 requires the buyer to deliver earnest money within a stated number of days. Missing this deadline gives the seller potential grounds for termination and creates an immediate trust account compliance issue under S.C. Code Section 40-57-137.

Third, buyer's agents verbally extend deadlines without written amendments. A phone call between agents saying "take another three days" is legally meaningless. Without a signed SCR Form 311 or other written amendment executed by both parties, the original deadline controls. Agents who rely on verbal agreements expose their clients—and themselves—to breach-of-contract claims.

Fourth, agents calculate deadlines from the wrong start date. The binding agreement date under SCR Form 310 is the date the last party signs—not the date of the initial offer, not the date the agent delivers the signed contract, and not the date both parties' agents have "acknowledged" the deal. Getting this wrong by even one day can eliminate an entire contingency window.

Fifth, agents neglect to calendar multiple deadlines independently. Transactions with a sale-of-home contingency, inspection contingency, and financing contingency have at least three separate clocks running. Treating them as one aggregate timeline is a recipe for missed obligations. This is precisely [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in multi-contingency contracts.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

The broker-in-charge carries direct supervisory liability under S.C. Code Section 40-57-145 and SCLLR Rule 137-14. If an affiliated agent misses a contingency deadline that results in client harm, the Commission does not stop at the agent—they examine whether the broker had systems in place to prevent the failure.

**Brokers should audit every active file for three specific compliance points.** First, verify that all contingency deadlines in the executed contract are calendared in a firm-wide tracking system—not just the agent's personal calendar or phone reminders. Second, confirm that all notices delivered under contingency provisions are time-stamped, saved in the transaction file, and accompanied by proof of receipt. Third, ensure that any deadline extensions are memorialized in signed written amendments before the original deadline expires.

SCLLR has indicated in recent consent orders that brokers who demonstrate documented supervisory systems receive significantly reduced penalties compared to those operating without audit trails. A [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that flags approaching deadlines across the brokerage is no longer a best practice—it is a de facto regulatory expectation.

Brokers should also pay attention to how agents use SCR Form 350 (Termination of Contract). An improperly executed termination—one delivered after the contingency period expires—does not terminate the contract. It constitutes a breach. If your agent sends Form 350 one day late, your brokerage may be on the hook for the buyer's lost earnest money and potentially the seller's damages.

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

The consequences of missing a South Carolina buyer contingency deadline cascade quickly. For the buyer, a missed inspection deadline means accepting the property as-is—potentially absorbing tens of thousands in undisclosed repair costs. For the agent, it means an E&O claim and a possible SCLLR complaint.

Under S.C. Code Section 40-57-110(A), the Commission may impose fines up to $1,000 per violation, require additional education, suspend a license for up to one year, or permanently revoke it. These penalties apply to both the agent and the broker-in-charge if supervisory failures contributed to the violation. The Commission also has authority to issue public reprimands that appear on the agent's permanent licensing record.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability for negligent misrepresentation or breach of fiduciary duty. South Carolina courts have consistently held that a real estate agent's failure to track and communicate deadlines constitutes a breach of the duty of competence owed to the client. The 2019 Appellate Court decision in Brisson v. Santee reinforced that agents cannot delegate deadline tracking to clients and then claim the client "should have known."
