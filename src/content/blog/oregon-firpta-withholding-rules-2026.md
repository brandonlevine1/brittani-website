## Understanding Oregon FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The Oregon FIRPTA withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 catch agents off guard more often than any other compliance issue in cross-border transactions. Federal law under the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (IRC Section 1445) requires buyers to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when purchasing real property from a "foreign person." Oregon layers its own nonresident withholding requirement on top, creating a dual obligation that can freeze proceeds, delay closings, and expose agents to liability if mishandled.

**This is not a title company problem alone — it is a listing agent problem, a buyer's agent problem, and a broker supervision problem.** The IRS does not care who dropped the ball. When withholding fails, transferee liability lands squarely on the buyer, and the professional reputations of every agent in the transaction take the hit.

The stakes are real. A $750,000 sale to a foreign national means $112,500 in federal withholding alone, before Oregon's separate state bite. Agents who treat FIRPTA as someone else's job are gambling with six-figure sums and their license.

## Federal FIRPTA Requirements That Apply in Oregon

IRC Section 1445 mandates that a transferee (buyer) withhold 15% of the "amount realized" — typically the sales price — when acquiring a U.S. real property interest from a foreign person. The term "foreign person" includes nonresident alien individuals, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates. U.S. citizens living abroad are not foreign persons for FIRPTA purposes.

The withholding must be remitted to the IRS within 20 days of the closing date using [IRS Form 8288](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8288) (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Form 8288-B is the application for a withholding certificate if the seller wants to reduce or eliminate the withholding amount before closing.

There are limited exemptions. The most commonly invoked in Oregon residential transactions is the buyer's residence exception: if the sales price is $300,000 or less and the buyer intends to occupy the property as a personal residence for at least 50% of the days it is in use during each of the first two 12-month periods, the withholding rate is zero. Between $300,001 and $1,000,000, the rate drops to 10% under the same residence-use requirement.

| Sales Price Range | Buyer Plans to Reside | Withholding Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Yes | 0% |
| $300,001 - $1,000,000 | Yes | 10% |
| Over $1,000,000 | Yes or No | 15% |
| Any amount | No (investment property) | 15% |

## Oregon's Separate Nonresident Withholding Under ORS 314.258

Oregon does not simply piggyback on FIRPTA. The state has its own independent withholding requirement for nonresident sellers under ORS 314.258, administered by the Oregon Department of Revenue. This applies to any seller who is not an Oregon resident, including U.S. citizens from other states and foreign nationals alike.

**The Oregon withholding is based on the seller's estimated tax liability on the gain, not on the gross sales price.** The standard withholding rate is the greater of (a) 8% of the net gain, or (b) 4% of the gross sales price, unless the seller files Form OR-WC (Oregon Withholding Certificate) with the Department of Revenue to reduce the amount. Sellers with little or no gain — for instance, those selling at a loss — can apply for partial or complete exemption through this form.

Closing agents in Oregon, including title companies and escrow officers, are designated as the "remitters" responsible for collecting and transmitting the state withholding to the Oregon Department of Revenue. This does not eliminate the agent's duty to flag the issue early. If the listing agent knows the seller is nonresident or foreign and fails to communicate this to the closing agent, the deal can implode days before the scheduled closing when the title company finally discovers the problem and must scramble for withholding calculations.

## How the Dual Withholding Works in Practice

Foreign sellers in Oregon face both federal and state withholding simultaneously. These are separate obligations, reported on different forms, sent to different agencies, and calculated differently. Agents who conflate them — or assume one satisfies the other — create compliance nightmares.

Consider a foreign national selling a Portland condo for $600,000 with a cost basis of $400,000. The federal FIRPTA withholding at the reduced residence rate (assuming a qualifying buyer) would be $60,000 (10% of $600,000). The Oregon withholding would be the greater of 8% of the $200,000 gain ($16,000) or 4% of the gross price ($24,000) — so $24,000. Total withheld: $84,000.

| Obligation | Basis of Calculation | Amount Withheld | Form Filed | Filed With |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal FIRPTA (10% rate) | Gross sales price | $60,000 | IRS Form 8288 / 8288-A | IRS |
| Oregon ORS 314.258 | Greater of gain% or gross% | $24,000 | OR-WC (if reducing) | Oregon Dept. of Revenue |
| Total | — | $84,000 | — | — |

The seller recovers any over-withholding by filing a federal tax return (Form 1040-NR) and an Oregon nonresident return (Form OR-40-N). But those refunds can take 6-12 months, which is why savvy listing agents encourage foreign sellers to file IRS Form 8288-B for a withholding certificate well before closing.

## Common Mistakes Oregon Agents Make with FIRPTA

The first and most frequent error is assuming the title company will handle everything. Title companies and escrow officers in Oregon will typically ask for the seller's FIRPTA certification, but they are not your compliance safety net. If the seller lies on their affidavit and the listing agent had reason to know the seller was foreign, the buyer — and by extension, the buyer's agent — may still face exposure.

The second mistake is failing to ask the right question at listing intake. Many agents ask "Are you a U.S. citizen?" but FIRPTA's definition of "foreign person" also captures resident aliens who have abandoned their green cards, foreign entities holding title through LLCs, and trusts settled by foreign persons. The question must be more precise: "Are you a U.S. person as defined by the IRS — a citizen, a resident alien with a valid green card or substantial presence, or a domestic entity?"

Third, agents routinely miss the 20-day remittance deadline for Form 8288. The clock starts at closing, not at recording. If the closing agent is slow to prepare the form, penalties accrue against the buyer. Agents representing buyers in foreign-seller transactions should calendar this deadline independently and follow up with the closing agent by day 10.

Fourth, some agents confuse the FIRPTA withholding certificate (Form 8288-B) timeline with Oregon's Form OR-WC process. The IRS can take 90 days or longer to process a withholding certificate application. Oregon's process through the Department of Revenue is separate and typically faster, but still requires advance planning. Agents who learn about the seller's foreign status two weeks before closing have almost no chance of obtaining either certificate in time.

Fifth, agents sometimes assume that because a seller has a U.S. mailing address or U.S. bank account, they are not a foreign person. Neither of these factors determines FIRPTA status. Only citizenship, lawful permanent residency, or meeting the substantial presence test matters.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Brokers in Oregon carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.805 and OAR 863-015-0260 for the acts of their licensed agents.** A single FIRPTA failure in a brokerage can trigger IRS scrutiny, client lawsuits, and Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) complaints. Proactive brokers build FIRPTA checkpoints into their transaction management systems rather than relying on individual agent memory.

Every listing file should contain either a signed non-foreign affidavit (certifying the seller is a U.S. person) or documentation that FIRPTA withholding procedures have been initiated. Brokers should audit for this document at the same stage they verify [seller disclosure compliance](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) — before the transaction moves past the contingency period.

Brokers should also verify that their agents understand the difference between federal FIRPTA withholding and Oregon's ORS 314.258 nonresident withholding. A quarterly training refresher, even 15 minutes during a team meeting, reduces errors dramatically. The [Oregon Real Estate Agency](https://www.oregon.gov/rea) does not currently mandate FIRPTA-specific continuing education, but brokers who make it part of their internal compliance protocol protect themselves from vicarious liability claims.

Transaction files involving foreign sellers should be flagged for enhanced review. Brokers should confirm that the closing agent has received copies of Form 8288-B (if filed) and Oregon Form OR-WC (if applicable), and that the 20-day remittance timeline is being tracked. This level of oversight is exactly [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) when no one takes ownership of the compliance chain.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

If the required FIRPTA withholding is not remitted, the IRS can assess the full withholding amount against the buyer, plus interest and penalties under IRC Section 6651. The buyer then has a potential claim against the seller, the closing agent, and — depending on the facts — the real estate agents who failed to identify or communicate the foreign seller's status.

Oregon's Real Estate Agency can discipline agents under ORS 696.301 for conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness. A pattern of FIRPTA-related failures in a brokerage could constitute grounds for license suspension or revocation, particularly if clients suffer financial harm. Civil liability is equally serious: a buyer forced to pay $90,000+ in penalties because their agent never flagged a foreign seller's status has a straightforward negligence claim.

Deal cancellation is also common. When a title company discovers mid-transaction that a seller is foreign and no withholding plan is in place, they may refuse to close until the withholding is arranged. If the buyer's funds have already been allocated and the additional withholding amount pushes the transaction beyond what the buyer can absorb at closing, the deal falls apart. Agents on both sides lose their commission, and the seller faces potential penalties for failing to cooperate with U.S. tax obligations.

## Practical Steps for Agents Listing Properties Owned by Foreign Sellers

Start at the listing appointment. Ask the seller to complete a FIRPTA status questionnaire before you sign the listing agreement. If the seller is a foreign person, discuss the withholding implications immediately so there are no surprises later. Recommend that the seller engage a tax attorney or CPA specializing in international taxation — not as optional advice, but as a condition of effective representation.

If the seller wants to reduce withholding, file IRS Form 8288-B at least 90 days before the anticipated closing date. Coordinate with the seller's tax professional to prepare the application, which requires calculating the seller's maximum tax liability on the disposition. Simultaneously, determine whether Oregon Form OR-WC should be filed with the Department of Revenue to reduce or eliminate the state withholding.

Document everything in writing. Send the seller a written summary of their withholding obligations, cc the closing agent, and retain a copy in the transaction file. They require affirmative action at listing intake, coordination with tax professionals, advance planning for withholding certificates, and disciplined deadline tracking through closing and beyond. Agents who build these steps into their standard workflow — whether manually or through [automated compliance tracking](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) — protect their clients, their brokers, and their own licenses from avoidable liability.
