## Missouri Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Division That Trips Up Agents

The question of Missouri who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 seems straightforward until you sit at a closing table and watch a deal crater because someone assumed wrong. Missouri does not have a single statute mandating which party pays each fee. Instead, the allocation is governed by contract language, local custom, and the specific forms agents choose to fill in — or leave blank.

That reality puts enormous responsibility on agents and their brokers. A misplaced checkbox on the Missouri Association of Realtors (MAR) residential sale contract can shift thousands of dollars from one party to another, and failing to disclose or document that shift properly opens the door to commission complaints, E&O claims, and license discipline under Missouri Revised Statutes Chapter 339.

## How Missouri Law Frames Closing Cost Responsibility

Missouri is not a state that legislates closing cost allocation in a prescriptive way. Unlike states with mandatory transfer taxes assigned to a specific party by statute, Missouri leaves most fee assignments to the parties' agreement. The governing principle comes from contract law: whatever the executed purchase agreement says controls.

**The Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under RSMo Chapter 339, does not publish a rule dictating who pays title insurance, escrow fees, or recording charges.** MREC's regulatory role focuses on agent conduct — specifically whether agents accurately represent the terms and ensure informed consent. Section 339.100(2)(3) gives the Commission authority to discipline licensees who engage in "any act which constitutes dishonest dealing" or "misrepresentation," which includes telling a client the seller "always" pays certain costs when the contract says otherwise.

The practical implication: agents must build cost responsibility into the contract explicitly. Relying on unwritten custom without contract language is a liability trap, especially when the deal involves out-of-state parties unfamiliar with Missouri norms.

## The Forms That Control the Allocation

Missouri agents working with MAR forms need to know exactly where closing cost allocation lives in the paperwork. The primary document is **MAR Form 100 — Residential Sale Contract**, which contains Section 12 addressing title evidence and title insurance. This section includes blanks for specifying who orders title evidence, who pays the owner's title policy premium, and whether a lender's policy is at buyer's expense.

MAR Form 100 also includes Section 15 (Closing/Possession), which addresses the closing fee split. Agents who leave these sections blank or write "per custom" without specifying the custom are creating ambiguity that title companies flag and attorneys exploit.

For seller concessions toward buyer closing costs, agents typically write terms into Section 27 (Additional Provisions) or use MAR Form 102 — Counter-Proposal to modify cost allocation during negotiations. The dollar amount or percentage cap must be specific. Writing "seller to pay buyer's closing costs" without a cap is a recipe for a post-inspection dispute.

Agents should also know MAR Form 115 — Amendment to Contract, which is the proper vehicle for modifying cost responsibility after initial contract execution. Using informal emails or text messages to "agree" on changes without amending the contract creates enforcement gaps that [cause deals to break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the final days before closing.

## Seller Concessions: What the Numbers Actually Look Like

Seller concessions remain one of the most common mechanisms for handling buyer closing costs in Missouri. Conventional loans through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac cap seller concessions based on the buyer's down payment amount. FHA allows up to 6% of the sale price in seller concessions. VA loans also permit up to 4% in certain categories.

**Agents who write seller concessions exceeding lender caps do not just risk loan denial — they risk MREC scrutiny if the contract was structured to inflate the purchase price to accommodate the excess.** Price inflation to cover concessions can constitute misrepresentation to the lender, implicating both agents and potentially triggering federal fraud statutes.

The practical ceiling on seller concessions in a Missouri transaction depends on the loan program, the appraised value, and the specific costs being covered. Agents must verify with the buyer's lender before finalizing concession amounts in the contract.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make on Closing Costs

Three to five errors appear repeatedly in MREC complaint files and title company rejection notices. These are not theoretical — they generate real liability.

**First, agents tell clients that custom is law.** Stating that "the seller always pays for title insurance in Missouri" without qualifying it as negotiable custom misrepresents the legal framework. When a seller refuses and the buyer feels misled, the agent's verbal assurance becomes Exhibit A in a MREC complaint under RSMo 339.100.

Second, agents fail to specify which title company will handle closing. MAR Form 100 Section 12 asks who selects the title company, but agents routinely leave this ambiguous. The result: two title companies open files, duplicate work is billed, and neither party wants to pay the excess fees.

Third, agents write seller concession amounts that exceed what the loan program allows. The contract then requires an amendment mid-transaction, which delays closing, frustrates the seller, and can cause the deal to fall apart entirely if the seller refuses to renegotiate.

Fourth, agents forget that recording fees and documentary stamps (in applicable jurisdictions like St. Louis City or Jackson County) need to be allocated in the contract. These fees appear on the closing disclosure and surprise clients who were told their agent "handled everything."

Fifth, agents use outdated forms. MREC and MAR periodically update form language. Using a 2023 version of Form 100 in a 2026 transaction may omit updated disclosure requirements or contain superseded language that creates enforceability questions.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under RSMo 339.100 and MREC Rule 20 CSR 2250-8.060, which requires brokers to maintain supervision over their licensees' transaction activities. A broker who fails to catch closing cost errors in contracts reviewed under their license shares exposure.

| Audit Point | What Broker Should Check | Risk If Missed |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Form version currency | Confirm MAR Form 100 is current 2026 edition | Enforceability challenges, E&O exposure |
| Concession cap compliance | Verify concession percentage matches loan program limits | Loan denial, potential fraud implications |
| Section 12 completion | Ensure title cost allocation is filled in, not blank | Ambiguity leading to closing delays or disputes |
| Section 27 specificity | Review additional provisions for vague cost language | Contract disputes, MREC complaints |
| Amendment trail | Confirm any post-execution cost changes use Form 115 | Unenforceable oral modifications |

Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint before contracts go to the title company. This is where [checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) every single time — a standardized review protocol catches the concession cap violation that an agent rushing between showings will miss.

MREC has authority under RSMo 339.150 to investigate brokerages and impose fines up to $2,000 per violation, suspend licenses, or require additional education. The Commission's complaint process, detailed on the [Missouri Division of Professional Registration website](https://pr.mo.gov/realestate.asp), allows any party to a transaction — including the other agent — to file against a licensee who mishandled cost disclosures.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of misallocating closing costs extend beyond regulatory action. Civil liability is the larger financial threat. A buyer who pays $4,000 in costs they believed the seller was covering has a breach of fiduciary duty claim against their agent. A seller forced to pay costs not in the signed contract may sue for breach of contract and name the listing agent as a co-defendant for negligent preparation.

**E&O insurance carriers report that closing cost disputes rank among the top five claim categories for Missouri residential agents.** The defense costs alone — even when the agent prevails — average $15,000 to $25,000 according to industry carrier data. Settlement amounts for clear misrepresentation cases run significantly higher.

Deal cancellation is the immediate consequence. Missouri contracts typically include provisions allowing termination for material misrepresentation. If a buyer discovers at the closing table that costs were allocated differently than their agent represented, they may refuse to close, and the seller's recourse against the buyer may be limited if the buyer's agent caused the confusion.
