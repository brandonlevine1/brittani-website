## Why New Hampshire HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention

The New Hampshire hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 carry real teeth for agents who assume condominium transactions work the same as single-family sales. Roughly 18 percent of all residential closings in the state involve a unit governed by either a condominium association under RSA 356-B or a homeowners' association organized under RSA 292 or RSA 356-C. Every one of those transactions imposes disclosure obligations that fall squarely on the listing agent's checklist—and sometimes on the buyer's agent too.

Getting it wrong doesn't just create awkward phone calls. It can unwind a deal days before closing or expose a brokerage to claims under the New Hampshire Consumer Protection Act (RSA 358-A). The statutes are not suggestions; they are enforceable mandates backed by the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC).

## The Statutory Framework: RSA 356-B and RSA 356-C

**New Hampshire's Condominium Act, RSA 356-B, is the primary authority governing condo resale disclosures in the state.** Section 46 of that statute mandates that sellers of condominium units furnish buyers with a resale certificate prepared by the association. The certificate must include the declaration, bylaws, rules, current budget, most recent audited financial statement, reserve fund status, pending special assessments, insurance coverage, and any pending litigation involving the association.

RSA 356-C applies to planned communities and non-condominium common interest developments. Its disclosure requirements mirror RSA 356-B in many respects but are less frequently triggered because fewer New Hampshire developments fall under this newer statute. Agents should verify which statute governs a property before ordering documents.

For properties governed by voluntary HOAs formed under RSA 292 (the state's nonprofit corporation statute), no statutory resale certificate obligation exists. That does not eliminate risk—buyers still expect the information, and [failure to disclose known material facts](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) can trigger liability under agency law and RSA 358-A.

## The Resale Certificate: What It Must Contain

The resale certificate under RSA 356-B:46 is not optional filler. The statute lists specific items, and the association is required to provide all of them. Here is how the key components break down compared to what agents often confuse with a standard seller disclosure:

| Document Element | Resale Certificate (RSA 356-B:46) | Seller's Property Disclosure (NHREC Form) |
|---|---|---|
| Current budget & financials | Required | Not included |
| Pending special assessments | Required | Seller marks "yes/no" |
| Reserve fund balance | Required | Not included |
| Pending litigation | Required | Seller marks "yes/no" |
| Physical condition of unit | Not included | Required |
| Insurance coverage summary | Required | Not included |
| Governing documents (declaration, bylaws, rules) | Required | Not included |

These two documents serve different purposes, and one never substitutes for the other. Agents regularly conflate them, which is exactly the kind of mistake that triggers cancellation rights.

## Timing, Delivery, and the Buyer's Cancellation Right

**Once the buyer receives the resale certificate, a 5-calendar-day cancellation window opens.** If the buyer does not receive the certificate before executing the purchase and sale agreement, the clock starts upon actual receipt. The contract remains voidable during that window regardless of what the purchase and sale agreement says about contingency deadlines.

The association has 10 business days from the date of the seller's written request to produce the resale certificate. If the association is late, the buyer's cancellation right simply extends. Agents cannot compress this timeline through contract language because the statute supersedes private agreements.

| Timeline Step | Responsible Party | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Request resale certificate from association | Seller (or listing agent on behalf of seller) | Immediately upon listing or upon acceptance |
| Association produces certificate | HOA/Condo board | 10 business days from written request |
| Deliver certificate to buyer | Seller or listing agent | Before or at contract execution (preferred) |
| Buyer cancellation window | Buyer | 5 calendar days from receipt |
| Refund of deposits upon cancellation | Escrow holder | Prompt return, no penalty to buyer |

Most experienced New Hampshire agents request the resale certificate the moment the listing goes active. Waiting until after an offer is accepted invites a scheduling collision between the 10-day production period and your buyer's mortgage lock expiration.

## NHREC Forms Agents Must Know

The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission publishes standardized forms that intersect with HOA and condo transactions. While NHREC does not publish a standalone HOA disclosure form the way some states do, several commission-mandated forms require HOA-related disclosures:

The **NHREC Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Form (RSA 477:4-c)** requires the seller to indicate whether the property is subject to an HOA, a condominium association, or a planned community regime. Sellers must disclose known assessments, pending litigation, and restrictions. This form does not replace the RSA 356-B:46 resale certificate; it supplements it.

The **NHREC Agency Disclosure Form** (required under RSA 331-A:25-d) must be presented at first substantive contact. While not HOA-specific, agents frequently forget to re-present it when a second agent from the same brokerage enters the transaction on the condo association side—particularly in designated agency scenarios.

The **New Hampshire Association of Realtors (NHAR) Purchase and Sale Agreement** (Form 200 series) contains a condominium addendum clause referencing the buyer's right to receive the resale certificate and the statutory cancellation period. Agents who use non-NHAR forms must ensure equivalent language appears in the contract, or risk the buyer discovering the cancellation right post-closing and claiming they were never informed.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA and Condo Disclosures

**Mistake one: Treating the seller disclosure as a substitute for the resale certificate.** The NHREC property condition disclosure form captures the seller's knowledge of the unit's physical condition. It does not contain association financials, reserve fund data, or pending litigation detail. Both documents must be delivered independently.

Mistake two: Failing to request the resale certificate early enough. Agents who wait until mutual acceptance to request the certificate from the association create a 10-business-day gap that stacks on top of the 5-day buyer cancellation window. In a fast-moving market, this can blow past the financing contingency deadline and spook lenders.

Mistake three: Not confirming which statutory regime applies to the property. A townhouse community may be organized as a condominium under RSA 356-B, a planned community under RSA 356-C, or a voluntary nonprofit HOA under RSA 292. The disclosure obligations differ significantly. Agents who assume "townhouse equals condo" risk delivering wrong or incomplete documents.

Mistake four: Relying on the association's verbal assurance that "nothing is pending." RSA 356-B:46 requires written disclosure. A phone call from the property manager saying "the reserve fund looks fine" does not satisfy the statute and will not protect you in a post-closing dispute.

Mistake five: Forgetting to document delivery and receipt dates. Without a signed acknowledgment showing the date the buyer received the resale certificate, proving that the 5-day cancellation window expired becomes a [deadline-tracking nightmare](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) if the deal later goes sideways.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance creates three layers of exposure. First, the buyer retains the right to cancel the contract until 5 days after they finally receive the resale certificate—even if that happens at the closing table. Second, the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission can initiate disciplinary proceedings under RSA 331-A:28 for failure to protect a client's interests, inadequate disclosure, or negligence. Sanctions range from reprimands to license suspension and fines up to $3,000 per violation.

Third, buyers who close without receiving proper disclosures may pursue damages under RSA 358-A (the Consumer Protection Act), which allows treble damages and attorney's fees. Courts have held that real estate agents can be individually liable under 358-A when they participated in or facilitated the non-disclosure. The [New Hampshire Real Estate Commission's administrative rules](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) (REA 600 series) spell out the standard of care expected.

Brokerages also face vicarious liability. If an agent's failure to deliver the resale certificate results in a lawsuit, the supervising broker's errors-and-omissions policy will likely be implicated, and NHREC may investigate the brokerage's supervisory practices.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers carry independent responsibility under RSA 331-A:16 to ensure agents in their office comply with disclosure laws. A reactive posture—waiting for a complaint—is not a defense. Here is what a sound brokerage compliance audit should include.

**Verify that every condo listing file contains a dated copy of the resale certificate request sent to the association.** If the association has not responded within 10 business days, the file should contain follow-up documentation. Brokers who review files only at closing miss the window to correct deficiencies.

Audit buyer-side files for a signed receipt acknowledging the date the resale certificate was delivered. This receipt should be a standalone document or a clearly dated entry in the transaction management system—not buried in an email thread the buyer may deny receiving.

Confirm that agents are correctly identifying the governing statute. A quarterly training session covering the distinctions between RSA 356-B, RSA 356-C, and RSA 292 properties takes less than an hour and pays for itself the first time it prevents a compliance violation.

Finally, review your brokerage's [transaction coordination workflow](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) to ensure that HOA document requests are triggered automatically upon listing activation or offer acceptance. Manual reminder systems fail at scale—especially during high-volume summer months in lake-region markets.
