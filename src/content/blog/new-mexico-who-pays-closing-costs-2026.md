The question of **New Mexico who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026** is not answered by custom alone. It is answered by the purchase agreement your client signs, the statutory requirements that attach to certain fees, and the negotiation you facilitate before a deal reaches the title company. Agents who treat closing cost allocation as an afterthought expose themselves to disputes, delayed closings, and regulatory complaints with the New Mexico Real Estate Commission (NMREC).

This post breaks down who typically pays what, the forms that govern the allocation, and the compliance traps agents fall into when they get lazy about line items.

## The Default Split: What Custom Dictates vs. What the Contract Controls

New Mexico has no statute that mandates a fixed buyer-seller split on most closing costs. Unlike states with codified transfer taxes assigned to one party, New Mexico eliminated its real estate transfer tax decades ago. That means almost every cost allocation is a matter of negotiation, written into the purchase agreement.

**Custom in Albuquerque, Las Cruces, and Santa Fe markets generally follows a pattern, but custom is not law.** If the contract is silent on a fee, the title company will call you — and the deal will stall until both parties agree in writing. The New Mexico Association of Realtors (NMAR) [Purchase Agreement Form 2103](https://www.nmrec.nm.gov/) includes specific blanks for assigning costs, and leaving them empty is a compliance risk.

## Statutory Obligations That Override Negotiation

While most costs are negotiable, a few obligations are fixed by New Mexico law. Under NMSA 1978, Section 47-13-4, the seller must provide a marketable title. The cost of curing title defects — satisfying liens, obtaining releases, resolving boundary disputes — falls on the seller regardless of what the purchase agreement says about "closing costs."

Property tax prorations follow NMSA 1978, Section 7-38-49, which establishes that property taxes attach to the land on January 1 of each year. The proration at closing splits the annual tax obligation based on the closing date. Neither party can contract away the underlying tax liability to the county; they can only agree on how to reimburse each other at settlement.

**Recording fees are set by county clerk offices and are not negotiable in amount**, only in who pays them. The fee schedule is established under NMSA 1978, Section 14-8-5. In 2026, most New Mexico counties charge between $25 and $50 for the first page and $10 for each additional page.

## Forms That Govern Closing Cost Allocation

Agents working within NMAR use specific forms that contain the fields where closing cost responsibilities are documented. Getting these wrong — or leaving them incomplete — is where deals break down.

NMAR Form 2103 (Residential Purchase Agreement) contains Section 10, which addresses closing costs, title insurance, surveys, and escrow fees. Each line requires the agent to designate buyer, seller, or split. NMAR Form 2104 (Counter Offer) is used when the original allocation is rejected and a new split is proposed.

NMAR Form 2126 (Seller's Disclosure Statement) does not address closing costs directly, but if the seller knows of HOA transfer fees or special assessments that will appear at closing, failure to disclose those under NMSA 1978, Section 47-13-2 can trigger a post-closing claim. Agents should cross-reference the disclosure with the cost allocation section to ensure nothing is missed.

For deals involving [deadline-sensitive contingencies](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the closing cost allocation must be finalized before the financing contingency period expires. Lenders need the Loan Estimate to match the contract terms, and any mismatch between what the purchase agreement says and what the Closing Disclosure reflects will delay funding.

## Post-NAR Settlement: Commission and Concessions in 2026

The 2024 NAR settlement reshaped how buyer-agent compensation flows through a transaction, and by 2026, New Mexico agents must treat commission as a distinct cost — not lumped into "seller closing costs" by default. Buyer-agent compensation is no longer offered through MLS, and any seller contribution toward it must be explicitly negotiated and documented.

**NMAR released updated addenda in late 2025 specifically addressing buyer-broker compensation agreements.** Agents must use NMAR Form 2140 (Buyer Representation Agreement) to establish compensation terms with their client before showing property. If the seller agrees to contribute toward buyer-agent fees, that contribution is written into the purchase agreement as a separate line item — distinct from seller concessions toward the buyer's closing costs.

This distinction matters for compliance. TRID rules require that seller concessions and seller-paid commissions appear in different sections of the Closing Disclosure. Conflating the two can trigger a loan-level compliance failure. Agents who instruct the title company to "just put it all under seller concessions" risk a funding delay or a lender kicking back the file.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

**First: leaving cost allocation fields blank in NMAR Form 2103.** This happens more often than brokers want to admit. When an agent submits a purchase agreement with empty fields in Section 10, the title company cannot prepare a preliminary settlement statement. The resulting back-and-forth wastes days and can push a deal past its closing deadline.

Second: assuming the seller always pays owner's title insurance. While this is the dominant custom in Bernalillo and Santa Fe counties, it is not universal in rural New Mexico. In Lea, Eddy, and Chaves counties, some transactions historically place this cost on the buyer. An out-of-area agent who writes the contract based on Albuquerque norms without confirming local practice may create a point of contention at closing that undermines client trust.

Third: failing to account for well and septic inspection costs in rural transactions. NMAR Form 2103 includes a section on inspections, but agents sometimes overlook that the New Mexico Environment Department requires septic system inspections for property transfer in certain jurisdictions. The cost — typically $300 to $500 — must be assigned to one party, and if it is not, the deal stalls at the point when the inspection is ordered but no one authorized payment.

Fourth: miscalculating property tax prorations by using the prior year's tax bill without verifying whether a reassessment occurred. The Bernalillo County Assessor's Office revalues properties annually, and agents who prorate based on stale numbers create a discrepancy that surfaces on the final settlement statement.

Fifth: confusing seller concessions (contributions toward buyer's closing costs) with price reductions. A $5,000 seller concession keeps the purchase price intact and allows the buyer to finance their closing costs. A $5,000 price reduction lowers the loan amount but does not help the buyer cover out-of-pocket settlement charges. Agents who conflate these two mechanisms cost their buyers real money.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Qualifying brokers under NMAC 16.61.19 carry supervisory responsibility for every transaction their agents close. When it comes to closing cost allocation, brokers should audit three things on every file before the closing package goes to the title company.

**First, verify that every cost allocation field in NMAR Form 2103 is completed.** A blank field is an unsigned promise — it creates ambiguity that can ripen into a complaint with the NMREC. Brokers who catch this during file review save themselves from post-closing disputes.

Second, confirm that any seller concession toward buyer closing costs does not exceed the lender's maximum allowable contribution. Conventional loans typically cap seller concessions at 3% for buyers putting less than 10% down. FHA caps at 6%. If the purchase agreement includes a concession that exceeds the lender's threshold, the contract must be amended — and the broker should catch this before the appraisal is ordered.

Third, ensure the buyer-broker compensation agreement (NMAR Form 2140) is on file and that any seller contribution toward buyer-agent compensation matches what appears in the purchase agreement. Mismatches between these documents create audit exposure for the brokerage under NMREC Rule 16.61.19.8, which requires brokers to maintain complete and accurate transaction records. Failure to comply can result in fines up to $1,000 per violation and potential license suspension.

For teams managing [high transaction volumes](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator), a systematic checklist that flags incomplete cost allocation fields before contracts go to escrow is not optional — it is a risk management requirement.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

The NMREC has authority under the Real Estate License Act (NMSA 1978, Sections 61-29-1 through 61-29-32) to discipline agents who submit incomplete contracts, misrepresent cost obligations, or fail to supervise the closing process. Penalties range from a letter of reprimand to license revocation, depending on the severity and pattern of conduct.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability when a buyer or seller suffers financial harm due to a poorly documented cost allocation. If a buyer arrives at closing expecting the seller to pay $4,000 in title insurance fees and discovers the contract is silent on the issue, the agent who drafted that contract is the first target of a demand letter. Errors and omissions insurance may cover the claim, but it will not cover the reputational damage or the spike in premiums that follows.

**Agents who treat [closing paperwork as an afterthought](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) are writing checks their E&O policy will eventually have to cash.** The better approach is to build a closing cost allocation review into every transaction workflow — ideally before the contract is even countersigned.
