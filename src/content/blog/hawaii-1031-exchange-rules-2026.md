## Hawaii 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: The Compliance Landscape Agents Cannot Ignore

Real estate agents working exchange transactions in Hawaii face a dual layer of tax compliance that mainland agents never encounter. Understanding Hawaii 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is not optional—it is the difference between a smooth closing and one that collapses under withholding disputes, delayed refunds, or outright penalties from the Hawaii Department of Taxation (DOTAX). The state's unique HARPTA withholding regime, which operates independently from the federal FIRPTA framework, creates traps for agents who assume a valid 1031 exchange automatically eliminates all tax obligations at closing.

Hawaii conforms to IRC Section 1031 under Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Section 235-7(a)(3), meaning a properly structured like-kind exchange defers state income tax on capital gains just as it does federally. But conformity does not mean simplicity. The withholding mechanics, the forms required, and the timing pressures are distinctly Hawaiian—and distinctly punishing when ignored.

## How HARPTA Interacts with 1031 Exchanges

**The Hawaii Real Property Tax Act (HARPTA), codified under HRS Sections 235-68 through 235-71, requires buyers to withhold 7.25% of the gross sales price when acquiring property from certain sellers.** This withholding applies regardless of whether the seller intends to complete a 1031 exchange. The statute does not contain an automatic exemption for exchange transactions.

Sellers planning a 1031 exchange have two paths to avoid or reduce withholding. The first is obtaining a withholding certificate (Form N-289) from DOTAX before closing, which either eliminates or reduces the required withholding. The second is proceeding with full withholding at closing and then filing Form N-289C after the exchange completes to claim a refund of the withheld amount.

The practical problem for agents is timing. Form N-289 requires DOTAX approval, which can take 60 to 90 days. If your seller is also working within the 45-day identification and 180-day completion windows mandated by IRC Section 1031, these overlapping deadlines create serious coordination challenges that [frequently cause agents to miss critical dates](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Key Forms and Filing Requirements for Hawaii 1031 Exchanges

Agents must be familiar with the specific forms that govern withholding and exchange reporting at the state level. The table below summarizes the primary documents involved.

| Form | Purpose | Filed By | Filed With | Timing |
|------|---------|----------|------------|--------|
| Form N-289 | Request reduced/eliminated HARPTA withholding | Seller (or representative) | DOTAX | Before closing (allow 60-90 days) |
| Form N-289A | Buyer's statement of withholding | Buyer or closing agent | DOTAX | Within 20 days after closing |
| Form N-289B | Seller notification of withholding | Buyer | Seller | At or before closing |
| Form N-289C | Refund claim for excess withholding | Seller | DOTAX | After closing (exchange completion) |
| Form N-11 or N-15 | Hawaii income tax return reporting exchange | Seller | DOTAX | Annual filing deadline |

Additionally, sellers must report the federal exchange on IRS Form 8824 and attach corresponding documentation to their Hawaii return. The state does not have its own exchange-specific reporting form beyond the income tax return, but DOTAX may request supporting documentation including the qualified intermediary agreement, exchange settlement statements, and identification notices.

**If Form N-289A is not filed within 20 days of closing, the buyer faces penalties under HRS Section 235-68(d).** The penalty equals the amount that should have been withheld, plus interest. This liability attaches to the buyer personally—not the escrow company, not the agent.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Hawaii 1031 Exchanges

Experienced agents still stumble on exchange-related compliance in Hawaii. These errors are not theoretical—they show up in deal files reviewed by brokers and in DOTAX enforcement actions.

**First, agents assume that a 1031 exchange automatically exempts the transaction from HARPTA withholding.** It does not. The exchange defers the tax liability, but the withholding mechanism is separate. Without a Form N-289 approval or an applicable exemption (such as the seller being a Hawaii resident selling their principal residence under HRS Section 235-68(b)), the 7.25% must still be withheld at closing.

Second, agents fail to coordinate the N-289 application timeline with the exchange timeline. A seller who enters into a purchase contract for a replacement property before receiving their N-289 approval may face a cash flow crisis at closing. The 7.25% withheld from the relinquished property sale can disrupt the exchange by reducing the funds available for reinvestment, potentially triggering partial boot recognition.

Third, agents conflate HARPTA with FIRPTA. Foreign sellers face both a 15% federal FIRPTA withholding and a 7.25% Hawaii HARPTA withholding—a combined 22.25% of gross sales price locked up at closing. Agents who only address one withholding regime leave their clients exposed to the other. The federal exemption process (IRS Form 8288-B) and the state process (Form N-289) are entirely separate filings with different agencies.

Fourth, listing agents neglect to advise sellers about the N-289 application early enough in the listing period. The application should be submitted the moment a seller decides to pursue an exchange—not after an offer arrives. Waiting until contract execution often means the approval will not arrive before closing.

Fifth, agents fail to document their advisory role. When a deal goes sideways because of withholding issues, the agent who verbally mentioned HARPTA but never documented the conversation faces liability exposure. Written disclosure of withholding obligations, ideally through a separate HARPTA advisory form signed by the client, protects the agent and demonstrates compliance with their [fiduciary duties around deal coordination](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under HRS Chapter 467 and Hawaii Administrative Rules Title 16, Chapter 99. When agents handle exchange transactions without proper compliance, the brokerage's license is at risk.

**Brokers should audit every transaction file involving a nonresident seller or a seller who has identified 1031 exchange intent for the following items:** a signed HARPTA disclosure or advisory form, evidence that Form N-289 was either submitted or that full withholding was collected, a copy of the qualified intermediary engagement letter, and confirmation that Form N-289A was filed within the 20-day statutory window.

| Audit Item | Red Flag If Missing | Potential Consequence |
|------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Signed HARPTA advisory | No evidence seller was informed | Agent liability, E&O claim |
| Form N-289 application or withholding confirmation | Seller may face unexpected withholding | Deal cancellation, client complaint |
| QI engagement letter in file | Exchange may not be properly structured | Tax deferral failure, malpractice risk |
| Form N-289A filing confirmation | Buyer penalty exposure | Buyer lawsuit against agent/brokerage |
| Exchange timeline documentation | Missed 45/180-day deadlines | Partial or full tax recognition |

Brokers should also verify that agents are not providing tax advice beyond their scope. The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC) has repeatedly emphasized that agents must refer clients to qualified tax professionals for exchange structuring. An agent who tells a seller "don't worry, the 1031 handles everything" without a tax advisor referral is operating outside their license.

Quarterly file reviews should flag any transaction involving out-of-state sellers, entity sellers (LLCs, trusts), or properties held less than two years—all of which carry elevated HARPTA and exchange compliance risk.

## Hawaii's Conformity to Federal 1031 Rules and Where It Diverges

Hawaii generally conforms to the federal Internal Revenue Code as of a specific date, updated periodically by the state legislature. For 2026, Hawaii's conformity means that the restrictions imposed by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017—limiting 1031 exchanges to real property only—apply at the state level as well. Personal property exchanges (artwork, equipment, vehicles) do not qualify for state tax deferral.

One area of divergence that trips up agents involves the treatment of boot. Under federal rules, any cash or non-like-kind property received in an exchange triggers gain recognition. Hawaii follows this treatment, but the state tax rate applied to recognized gain can reach 11% for high-income taxpayers under HRS Section 235-51. This means that even partial boot recognition in Hawaii carries a heavier state tax cost than in most other jurisdictions.

Agents should also be aware that Hawaii does not offer a state-level installment sale election for boot recognized in an exchange. The full state tax on recognized gain is due in the year of the exchange, not spread over future payment periods. This catches sellers off guard when they receive even modest amounts of boot and face an immediate Hawaii tax bill alongside the HARPTA refund process.

## Liability and Consequences When Compliance Fails

The consequences for noncompliance fall on multiple parties. Under HRS Section 235-68(d), a buyer who fails to withhold the required 7.25% becomes personally liable for that amount plus interest at 8% per annum. The buyer's agent, while not statutorily named as the withholding party, may face civil liability if they actively advised the buyer that withholding was unnecessary.

HIREC can discipline agents under HRS Section 467-14 for conduct that demonstrates incompetence, including failure to understand and apply HARPTA requirements in transactions where they clearly apply. Disciplinary actions range from fines to license suspension. In 2024, HIREC issued guidance reminding licensees that HARPTA awareness is considered part of minimum competence for agents handling investment property sales.

**Escrow companies will typically refuse to close a transaction if HARPTA compliance is unclear.** This means that an agent's failure to address withholding early does not just create post-closing tax problems—it kills deals before they close. The escrow officer's refusal to disburse funds without proper withholding documentation is the most common way these compliance gaps surface.

For brokerages, E&O carriers increasingly scrutinize 1031 exchange involvement. A pattern of files lacking HARPTA documentation or exchange timeline tracking can result in premium increases or coverage exclusions. Brokers who implement [systematic checklist-based compliance processes](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) protect both their agents and their insurability.
