## Understanding Oregon Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Oregon stands apart from most states in its approach to transfer taxes. There is no statewide real estate transfer tax, which means agents must track a patchwork of local ordinances that change on their own schedules. Familiarity with Oregon transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 is not optional—it directly affects your closing disclosures, your client's bottom line, and your compliance exposure.

The state's recording fees, governed by ORS Chapter 205, apply uniformly across all 36 counties. Transfer taxes, by contrast, are authorized under ORS 306.815 and imposed only by specific local jurisdictions. Agents who conflate these two categories—or assume a single statewide rate—invite errors that compound quickly at the closing table.

This post breaks down the exact fee schedules, identifies the forms you should be using, and flags the compliance pitfalls that catch even experienced licensees off guard.

## Recording Fee Structure Under ORS 205.320

**Oregon's recording fees are set by statute and apply to every county clerk's office in the state.** ORS 205.320 establishes the fee schedule for documents submitted for recording, and the 2026 rates remain consistent with the most recent statutory amendments. The base fee for the first page of any recorded document—deeds, deeds of trust, reconveyances, and assignments—is $111. Each additional page costs $5.

Non-standard documents, such as those with oversized pages or unusual formatting, may incur additional charges at the clerk's discretion. Plat maps and surveys have a separate fee structure under ORS 205.350, starting at $500 for the first sheet.

Agents routinely estimate recording fees on buyer net sheets and seller net sheets using the OREF (Oregon Real Estate Forms) Estimated Net to Seller worksheet. If you are using OREF Form 024 (Seller's Estimated Net Proceeds), you must input the correct recording fee, not a generic "approximately $200" figure. The difference between a two-page deed and a ten-page deed of trust with exhibits is real money.

| Document Type | First Page Fee | Additional Pages | Statutory Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warranty Deed | $111 | $5/page | ORS 205.320(1) |
| Deed of Trust | $111 | $5/page | ORS 205.320(1) |
| Reconveyance | $111 | $5/page | ORS 205.320(1) |
| Lien Release | $111 | $5/page | ORS 205.320(1) |
| Plat (first sheet) | $500 | $250/sheet | ORS 205.350 |

County clerks also collect a $20 additional fee per document for the County Clerk Records Fund under ORS 205.323, which is sometimes missed on preliminary estimates. That $20 applies on top of the base $111, bringing a single-page deed's actual cost to $131.

## Local Transfer Taxes: Portland, Washington County, and Beyond

**Oregon's transfer tax exposure is concentrated in a handful of jurisdictions, and agents working metro-area deals must know the exact rates.** The most significant transfer tax in the state belongs to Washington County, which imposes a transfer tax of $1 per $1,000 of consideration under Washington County Code Chapter 3.08. For a $600,000 home, that is $600 owed at closing.

The City of Portland adopted its own transfer tax structure effective 2025, codified under Portland City Code 7.02. Portland's tiered rate schedule applies to properties selling above $500,000:

| Sale Price Tier | Rate | Example Tax on $800,000 Sale |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $500,000 | 0% | $0 |
| $500,001 – $2,000,000 | 1.0% | $3,000 (on $300K above threshold) |
| $2,000,001 – $5,000,000 | 1.25% | N/A |
| Over $5,000,000 | 1.5% | N/A |

These rates are subject to annual council review, and agents should verify the current schedule through the [City of Portland Revenue Division](https://www.portland.gov/revenue) before preparing net sheets for listings in city limits.

Under ORS 306.815, local governments that adopted a transfer tax before 1985 were grandfathered in. Washington County is the primary example. The Portland tax was enabled through a separate voter-approved measure. No other Oregon city currently imposes a general real property transfer tax, though several have explored it. Agents working in Lane County, Jackson County, or Deschutes County do not face a local transfer tax—yet.

## Forms and Disclosures Agents Must Use

Oregon real estate transactions rely heavily on OREF forms, which are maintained by Oregon Real Estate Forms LLC and updated regularly. For transfer tax and recording fee disclosures, the key forms include:

OREF Form 024 (Seller's Estimated Net Proceeds) is where agents document the seller's expected transfer tax liability. OREF Form 003 (Buyer's Estimated Costs) should reflect anticipated recording fees for the deed and deed of trust on the buyer's side. Both forms are worksheets, not binding contracts, but their accuracy creates or destroys client trust.

When working in Washington County, agents should also confirm that the title company or escrow officer has accounted for the county transfer tax on the settlement statement. This responsibility is shared—agents cannot simply assume the closer will catch it. If a listing agent publishes a seller net sheet omitting the Washington County transfer tax on a $750,000 listing, the seller is surprised by $750 at closing. That surprise erodes the relationship and sometimes the deal.

For [understanding where breakdowns happen in deals](/blog/where-deals-break-down), transfer tax surprises rank among the top causes of last-minute seller resistance, particularly with first-time sellers who have never encountered the fee.

## What Happens When Agents Get This Wrong

**Failure to accurately disclose estimated transfer taxes and recording fees exposes agents to multiple layers of liability.** At the regulatory level, the Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA)—the state's licensing body—treats material misrepresentation of transaction costs as a potential violation of ORS 696.301, which governs grounds for disciplinary action against licensees. Penalties range from formal reprimands to license suspension.

At the civil level, a seller who receives significantly less at closing than an agent's net sheet projected may pursue a claim for negligent misrepresentation. Oregon courts have recognized that real estate agents owe a duty of reasonable care in preparing financial estimates, even when those estimates are labeled "approximate."

> ORS 696.301(1): "The commissioner may suspend, revoke, or refuse to issue or renew a license... if the licensee has been guilty of fraud, misrepresentation, or dishonest dealing."

Beyond regulatory and civil risk, [agents who miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on fee-related corrections can trigger TRID (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure) tolerance violations when lender closing disclosures do not match actual costs. Recording fees fall into the zero-tolerance category under TRID—meaning any overcharge, even by one dollar, requires a lender cure. Agents who contribute to these errors lose referral relationships with loan officers.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Oregon Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

The first recurring error is assuming Oregon has no transfer tax at all. Agents who earned their license outside the Portland metro area—or who moved from a state with a flat statewide transfer tax—often tell sellers there is "no transfer tax in Oregon." That statement is false in Washington County and Portland, and making it in writing can create liability.

The second mistake is using outdated OREF form versions. OREF updates its forms on a periodic basis, and the estimated cost worksheets reflect fee changes. Agents who rely on saved PDF copies from prior years—rather than pulling current forms from their MLS or forms provider—risk quoting 2024 recording fees instead of 2026 figures. The County Clerk Records Fund fee under ORS 205.323 was not always $20; using old templates introduces inaccuracy.

The third error is miscalculating Portland's tiered transfer tax. Portland's structure taxes only the portion of the sale price exceeding $500,000, not the entire sale price. Agents who multiply the full sale price by 1% dramatically overstate the seller's obligation. On a $700,000 sale, the correct tax is $2,000 (1% of $200,000), not $7,000.

The fourth mistake is failing to identify which party bears the transfer tax in negotiation. ORS 306.815 states that the tax is the responsibility of the seller unless the parties agree otherwise. Agents who draft addenda shifting transfer tax to the buyer without clear contractual language—or who verbally promise "the buyer will cover it" without documenting the agreement—create disputes that can delay or kill closings.

The fifth error is ignoring exempt transactions. Transfers between family members, transfers to trusts for estate planning purposes, and certain government acquisitions may be exempt from local transfer taxes. Agents who automatically include transfer tax on every net sheet—without checking exemption criteria—overstate costs for clients who qualify. Washington County Code 3.08.050 lists specific exemptions that agents should review with the title officer.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers have supervisory obligations under ORS 696.022 that extend to transaction cost disclosures. A principal broker who allows agents to distribute inaccurate net sheets without review is not insulated from liability simply because the agent acted independently.

**At minimum, brokers should audit a random sample of estimated cost worksheets quarterly to verify that recording fees and transfer taxes reflect current rates.** This is particularly important for offices with agents working across jurisdictional lines—an agent listing in both Clackamas County (no local transfer tax) and Washington County (transfer tax applies) must switch mental models between transactions.

Brokers should also maintain a current fee reference sheet, updated at least annually, that all agents in the office can access. This reference should list recording fees under ORS 205.320, the County Clerk Records Fund surcharge under ORS 205.323, Washington County transfer tax rates, and Portland's tiered transfer tax brackets. Distributing this as a pinned resource in the brokerage's transaction management system—or through whatever [checklist system beats individual memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory)—prevents the most common errors before they reach clients.

Finally, brokers must verify that agents are using current-year OREF forms. A simple policy requiring agents to pull forms from the authorized digital provider at the time of each transaction—rather than reusing downloaded copies—eliminates version-control problems. Training on Portland's tiered calculation should be part of annual office compliance meetings, given the frequency of errors.
