# Utah FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: What Every Agent Must Know Before Closing

Failing to identify a foreign seller early in the listing process is one of the fastest ways to blow up a deal that is otherwise ready to close. Understanding the **Utah firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026** is not optional knowledge for agents working in markets like Park City, St. George, or the Salt Lake Valley, where international buyers and sellers are increasingly common. The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act imposes a mandatory federal withholding of 15% on the gross sales price, and Utah layers its own 6.0% state income tax withholding on top of that.

This post covers exactly what agents and brokers in Utah need to know—specific forms, deadlines, liability exposure, and the audit steps that keep your brokerage out of trouble.

## How FIRPTA Works at the Federal Level

FIRPTA, codified under IRC Section 1445, requires the buyer (transferee) to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when purchasing real property from a "foreign person." A foreign person includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates. The withholding is not a tax in itself—it is a prepayment against the seller's actual U.S. tax liability on the gain.

The withheld amount must be remitted to the IRS within 20 days of closing using [IRS Form 8288](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8288) (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and IRS Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons). The buyer receives a copy of 8288-A stamped by the IRS, and the seller uses it to file their U.S. income tax return claiming credit for the withholding.

Reduced rates exist in limited circumstances. If the sales price is $300,000 or less and the buyer signs an affidavit stating they will use the property as a residence for at least 50% of the first two years, the withholding drops to zero. For transactions between $300,001 and $1,000,000 with the same buyer-residence requirement, the rate is 10%.

## Utah's Additional State Withholding Obligation

**Utah imposes its own withholding requirement under Utah Code Section 59-10-1403.** When a nonresident (including a foreign person) sells real property located in Utah, the transferee or withholding agent must withhold 6.0% of the amount required to be reported on the seller's Utah return—effectively 6.0% of the gain, though in practice many title companies withhold on the gross proceeds to avoid calculating gain at the closing table.

The form agents and title companies must know is **Utah Form TC-675R** (Return of Income Tax Withholding for Nonresidents). This form is filed with the Utah State Tax Commission within 30 days of the date of the transfer. The seller can apply for a reduced withholding or a waiver by filing **Form TC-675W** (Application for Withholding Certificate for Nonresident Sellers of Real Property) before closing, but the application takes time—sometimes four to six weeks.

Here is how the federal and state obligations compare side by side:

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | Utah State |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding rate | 15% of gross sales price | 6.0% of gain (often applied to gross) |
| Applicable statute | IRC Section 1445 | Utah Code 59-10-1403 |
| Remittance form | IRS Form 8288 + 8288-A | Utah Form TC-675R |
| Filing deadline | 20 days after closing | 30 days after transfer |
| Reduced rate application | IRS Form 8288-B | Utah Form TC-675W |
| Primary liable party | Buyer (transferee) | Transferee or withholding agent |

Agents should note that both obligations apply simultaneously. A foreign seller in Utah can face a combined initial withholding of up to 21% of the sales price before any exemptions or reductions are applied.

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

**The liability chain under FIRPTA starts with the buyer, but it does not end there.** If the full 15% is not withheld, the buyer is personally liable for the withholding amount, plus interest and penalties under IRC Section 6651. This means your buyer client could owe the IRS hundreds of thousands of dollars on a transaction they thought was clean.

For the listing agent, liability typically comes through negligence claims rather than direct statutory penalties. If you represented the seller and failed to disclose—or failed to investigate—the seller's foreign status, the buyer's agent or the buyer themselves may bring a claim against you for negligent misrepresentation. The Utah Division of Real Estate can also open a disciplinary action if your conduct violated Utah Admin. Code R162-2f-401a, which governs standards of practice and honesty in dealing.

Title companies that serve as the withholding agent face direct exposure under both federal and state law. If the title company fails to remit the withheld amount, the Utah State Tax Commission can assess the unpaid amount against the company under Utah Code 59-10-1403(5), plus a penalty of up to 100% of the amount that should have been withheld.

Deal cancellation is the other risk. When [a transaction hits a last-minute compliance snag](/blog/where-deals-break-down), buyers get nervous and start looking for exit clauses. A FIRPTA issue discovered three days before closing—because no one asked the right questions at listing—can crater a deal that took months to put together.

## Common Mistakes Utah Agents Make with Foreign Seller Transactions

The errors below come up repeatedly in enforcement actions and brokerage E&O claims across the state.

**First: Assuming "foreign" means "not living in the U.S."** A nonresident alien who lives in Utah on a visa may still be classified as a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. The test is not where the seller lives—it is their tax residency status under the substantial presence test or green card test. Agents who skip this distinction miss withholding obligations entirely.

Second: Relying on the seller's verbal assurance of citizenship. The proper procedure is to obtain a signed FIRPTA Affidavit of Non-Foreign Status (sometimes called a "Seller's Certification" based on the template in Treasury Regulation 1.1445-2(b)(2)). Without this affidavit in the file, the buyer has no protection from liability if the seller later turns out to be a foreign person.

Third: Failing to coordinate early with the title company on the Utah state withholding calculation. Some agents discover at final settlement that the title company withheld 6.0% of gross proceeds instead of gain, leaving the seller short on net proceeds and angry about a figure no one mentioned during listing.

Fourth: Not advising the seller to file Form TC-675W early enough to receive a withholding certificate before closing. The application process can take weeks. If the agent waits until the purchase contract is signed to raise this, there is rarely enough time.

Fifth: Confusing the buyer's residence exemption with a blanket exemption. The reduced federal rates (0% under $300K, 10% under $1M) only apply when the buyer intends to use the property as their own residence. If the buyer is an investor or an LLC, those exemptions vanish—and the full 15% applies no matter the price.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under Utah Code 61-2f-401 and Utah Admin. Code R162-2f-401a. When a foreign seller transaction goes sideways, the Utah Division of Real Estate will ask what systems the brokerage had in place to prevent the failure. A shrug and "my agent should have known" is not an acceptable answer.

**Every brokerage handling Utah residential or investment transactions should maintain a written policy requiring agents to collect a signed FIRPTA affidavit on every listing.** This is not just for foreign sellers—it is a blanket practice that protects the file regardless of who the seller turns out to be. If the seller cannot or will not sign, that fact itself is a red flag that triggers the withholding analysis.

Brokers should audit transaction files for the following elements before closing is finalized:

| Audit Item | Where It Should Appear | Red Flag if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Signed FIRPTA Affidavit of Non-Foreign Status | Listing file or seller disclosure package | Possible foreign seller not identified |
| Copy of Form TC-675R (if applicable) | Closing file from title company | State withholding may not have been remitted |
| Withholding certificate (TC-675W approval) | Seller correspondence file | Seller may dispute net proceeds post-closing |
| Buyer's residence affidavit (if reduced rate claimed) | Buyer file | Reduced rate may be improperly applied |
| Communication log showing FIRPTA discussed with seller | Agent notes or CRM | No evidence of disclosure = liability exposure |

## Timing the Withholding Certificate Application

Sellers who expect their actual tax liability to be less than 15% of gross proceeds—which is common when the property has minimal gain—can apply for a withholding certificate from the IRS using Form 8288-B. This form asks the IRS to approve a reduced withholding amount or a full exemption. The IRS generally processes these within 90 days, though delays are common.

For Utah's parallel process, the seller files Form TC-675W with the Utah State Tax Commission. Processing times vary but agents should assume at least 30 to 45 days. The practical takeaway: these applications must be filed before the property is even under contract if there is any hope of having them resolved before closing.

If the certificates are not issued by closing, the full withholding amounts must still be collected. The withheld funds are held in escrow and released to the seller (or partially refunded) once the IRS and Utah State Tax Commission issue their approvals. Agents who promise sellers that "we'll sort it out after closing" create a trust problem when hundreds of thousands of dollars sit in escrow for months.

## Working with Title Companies and Tax Counsel

The title company or closing attorney typically acts as the withholding agent in Utah transactions. However, the agent's role is to surface the issue, make appropriate referrals, and ensure the seller has competent tax counsel reviewing their specific situation. Agents are not expected to calculate tax liability themselves—and should not attempt to.

**Your job is identification and referral, not computation.** Document that you asked, document that you referred, and document that the seller acknowledged the withholding requirement in writing. This paper trail is what protects you in a later dispute. If you want a system that reminds you to collect these documents at the right stage of the transaction, a [checklist-driven approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) beats relying on memory every time.

Coordinate with the title company at the earliest possible stage—ideally within 48 hours of taking a listing from a seller whose citizenship status is uncertain. The title company needs lead time to prepare the withholding calculations, prepare the TC-675R filing, and ensure the HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure reflects the withheld amounts accurately.

## Staying Current with Utah FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The Utah State Tax Commission updates withholding rates and procedural requirements periodically, and agents who rely on outdated training material risk noncompliance. For 2026, the state withholding rate remains at 6.0%, and the TC-675R filing window remains 30 days post-transfer. Federal FIRPTA rates have not changed since the PATH Act of 2015 set the general rate at 15%.

Agents who build compliance into their standard workflow—rather than treating foreign seller transactions as one-off emergencies—will close these deals smoothly while competitors scramble.
