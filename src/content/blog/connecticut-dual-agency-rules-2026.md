Connecticut's agency disclosure framework is one of the most prescriptive in New England, and getting it wrong carries real consequences. If you hold a Connecticut license, understanding Connecticut dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 is not optional — it is the baseline for practicing legally and protecting your brokerage from liability. The statutes have teeth, the forms are mandatory, and the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection does not treat violations as minor paperwork oversights.

This post breaks down the specific statutes governing dual and designated agency, the forms you must use and when, what happens when agents fail to comply, and what brokers need to check on every single file.

## How Connecticut Defines Dual Agency Under Statute

Connecticut General Statutes Section 20-325a through 20-325f establish the state's agency disclosure requirements. Under Section 20-325a(b), a dual agent is defined as a licensee who represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. The statute draws a hard line: dual agency cannot exist without the informed, written consent of all parties.

**The consent requirement is not a suggestion — it is a condition precedent to the agency relationship itself.** Without it, the licensee has no lawful authority to act as a dual agent, and any actions taken in that capacity are legally suspect. Connecticut courts have consistently held that the fiduciary duties owed to each party are inherently conflicted in a dual agency arrangement, which is precisely why the consent must be explicit and documented.

The [Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/Real-Estate/Real-Estate-Commission) oversees enforcement and has published guidance clarifying that verbal disclosure alone does not satisfy the statutory requirement. Written consent must be obtained before any substantive negotiation occurs on behalf of both parties.

## Designated Agency: How Connecticut Separates Duties Within a Brokerage

Designated agency — sometimes called appointed agency — allows a single brokerage to represent both sides of a transaction by assigning separate licensees to the buyer and seller. Connecticut recognizes this arrangement under Sections 20-325b and 20-325d, provided specific conditions are met.

The broker must formally designate each agent in writing. Once designated, each agent owes full fiduciary duties exclusively to their assigned client. The supervising broker shifts to a neutral, non-advocacy role and cannot share confidential information between the two designated agents.

| Feature | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Number of agents involved | One agent, both sides | Two separate agents, one brokerage |
| Fiduciary duty scope | Limited to both parties equally | Full fiduciary to assigned client |
| Broker's role | Active dual agent or supervisor | Neutral supervisor only |
| Consent requirement | Written from both parties | Written from both parties |
| Confidentiality obligation | Cannot advocate for either side | Full confidentiality per client |

The distinction matters enormously in practice. Under dual agency, the single agent cannot advise either party on price, terms, or negotiation strategy. Under designated agency, each agent can advocate fully — but only if the information wall holds. If the broker or office staff leaks confidential information between the two sides, the designated agency collapses into de facto dual agency without proper consent.

## The Specific Forms Connecticut Requires

Connecticut mandates the use of the **Disclosure of Brokerage Relationship** form, which must be presented at the first personal meeting where a specific property is discussed. This is not a listing appointment requirement alone — it applies to buyer consultations as well.

The form identifies whether the licensee is acting as a seller's agent, buyer's agent, dual agent, or designated agent. Each category triggers different obligations and limitations, and the form must be signed by the consumer acknowledging receipt and understanding.

For dual agency specifically, the Real Estate Commission requires that the dual agency consent section be completed and signed by both the buyer and seller before the licensee proceeds. The timing here is critical: completing this form after a purchase agreement is already signed creates a retroactive consent problem that regulators view skeptically.

There is no single universal form number like some states issue (e.g., Wisconsin's WB-36). Instead, the Connecticut Real Estate Commission publishes approved language that brokerages must incorporate. Many large Connecticut brokerages use their own compliance-reviewed versions that track the statutory language verbatim. If your brokerage has modified the state's recommended language, your broker should have legal counsel confirming the modifications still satisfy Sections 20-325a through 20-325f.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**The penalties for non-compliance range from deal-killing to career-ending.** Connecticut treats agency disclosure violations as grounds for disciplinary action under CGS Section 20-320(a), which authorizes the Real Estate Commission to suspend, revoke, or refuse to renew a license for violating any provision of Chapter 392.

Fines can reach up to $25,000 per violation under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act (CUTPA), CGS Section 42-110b, if a consumer files a complaint alleging deceptive practices related to undisclosed dual agency. Courts have also awarded actual damages, attorney's fees, and in egregious cases, punitive damages.

From a transactional standpoint, a failure to obtain proper dual agency consent can render the purchase agreement voidable. If either party discovers post-closing that they were in an undisclosed dual agency, they may have grounds to rescind the transaction or sue for damages. This is not theoretical — Connecticut Superior Court has addressed these claims in reported decisions, and the outcomes for non-compliant agents have been uniformly negative.

Beyond individual liability, the supervising broker faces exposure under CGS Section 20-312a for failing to adequately supervise licensees. If the broker knew or should have known that agents were operating as undisclosed dual agents, the brokerage's license is also at risk. This creates a powerful incentive for brokers to implement proactive file audits rather than rely on agent self-reporting.

## Common Mistakes Connecticut Agents Make

After reviewing disciplinary actions and speaking with compliance attorneys, five errors appear repeatedly in Connecticut agency violation cases.

**First, agents disclose agency status too late.** The statute requires disclosure at the "first personal meeting to discuss a specific property." Agents who wait until the offer is being drafted have already violated the timing requirement, even if the form eventually gets signed. The Commission interprets "first personal meeting" broadly to include video calls and in-person showings where a particular listing is discussed substantively.

Second, agents treat the disclosure form as a formality rather than a conversation. Sliding a form across the table without explaining the material limitations of dual agency — particularly the prohibition on price counseling — fails to satisfy the "informed" consent standard. A signed form without genuine understanding can still be challenged as inadequate consent.

Third, agents in designated agency arrangements share confidential client information with their brokerage's other designated agent, often inadvertently. Office chatter, shared CRM notes, or a broker who casually mentions one client's motivation to the other side's agent destroys the information wall. When that wall fails, the designated agency is legally compromised, and similar to [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down), it often happens through small communication lapses rather than intentional misconduct.

Fourth, agents assume that because their brokerage "allows" dual agency, no further steps are needed. Brokerage policy cannot override statutory consent requirements. Each transaction requires its own independent, transaction-specific written consent.

Fifth, agents confuse facilitator or transactional brokerage status — which Connecticut does not formally recognize the same way some other states do — with dual agency. Connecticut's statutory framework does not include a "transaction broker" category. If you represent both sides, you are a dual agent and must comply with dual agency rules regardless of what label you prefer.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Connecticut bear direct supervisory liability under Chapter 392. This is not a passive responsibility — it requires active file review and systems that catch compliance failures before they become complaints.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Frequency |
|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| Disclosure timing | Form signed before or at first substantive meeting | Every transaction |
| Dual agency consent | Both parties' signatures dated before offer execution | Every dual agency file |
| Designated agent appointments | Written designation in file with date | Every in-house two-sided deal |
| Information wall documentation | Separate file access, no shared notes between agents | Quarterly systems check |

## Designated Agency and the Broker's Neutral Role

One of the least understood aspects of Connecticut designated agency is the broker's mandatory neutrality. Once a broker appoints two agents to opposite sides of the same transaction, the broker cannot coach, advise, or advocate for either party. This creates a practical tension in smaller offices where the broker is also actively selling.

If a broker-owner is personally representing the seller and another agent in the office is representing the buyer, the broker cannot simultaneously serve as neutral supervisor and active seller's agent. This scenario effectively requires either declining the designated agency arrangement or bringing in an outside broker to supervise.

The statute does not carve out exceptions for small offices. A two-person brokerage where both the broker and a single agent want to represent opposite sides faces a structural impossibility — there is no neutral party available to supervise. In that scenario, dual agency with full written consent from both parties is the only lawful path, and even then, the limitations on advocacy apply fully.
