## Understanding Nebraska Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on documentary stamps or recording fees does not just delay a closing — it can kill one. The Nebraska transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by the same statutory framework agents have worked under for years, but fee amounts, county-level base page charges, and form requirements still trip up licensees who rely on memory instead of verification. This guide breaks down exactly what Nebraska agents and brokers need to know about current obligations, the forms that must accompany every deed, and the penalties that follow noncompliance.

Nebraska's documentary stamp tax is codified under Nebraska Revised Statutes Chapter 76, Article 9 (Sections 76-901 through 76-908). The Nebraska Department of Revenue and each county's Register of Deeds office share enforcement authority. Agents do not personally pay these taxes, but they are responsible for ensuring their clients understand who owes what and when — because misinformation at the listing appointment or buyer consultation creates liability that flows uphill to the brokerage.

## The Documentary Stamp Tax: Rate, Calculation, and Who Pays

**Nebraska imposes a documentary stamp tax of $2.25 per $1,000 of consideration, or any fraction thereof.** That means a property selling for $350,500 would owe stamps calculated on $351,000 — the next full thousand — resulting in a tax of $789.75. The tax applies to every conveyance of real property by deed unless a statutory exemption under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-902 applies.

The grantor (seller) is the party statutorily responsible for payment under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-901. However, purchase agreements frequently reallocate this cost, so agents must read the contract language carefully. If the contract is silent, the seller pays by default, and the title company or closing attorney will collect accordingly.

Exemptions exist for transfers between spouses, transfers by court order, corrections of previously recorded deeds, and conveyances to governmental bodies. A complete list appears in Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-902. Agents should never assume an exemption applies without confirming with the title company and referencing the statute directly.

## Recording Fees: County-Level Base Charges

Recording fees in Nebraska are separate from the documentary stamp tax and are governed by Neb. Rev. Stat. 33-109. The base recording fee for most instruments, including deeds, is set by statute but can vary slightly by county for additional page charges and technology surcharges.

| Fee Component | Amount (2026) | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| First page recording | $10.00 | Neb. Rev. Stat. 33-109 |
| Each additional page | $6.00 | Neb. Rev. Stat. 33-109 |
| Documentary stamp tax | $2.25 per $1,000 | Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-901 |
| Real Property Transfer Statement filing | No separate fee | Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-214 |
| Mortgage recording (first page) | $10.00 | Neb. Rev. Stat. 33-109 |

Counties may impose an additional fee of up to $1.00 per page for a Records Preservation and Modernization Fund under Neb. Rev. Stat. 33-109.02. Douglas, Lancaster, and Sarpy counties all assess this surcharge. Agents working multi-county transactions need to verify with each Register of Deeds office rather than applying a single fee schedule across the board.

The buyer typically pays recording fees on the deed and mortgage, while the seller pays for recording any release or satisfaction of their existing mortgage. This allocation is customary, not statutory, so the purchase agreement controls.

## Required Forms: Nebraska Form 521 and Deed Specifications

**Every deed presented for recording must be accompanied by a completed Nebraska Real Property Transfer Statement, Form 521, filed with the county Register of Deeds.** This form reports the sale price, property description, buyer and seller identities, and whether an exemption from documentary stamps is claimed. The Nebraska Department of Revenue uses Form 521 data for property tax assessment equalization studies across the state.

Form 521 requires the signatures of both the buyer and seller (or their authorized agents). If the form is incomplete or unsigned, the Register of Deeds will reject the document package. Agents should confirm at the pre-closing stage that the title company or attorney has a fully executed Form 521 ready for submission.

Deeds themselves must meet formatting requirements under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-211 through 76-238 — standard letter-size paper, minimum 1-inch margins on all sides, a 3-inch top margin on the first page for recorder indexing, and legible black ink. Non-conforming documents face rejection or a non-standard document surcharge in many counties.

For agents who want to double-check current form versions, the [Nebraska Department of Revenue](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/) maintains downloadable copies of Form 521 on its website, along with instructions for exemption claims.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Taxes and Recording

Mistakes in this area rarely produce dramatic courtroom scenes. They produce something worse: delayed closings, frustrated clients, and brokerage compliance flags that accumulate over time. Here are the errors that show up most frequently.

**First, agents misquote the documentary stamp tax amount during listing presentations.** They round incorrectly, forget the "fraction thereof" rule, or confuse the Nebraska rate with rates from neighboring states. A $500,000 sale produces $1,125.00 in documentary stamps — not $1,125.50, not $1,000. Getting this wrong in a net sheet erodes client trust and can constitute a material misrepresentation if the seller relies on it in accepting an offer.

Second, agents fail to confirm which party is paying the transfer tax before the closing disclosure is issued. The contract may say "seller pays customary closing costs," but in some Nebraska markets — particularly in competitive multiple-offer situations — buyers have agreed to cover documentary stamps as a concession. If the agent does not communicate this to the title company early, the closing disclosure arrives wrong, triggering a new three-day review period under TRID rules and pushing the closing date.

Third, agents assume that transfers between family members or LLCs owned by the same person are automatically exempt from documentary stamps. They are not always exempt. Transfers to an LLC in which the grantor holds less than a 100% interest, for example, may trigger tax liability. The statute is narrow; agents should never advise clients on exemption eligibility without directing them to legal counsel or the title company.

Fourth, agents neglect to verify county-specific recording requirements when listing or selling property in rural counties where they rarely transact. A deed that records smoothly in Douglas County may be rejected in a smaller county due to different margin requirements or indexing preferences. One phone call to the Register of Deeds office prevents a rejection that delays the chain of title transfer.

Fifth, agents forget to account for recording fees on both sides of the transaction in net sheets and buyer cost estimates. The seller's payoff of an existing mortgage requires a satisfaction to be recorded, and that fee belongs on the seller's side of the ledger. Omitting it makes the agent look careless at best and deceptive at worst.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Nebraska Real Estate License Act, Neb. Rev. Stat. 81-885 et seq. The Nebraska Real Estate Commission holds designated brokers accountable for the conduct of their affiliated licensees — including material misrepresentations about closing costs.

A broker's compliance audit should verify three things on every closed file. First, that the net sheet or seller estimate provided at listing matched the actual documentary stamp tax within a reasonable margin. Second, that the purchase agreement clearly allocates responsibility for the transfer tax and recording fees. Third, that Form 521 was completed and filed — brokers can confirm this by checking the recorded deed package in the county's online records portal.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Net sheet accuracy | Documentary stamp amount matches $2.25/$1,000 rule | Misrepresentation complaint to NREC |
| Contract allocation clause | Transfer tax responsibility stated or defaulting to statute | Closing delay, potential rescission |
| Form 521 completion | Signed by both parties, filed with deed | Recording rejection, assessment gap |
| County fee verification | Correct county's fee schedule applied | Client overcharge or underfunding at closing |
| Exemption documentation | Legal basis cited if claiming exemption | Tax evasion liability, fine up to $1,000 |

Brokers running [weekly pipeline reviews](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) should add recording fee verification to their pre-closing checklist rather than relying on the title company alone. The title company is a partner, not a backstop for agent negligence.

## Penalties for Noncompliance: Fines, Liability, and Deal Risk

Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-903 specifies that any person who willfully fails to pay or evades the documentary stamp tax commits a Class III misdemeanor, punishable by a fine of up to $500, up to three months in jail, or both. A separate civil penalty of up to $1,000 may be assessed. These penalties attach to the party responsible for payment — not to the agent — but an agent who actively facilitates evasion (such as advising a client to understate consideration on Form 521) faces potential accessory liability and virtually certain disciplinary action from the Nebraska Real Estate Commission.

From a practical standpoint, the more common consequence is deal disruption. A Register of Deeds office that rejects a document package sends the entire closing into limbo. The buyer's lender may require a new closing disclosure with corrected figures, restarting timing requirements. In purchase agreements with tight deadlines, this can trigger a contractual default — and [that is exactly where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Agents also face disciplinary risk under Neb. Rev. Stat. 81-885.24, which authorizes the Nebraska Real Estate Commission to sanction licensees for incompetence, negligence, or untrustworthiness. Repeatedly misquoting closing costs or failing to ensure proper documentation falls squarely within these categories. Sanctions range from a letter of reprimand to license revocation.
