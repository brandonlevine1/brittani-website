The national real estate commission overhaul that began with the NAR settlement has landed squarely in Vermont, and agents who treat it as background noise are courting real trouble. Understanding Vermont commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 is no longer optional knowledge — it is a baseline licensing competency. Every buyer showing, every listing agreement, and every closing table now carries compliance stakes that did not exist two years ago.

This article breaks down the specific Vermont statutes, forms, regulatory bodies, and enforcement realities that licensed agents and brokers need to internalize right now.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed Commission Rules in Vermont

The August 2024 NAR settlement eliminated cooperative compensation offers from MLS systems nationwide. In Vermont, this means PrimeMLS — the dominant MLS serving the state — no longer permits listing agents to advertise a commission split to buyer agents through its data fields. The change is structural, not cosmetic.

Vermont's real estate practice is governed by 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41 and administered by the Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC), housed within the Office of Professional Regulation under the Secretary of State. VREC did not need to pass new legislation to enforce the settlement's requirements; the existing statutory framework around disclosure, agency relationships, and licensee conduct already provides the enforcement backbone. What changed is how VREC interprets existing obligations in light of the new commission reality.

Agents must now treat compensation as a fully disclosed, independently negotiated term — not a default embedded in the MLS. That single shift has rewritten the practical workflow of every Vermont transaction.

## Written Buyer Agreements: Vermont's New Non-Negotiable

**Before a buyer agent shows a single property in Vermont, a signed written buyer representation agreement must be in place.** This requirement flows directly from the NAR settlement terms and is enforced by PrimeMLS participation rules. VREC's existing mandate under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296 already required written disclosure of agency relationships, but the settlement raised the bar from disclosure to binding agreement.

The Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) has updated its standard forms to reflect these changes. Agents should be using the current VAR Buyer Representation Agreement, which specifies the compensation the buyer agrees to pay their agent, the duration of the agreement, and the scope of services. The form must state compensation in a clear, objective manner — a flat dollar amount or a defined percentage — rather than open-ended or "whatever the seller offers" language.

This is where many agents stumble. A vague compensation term renders the agreement non-compliant and exposes the agent to a VREC disciplinary complaint. The [consequences of missing compliance deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) apply here with force: a defective buyer agreement can unwind a deal after ratification if challenged.

## Commission Disclosure at the Listing Side

Listing agents in Vermont face their own set of recalibrated obligations. The VAR Listing Agreement form now must clearly disclose to the seller that buyer-agent compensation is not offered through the MLS. Sellers can still choose to offer concessions that cover buyer-agent fees, but this must be negotiated and documented outside MLS data fields.

Here is how the flow works in practice:

| Step | Pre-Settlement | Post-Settlement (2026) |
|------|---------------|----------------------|
| Listing agent sets co-op commission | Entered in PrimeMLS | Prohibited in PrimeMLS |
| Buyer-agent compensation source | Assumed from MLS offer | Negotiated per transaction |
| Seller disclosure of compensation | Embedded in listing agreement | Separate, explicit disclosure required |
| Buyer agreement on compensation | Often informal or absent | Written, signed, specific amount required |
| Compensation negotiation timing | Before listing goes live | During offer negotiation or prior |

Sellers must understand, in writing, that they are not obligated to pay the buyer's agent. If they choose to, the amount and terms belong in the purchase and sale agreement or in a separate seller concession addendum — not in the MLS listing.

## Specific Forms Vermont Agents Must Use

Vermont agents operating through VAR have access to updated standard forms. The critical documents in the post-settlement environment include the VAR Buyer Representation Agreement, the VAR Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement, and the standard Purchase and Sale Contract with the updated compensation provisions. Agents should also be familiar with the VAR Agency Disclosure Form, which VREC requires under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296 to be provided at first substantive contact with any party.

**The agency disclosure form is not the same as the buyer representation agreement, and confusing the two is a compliance violation.** The disclosure form explains the types of agency relationships available in Vermont. The buyer representation agreement is a binding contract specifying compensation and scope. Both are required; neither replaces the other.

For transactions involving dual agency — still permitted in Vermont with informed written consent — agents must use the VAR Disclosed Dual Agency Consent Form. Under the new commission framework, dual agency raises heightened scrutiny because the agent is negotiating compensation terms on behalf of both parties. VREC has signaled that dual agency complaints involving unclear commission arrangements will receive aggressive review.

Agents working outside the VAR forms system should confirm with their broker that any proprietary forms meet the minimum requirements of 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41 and the [current PrimeMLS rules](https://www.primemls.com).

## What Happens If You Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance in Vermont range from inconvenient to career-ending. VREC has authority under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299 to impose disciplinary sanctions including fines, license suspension, and license revocation. A first offense for failing to provide proper agency disclosures or operating without a valid buyer agreement can result in a formal reprimand and fines that typically start around $1,000 but can escalate with repeated violations.

Beyond VREC sanctions, agents face civil liability. A buyer or seller who was not properly informed about commission obligations can pursue a claim for damages. If a court finds that an agent collected a fee without a valid written agreement, the agent may be ordered to disgorge the commission entirely. Vermont courts have historically been protective of consumer interests in professional licensing disputes, and the post-settlement environment has only sharpened that tendency.

| Violation | Potential Consequence |
|-----------|---------------------|
| No signed buyer agreement before showing | VREC fine, MLS sanction, commission disgorgement |
| Commission offer in MLS data fields | PrimeMLS fine, potential VREC complaint |
| Missing agency disclosure form | VREC disciplinary action, civil liability |
| Vague or open-ended compensation terms | Agreement deemed non-compliant, deal cancellation risk |
| Dual agency without written consent on compensation | Heightened VREC scrutiny, potential license suspension |

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a buyer discovers mid-transaction that their agent's compensation terms were never properly documented, they have grounds to void the agreement. That does not just cost the agent a commission — it exposes them to a claim from the seller for lost time and damages.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Are Making Right Now

Even agents who understand the new rules in theory are making predictable errors in practice. These are the five most frequent missteps showing up in Vermont transactions since the settlement took effect.

**First, agents are still verbally quoting commission rates to buyers without getting the written agreement signed first.** A verbal understanding is not compliant. The written agreement must precede any property tours, and the compensation term must be specific.

Second, some listing agents are using the "agent remarks" or "showing instructions" fields in PrimeMLS to signal commission availability. PrimeMLS compliance staff actively monitors these fields, and violations trigger fines and can escalate to VREC referral. The workaround is not clever; it is traceable and penalized.

Third, agents are recycling pre-settlement forms. The older VAR Buyer Representation Agreement that referenced MLS cooperative compensation is obsolete. Using it creates a defective agreement that may not survive legal challenge. Always confirm you are using the most current version of any VAR form, which you can find on how [broken workflows create compliance gaps](/blog/why-transaction-coordination-is-broken).

Fourth, buyer agents are failing to discuss compensation during the initial consultation. They wait until an offer is being drafted to raise the topic, which creates pressure and confusion. The compensation conversation must happen at the outset — ideally at or before the signing of the buyer representation agreement.

Fifth, agents neglect to document seller concessions properly when a seller agrees to cover buyer-agent compensation. A verbal agreement from the seller is not enough. The concession must appear in the purchase and sale agreement or in a standalone written addendum, signed by the seller.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Vermont carry supervisory liability under 26 V.S.A. Section 2297. If an agent under your brokerage fails to comply with the new commission rules, VREC can and will hold the broker responsible. This is not theoretical — VREC has issued guidance reminding brokers that supervisory obligations extend to form compliance and compensation disclosures.

**Every Vermont broker should be auditing three things on every transaction file: the signed buyer representation agreement, the compensation disclosure on the listing side, and the final settlement statement showing that commission disbursements match what was agreed to in writing.** Missing any one of these creates an audit trail gap that VREC investigators will flag.

Brokers should also establish a firm policy on MLS compliance. Agents should not be posting listings without the broker or a designated compliance reviewer confirming that no compensation language appears in prohibited fields. A single PrimeMLS violation from one agent can trigger a brokerage-wide review.

Training is another obligation that brokers cannot treat casually. VREC's continuing education requirements under 26 V.S.A. Section 2295 include mandatory coursework on agency and disclosure. Brokers should supplement state-mandated CE with internal training specifically addressing the post-settlement commission workflow.
