## Understanding Vermont Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Vermont has never been a state that takes agency relationships lightly. The **Vermont dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** framework places explicit disclosure and consent requirements on licensees, and the consequences for getting them wrong range from voided contracts to license revocation. If you hold a Vermont license, you need to know exactly where the lines are drawn.

The governing authority here is the Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC), which operates under the Office of Professional Regulation. Agency duties for licensees are codified primarily in [26 V.S.A. Chapter 41](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/26/041), with Subchapter 3 addressing agency relationships in detail. The statutes don't leave much room for interpretation, and neither does the Commission when enforcement actions come up.

Vermont recognizes several agency relationship types: seller agency, buyer agency, dual agency, designated agency, and transaction facilitation. The distinctions matter because each type triggers different fiduciary obligations, different disclosure requirements, and different liability profiles for the licensee and the brokerage.


## How Vermont Defines Dual Agency and Designated Agency

**Dual agency in Vermont occurs when a single licensee, or a brokerage without designated agents, represents both the buyer and seller in the same transaction.** Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296, dual agency is permitted only when both parties give informed written consent after receiving a clear explanation of what dual agency means and what they are giving up.

The statute is explicit: a dual agent cannot advocate for one party over another. You cannot advise the buyer to offer less while simultaneously counseling the seller to hold firm. Your fiduciary duty to both parties becomes limited the moment dual agency kicks in, and both clients must understand that limitation in writing before you proceed.

Designated agency offers a structural alternative. Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2297, a principal broker may designate separate licensees within the same brokerage to represent the buyer and the seller independently. This preserves the full fiduciary relationship each client expects, because each designated agent owes undivided loyalty to their assigned client.

| Feature | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Number of licensees involved | One licensee represents both parties | Two separate licensees, one for each party |
| Fiduciary duty | Limited; no advocacy for either side | Full fiduciary duty to assigned client |
| Written consent required | Yes, from both parties | Yes, from both parties |
| Broker's role | Broker may be the dual agent | Broker designates and supervises both agents |
| Confidentiality | Cannot share either party's confidential info with the other | Each agent protects their client's information independently |


## The Forms You Must Use and When to Present Them

Vermont requires specific disclosure forms at defined points in the transaction. The primary form is the **Agency Disclosure and Consent Form**, which VREC mandates be presented to all parties at the earliest substantive contact. "Substantive contact" means the moment a conversation goes beyond general market information and into specific property discussions or financial details.

For dual agency situations, agents must present the **Consent to Dual Agency** section of the disclosure form. Both the buyer and the seller sign this form, acknowledging that they understand the limitations. The form language tracks the statute closely, spelling out that the dual agent will not disclose confidential information from one party to the other and will not advocate for either side.

**When a brokerage opts for designated agency, the principal broker completes the designation in writing and provides the Designated Agency Disclosure to both clients.** This document identifies the specific licensees assigned to each party and confirms that the brokerage will implement information barriers to protect confidentiality. The Vermont Real Estate Commission publishes updated form templates periodically, and agents should verify they are using the most current version available from the VREC website.

The timing matters as much as the form itself. If you present the disclosure after a client has already shared pricing strategy or negotiation limits, you have a problem. The Commission's position is that late disclosure undermines the "informed" part of informed consent, which can render the consent defective.


## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalties for violating Vermont's agency disclosure rules are not theoretical. VREC has the authority under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299 to impose disciplinary action including license suspension, license revocation, and monetary fines. Enforcement actions are a matter of public record and appear in the Commission's disciplinary database.

Beyond administrative penalties, agents and brokerages face significant civil liability. A buyer or seller who was not properly informed of a dual agency relationship can seek rescission of the purchase contract. That means the deal unwinds entirely, and the agent may be personally liable for damages the aggrieved party suffered, including lost earnest money, inspection costs, appraisal fees, and consequential damages.

**Errors and omissions insurance may not cover claims arising from undisclosed dual agency, because many E&O policies exclude intentional or knowing violations of licensing law.** If a court or the Commission finds that you knew you were in a dual agency situation and failed to disclose it, your carrier may deny the claim. That leaves you personally exposed, which is exactly the outcome the statutes are designed to prevent.


## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Vermont Agency Rules

Even experienced licensees trip over agency compliance in Vermont. The errors tend to cluster around five recurring scenarios that come up in Commission complaints and civil litigation.

**First, agents frequently confuse transaction facilitation with dual agency.** Transaction facilitation in Vermont means the licensee assists both parties without representing either one. It is a distinct relationship with its own disclosure requirements. Agents who think they are "just helping both sides" without formal dual agency consent are often operating in an undisclosed dual agency, which is a violation regardless of intent.

Second, agents within the same brokerage assume that designated agency is automatic. It is not. The principal broker must affirmatively designate the agents and document the designation. Two agents in the same office who happen to be working with a buyer and a seller on the same property are not designated agents until the broker formally assigns them and the required disclosures are signed.

Third, agents present the disclosure forms at the wrong time. Waiting until the offer stage to disclose a dual agency relationship is too late under Vermont law. The statute requires disclosure at first substantive contact, and the Commission interprets this strictly. Similar timing pitfalls arise in [other states with strict disclosure windows](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026), but Vermont's enforcement has been particularly consistent on this point.

Fourth, agents fail to maintain confidentiality after entering dual agency. Even with consent, a dual agent cannot reveal that a buyer is willing to pay more or that a seller would accept less. Agents who let slip any negotiation intelligence from one party to the other violate the statute and their fiduciary duty simultaneously.

Fifth, agents operating on teams sometimes create accidental dual agency because team structures blur the lines between individual representation and brokerage-level representation. If two team members under the same license holder are working both sides, the agency relationship defaults to dual agency unless the broker has formally designated each member and disclosed it properly.


## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Vermont carry direct supervisory liability for agency compliance within their firms. Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2295, the broker is responsible for ensuring that every licensee affiliated with the brokerage follows the disclosure and consent requirements. If an agent in your office botches a dual agency disclosure, the Commission can and does hold the broker accountable.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Agency disclosure forms in transaction files | Confirm signed, dated, and presented at first substantive contact | Every transaction |
| Designated agency documentation | Verify broker's written designation and disclosure to both parties | Every in-house transaction |
| Confidentiality protocols | Ensure information barriers are in place between designated agents | Quarterly review |
| Form version currency | Confirm agents are using current VREC-approved form templates | Annually or when VREC updates |
| Team structure compliance | Audit team transactions for accidental dual agency | Every team transaction |

Brokers should also implement a written office policy on agency relationships that goes beyond what the statute requires. The policy should spell out when and how agents must escalate potential dual agency situations to the broker, what information barriers look like in practice, and the consequences within the firm for noncompliance. The Commission looks favorably on brokerages that demonstrate proactive compliance infrastructure during enforcement reviews.

**Tracking all of this across dozens or hundreds of transactions per year is where most brokerages struggle.** The disclosure timing, the form versions, the designation paperwork, and the confidentiality protocols all need to be verified for every transaction file. Relying on individual agents to self-police creates gaps that only surface when a complaint is filed, which is the worst possible time to discover a missing form. ## Designated Agency and the Information Barrier Problem

The concept of an "information barrier" within a brokerage sounds clean on paper but gets messy in practice. Vermont expects that when two agents within the same firm are designated to represent opposing parties, the brokerage prevents any sharing of confidential client information between those agents. In a large firm with separate offices, this is manageable. In a four-person shop where everyone shares a printer and a coffee pot, it requires deliberate effort.

Brokers need to document how the barrier works. This means written protocols that specify which files are restricted, how electronic records are segregated, and what happens during office meetings when both transactions are discussed. The Commission has not published a prescriptive standard for information barriers, but the expectation is that the broker can demonstrate a good-faith system that actually functions.

Agents on both sides of a designated agency arrangement should avoid discussing the transaction with each other entirely. Casual hallway conversations about a shared deal, even without disclosing confidential terms, can create the appearance of a compromised barrier. Appearances matter in enforcement proceedings, because the Commission evaluates whether the brokerage's practices were sufficient to protect the clients' interests.


## How Vermont Compares to Neighboring States

Vermont's approach sits in a middle ground nationally. Some states prohibit dual agency outright, while others allow it with minimal guardrails. Vermont permits it but wraps it in disclosure and consent requirements that have real teeth. The designated agency option gives brokerages a path to avoid dual agency entirely, which is the route many firms prefer given the liability exposure.

Compared to states like Wisconsin, which has [its own detailed dual agency framework](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026), Vermont places a heavier emphasis on the broker's supervisory role and the formal designation process. The common thread across most states is that written consent is non-negotiable, but Vermont's enforcement record suggests the Commission takes the timing and quality of that consent more seriously than many of its counterparts.

Agents who hold licenses in multiple New England states should pay close attention to the differences. What satisfies the disclosure requirement in New Hampshire or Massachusetts may fall short in Vermont, and assuming equivalence is a fast path to a complaint.
