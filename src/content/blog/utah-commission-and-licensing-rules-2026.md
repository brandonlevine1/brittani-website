## Understanding Utah Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Utah Division of Real Estate (DRE), operating under the Department of Commerce, has refined its oversight of commission structures and licensing requirements heading into 2026. For agents and brokers operating along the Wasatch Front and beyond, the **Utah commission & licensing rules 2026** framework governs everything from how you get paid to how you maintain your license. Ignoring these rules does not merely invite a slap on the wrist — it opens the door to deal cancellations, civil lawsuits, and license revocation.

Utah's regulatory environment sits at the intersection of state statute (Title 61, Chapter 2f of the Utah Code) and administrative rule (R162-2f). Both carry the force of law, and agents frequently confuse which document governs which requirement. This post breaks down exactly what you need to know about commission handling, licensing maintenance, and the specific pitfalls that catch even experienced licensees off guard.

The [Utah Division of Real Estate](https://realestate.utah.gov/) publishes its rules through the state's administrative code portal, and agents should bookmark both the statute and rule sets for reference during transactions.

## Commission Structure and Payment Rules Under Utah Code 61-2f

**All commission payments in Utah must flow through the principal broker.** This is not a suggestion or a best practice — Utah Code 61-2f-401(2) explicitly states that a sales agent or associate broker may not accept compensation for real estate services from any person or entity other than the principal broker with whom they are affiliated. Violating this rule triggers disciplinary action regardless of whether the transaction otherwise closed cleanly.

The statute does not dictate commission rates. Utah adheres to the principle that commissions are negotiable between parties, and any attempt to fix rates among brokerages violates federal antitrust law. However, the mechanism of payment is strictly regulated. An agent who receives a referral fee check directly from an out-of-state brokerage, for example, has violated 61-2f-401(2) even if the amount was earned legitimately.

Commission disputes between cooperating brokerages are typically handled through the Utah Association of Realtors (UAR) arbitration process for members, or through civil court. The DRE generally does not intervene in disputes over commission amounts — its jurisdiction covers whether the commission was handled in a legally compliant manner.

| Scenario | Compliant? | Governing Rule |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| Agent receives commission check from own broker | Yes | 61-2f-401(2) |
| Agent receives referral fee directly from cooperating broker | No | 61-2f-401(2) |
| Broker pays bonus directly to another brokerage's agent | No | 61-2f-401(2) |
| Agent's LLC receives commission from principal broker | Yes, if registered with DRE | R162-2f-401a |
| Team leader distributes commission to team members through broker | Yes | 61-2f-401(2) |

Agents who form LLCs or business entities to receive commission must register that entity with the DRE. The entity does not need its own license, but it must be disclosed and authorized through the principal broker. Failing to register means the entity is receiving unauthorized compensation.

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Obligations

Utah real estate licenses are issued for two-year terms. The renewal cycle requires 18 hours of approved continuing education (CE), split between 9 hours of mandatory core topics and 9 hours of elective coursework. Utah Administrative Rule R162-2f-204 governs CE requirements, and the DRE maintains a list of approved providers and courses.

**Missing the renewal deadline does not immediately void your license, but it does make every transaction you touch during the lapse period a potential violation.** Practicing on an expired license violates Utah Code 61-2f-301, which carries penalties including fines, license suspension, and potential criminal misdemeanor charges. The DRE allows a grace period for late renewal with a penalty fee, but any real estate activity conducted during the lapse period remains a violation.

New agents must complete 120 hours of pre-licensing education and pass the Utah-specific portion of the licensing exam administered through Pearson VUE. Associate brokers need an additional 120 hours beyond the sales agent requirement, plus three years of active practice. Principal brokers must hold an associate broker license for at least two of the preceding five years before applying.

The distinction between a sales agent, associate broker, and principal broker matters enormously for commission handling and supervisory liability. Under R162-2f-401a, only the principal broker holds the authority to establish trust accounts, disburse funds, and receive compensation from outside parties on behalf of affiliated licensees.

## Key Utah Forms That Govern Commission Disclosure

Utah's transaction forms are primarily produced by the UAR and approved for use statewide. Several forms directly address commission and compensation matters that agents must handle correctly.

The **REPC (Real Estate Purchase Contract)** — UAR Form 100 — contains Section 7 addressing broker compensation. This section was revised following the NAR settlement guidelines to clarify when and how buyer-broker compensation is disclosed within the purchase agreement. Agents must complete this section accurately, particularly when the seller is offering concessions that cover buyer-agent compensation.

The **Buyer Broker Agreement** — UAR Form 200 — establishes the compensation arrangement between the buyer and their agent's brokerage. Under current DRE guidance for 2026, this form should be signed before substantive brokerage services begin. The form specifies the commission rate or flat fee, the duration of the agreement, and the conditions under which the buyer may owe compensation if the seller does not offer it.

The **Agency Disclosure** — UAR Form 210 — must be provided at the earliest reasonable opportunity. While this form primarily addresses representation relationships rather than commission, it establishes the fiduciary framework within which commission discussions occur. An agent who fails to provide Form 210 before substantive discussions about compensation has put the entire transaction at regulatory risk.

| Form Number | Form Name | Commission Relevance | When Required |
|-------------|-----------|---------------------|---------------|
| UAR 100 | Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC) | Section 7: broker compensation terms | At offer submission |
| UAR 200 | Buyer Broker Agreement | Defines buyer-agent compensation | Before brokerage services begin |
| UAR 210 | Agency Disclosure | Establishes representation framework | Earliest reasonable opportunity |
| UAR 250 | Listing Agreement | Seller-agent compensation terms | Before marketing property |
| UAR 300 | Commission Disbursement Authorization | Directs title company on splits | Before closing |

The Commission Disbursement Authorization (UAR Form 300) is particularly critical at closing. This form instructs the title company or closing agent on exactly how to split and distribute commission funds. Errors on this form result in delayed closings, misdirected payments, and potential trust account violations if funds are sent to unauthorized recipients.

## Common Mistakes Utah Agents Make With Commission and Licensing

**Mistake 1: Accepting compensation before the transaction closes without broker authorization.** Some agents negotiate advance draws or pre-closing bonuses directly with clients or cooperating agents. Under 61-2f-401, any compensation — regardless of timing — must be received through the principal broker. Advance payment arrangements must be documented and processed through the brokerage's trust or operating account.

**Mistake 2: Letting continuing education lapse while maintaining active listings.** Agents sometimes fail to track their CE cycle end date, especially when it falls mid-transaction. Every day spent practicing on an expired license is a separate violation under Utah Code 61-2f-301. The DRE has historically issued fines of $1,000-$5,000 for practicing while unlicensed, and affected transactions may face contract voidability challenges from opposing parties.

Mistake 3: Failing to update the DRE within 10 days of a change in affiliation, personal information, or business entity status. Utah Administrative Rule R162-2f-202 requires prompt notification of any change that affects the license record. Agents who switch brokerages and begin working under the new broker before the transfer is recorded with the DRE are technically practicing without proper licensure during the gap.

Mistake 4: Not disclosing personal interest in a transaction when receiving commission. Under Utah Code 61-2f-401(4), licensees must disclose any direct or indirect interest in a property — including situations where the agent is also the buyer, seller, or holds an ownership stake. Failure to disclose voids the agent's entitlement to commission and may trigger rescission rights for the other party.

Mistake 5: Using outdated forms that reference superseded commission structures. The post-NAR-settlement changes to buyer compensation disclosure mean that forms from 2023 or earlier may contain language that creates compliance issues. Agents should verify they are using the most current UAR-approved versions, which are updated annually and distributed through the UAR's form library.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Utah carry direct supervisory liability under Utah Code 61-2f-402. When an affiliated agent violates commission or licensing rules, the broker faces potential discipline even if they were unaware of the violation. The DRE's position is that ignorance of an agent's misconduct reflects a failure of supervision.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of three specific areas: trust account records, agent license status, and commission disbursement documentation.** Trust accounts are the most common source of disciplinary actions against Utah brokerages. Under R162-2f-401a(3), brokers must reconcile trust accounts monthly and retain records for at least three years after the transaction closes.

Agent license verification should not be a one-time event at hiring. Brokers should implement a system that flags upcoming renewal deadlines for every affiliated licensee. The DRE's online license lookup tool provides real-time status information, and a quarterly check takes minutes but prevents catastrophic exposure. If an agent closes a transaction while technically unlicensed, the broker may face liability for the entire commission earned and potential regulatory sanctions.

Commission disbursement records must match the instructions provided on UAR Form 300 and align with the terms specified in the listing agreement (UAR Form 250) and buyer broker agreement (UAR Form 200). Discrepancies between these documents create audit red flags and may indicate unauthorized commission splits, undisclosed referral arrangements, or kickback schemes that violate both state law and RESPA.

## Utah Commission & Licensing Rules 2026: Penalties and Enforcement Actions

The Utah Real Estate Commission, which operates as an advisory body to the DRE, reviews disciplinary matters and recommends sanctions. Penalties for commission and licensing violations range from educational requirements and probation to outright license revocation. The DRE publishes enforcement actions on its website, and these are searchable public records.

Fines for individual violations can reach $5,000 per occurrence under Utah Code 61-2f-504. For systematic violations — such as a brokerage operating without proper trust account procedures — cumulative fines can reach tens of thousands of dollars. Civil liability from affected consumers adds another layer. An agent found to have practiced while unlicensed may be ordered to disgorge all commission earned during the lapse period.

Criminal prosecution is rare but possible. Utah Code 61-2f-501 classifies certain licensing violations as class A misdemeanors, carrying potential jail time of up to 364 days and fines up to $2,500. These provisions typically apply to individuals who never held a license and practiced real estate, but they can also apply to agents whose licenses were revoked and who continued practicing.

Contract enforceability is perhaps the most overlooked consequence. Under Utah law, contracts procured through the services of an unlicensed individual may be voidable at the option of the harmed party. This means a buyer or seller who discovers their agent was practicing on an expired license could potentially unwind the entire transaction — even after closing.
