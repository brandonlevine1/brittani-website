## Understanding Mississippi Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Mississippi who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not merely academic for licensed agents. It dictates how you draft offers, advise clients, and structure negotiations in a state where customs vary by county and attorney involvement is the norm rather than the exception. Getting this wrong exposes you to license complaints, deal collapses, and malpractice claims.

Mississippi is an attorney-closing state, which means the allocation of closing costs carries a different weight than in states where title companies drive the process. The Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) holds agents accountable for accurate disclosure and proper documentation of cost responsibility in every transaction file. Agents who treat closing cost allocation as an afterthought are the ones whose files get flagged in audits.

## The Legal Framework Governing Cost Allocation

Mississippi does not have a single statute that mandates which party pays which closing cost. Instead, the allocation is governed by contract terms, established custom, and the requirements of the buyer's lender. Miss. Code Ann. Section 89-1-1 et seq. addresses conveyancing requirements, while [MREC regulations](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/) under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-1 through 73-35-37 govern agent conduct in representing cost obligations.

The Mississippi Association of Realtors (MAR) publishes standardized forms that serve as the primary vehicle for memorializing cost allocation. MAR Form 601, the Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement, contains specific provisions where agents must indicate closing cost responsibilities. The Settlement Statement Addendum (MAR Form 605) provides additional line-item detail when the standard form's checkboxes are insufficient.

**Failure to properly complete these forms does not just create confusion — it creates liability.** Under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-21, the MREC can impose disciplinary sanctions for misrepresentation, omission of material facts, or negligent preparation of documents. Sanctions range from mandatory continuing education to license suspension and fines up to $5,000 per violation.

## Seller Concessions and TRID Compliance

**Seller concessions — often called seller-paid closing costs — remain one of the most powerful negotiation tools in Mississippi, but agents routinely document them incorrectly.** A concession must be reflected on MAR Form 601 in the specific financing terms section, not buried in a separate addendum that a closing attorney might overlook.

Conventional loans typically cap seller concessions at 3% of the purchase price for buyers putting down less than 10%, 6% for down payments between 10% and 25%, and 9% for down payments above 25%. FHA loans cap at 6%, and VA loans allow up to 4% in concessions plus reasonable and customary fees. Mississippi agents who promise concessions exceeding lender caps put deals on a collision course with underwriting rejection.

The TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure (TRID) rules require that seller concessions appear on the buyer's Closing Disclosure in Section H. When a Mississippi closing attorney identifies a discrepancy between the purchase agreement and the Closing Disclosure, the closing gets delayed — often by days, sometimes by weeks. Agents who understand [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) recognize that closing cost disputes rank among the top three reasons Mississippi transactions fail to close on schedule.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make

The following errors surface repeatedly in MREC complaint files and broker audit findings. Each one is preventable with proper training and file management.

**First, agents fail to specify cost allocation in the initial offer.** Leaving MAR Form 601's closing cost fields blank or checking "per local custom" without defining what that custom is creates ambiguity that leads to disputes at the closing table. The MREC expects agents to advise their clients on customary allocations and memorialize the agreement in writing before acceptance.

Second, agents confuse "seller concessions" with "seller-paid costs." A concession is a credit toward the buyer's costs. A seller-paid cost is a specific expense the seller agrees to pay directly. The distinction matters for lender underwriting, TRID disclosure, and appraisal guidelines. Conflating the two can result in an appraisal adjustment that kills the buyer's financing.

Third, agents representing sellers fail to account for the cost of the owner's title insurance when estimating net proceeds. In Mississippi, the owner's policy premium varies by property value and title company, typically ranging from $500 to $2,500 on a median-priced home. Omitting this from a net sheet misleads the seller and violates the agent's fiduciary duty under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-3.

Fourth, agents neglect to verify whether the buyer's lender allows certain costs to be paid by the seller. Some loan programs prohibit the seller from paying specific fees. When an agent drafts an addendum assigning those fees to the seller without lender confirmation, the closing attorney must restructure the settlement — delaying closing and eroding client confidence.

Fifth, agents fail to update the cost allocation when contract amendments change the purchase price or financing terms. A price reduction negotiated after inspection changes the math on percentage-based concessions. Missing this creates a Closing Disclosure that does not match the amended contract, triggering a mandatory three-day waiting period reset under TRID.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Mississippi carry direct liability for their agents' closing cost representations under MREC Rule 3.2, which requires brokers to maintain adequate supervision of all licensees. A broker who fails to catch recurring documentation errors faces their own disciplinary exposure, separate from the agent's individual liability.

**Brokers should audit every transaction file for three specific elements related to closing costs.** First, confirm that MAR Form 601 contains a completed closing cost allocation section with no blank fields. Second, verify that any seller concession amount falls within the applicable loan program's cap. Third, ensure that any amendment to the original terms includes a corresponding update to the cost allocation provisions.

Beyond individual file review, brokers should establish a quarterly audit calendar that samples closed transaction files and checks for pattern errors. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on disclosure updates are statistically more likely to have closing cost documentation deficiencies in the same files. A single MREC audit finding can trigger a full-office investigation, so systematic internal review is not optional — it is a risk management imperative.

## Protecting Yourself When Customs Conflict With Lender Requirements

Mississippi's attorney-closing model creates a built-in checkpoint, but agents cannot rely on the closing attorney to catch every contractual deficiency. The attorney's role is to close the transaction per the contract's terms, not to renegotiate poorly drafted cost allocations on the agent's behalf.

When a lender's requirements conflict with the contractual cost allocation, the agent must initiate an amendment. This typically involves MAR Form 610, the General Amendment to Contract. The amendment must clearly state which cost is being reassigned, who will now pay it, and the dollar amount or calculation method. Vague language like "buyer and seller agree to work out closing costs" is unenforceable and will be rejected by any competent closing attorney.
