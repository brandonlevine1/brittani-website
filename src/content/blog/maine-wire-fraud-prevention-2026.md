# Maine Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Every Agent Must Do Before Closing

Wire fraud losses in real estate transactions exceeded $446 million nationally in 2023 according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Maine is not immune. Understanding Maine wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional knowledge for licensees—it is a practice standard that the Maine Real Estate Commission actively monitors during audits and complaint investigations.

The state has taken a layered approach to fraud prevention, combining statutory duties under Title 32 with industry-standard forms promoted by the Maine Association of REALTORS (MAR). Agents who treat wire fraud advisories as an afterthought are exposing themselves to disciplinary action, civil liability, and the catastrophic reputational damage that comes when a client loses their life savings three days before closing.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind Maine's Wire Fraud Rules

Maine's approach to wire fraud prevention sits within the broader consumer protection duties codified in 32 M.R.S. Section 13196, which establishes the grounds for license discipline including "incompetence" and "failure to disclose material information." The Maine Real Estate Commission interprets the failure to warn clients about wire fraud risks as a potential violation of these standards.

Unlike states that have passed standalone wire fraud notification statutes, Maine relies on the Commission's rulemaking authority under 32 M.R.S. Chapter 114 and its published guidance bulletins. The Commission's 2024 advisory bulletin explicitly stated that licensees must provide "written notification of wire fraud risks prior to any wire transfer of funds in connection with a real estate transaction." This language effectively creates a mandate even without a single dedicated statute.

The [Maine Real Estate Commission](https://www.maine.gov/pfr/professionallicensing/professions/real-estate/real-estate-commission) has enforcement authority to investigate complaints related to wire fraud failures and can impose sanctions ranging from reprimand to license revocation.

## Forms and Documentation Requirements

**MAR Form 905 (Wire Fraud Advisory) is the industry-standard disclosure form used by most Maine brokerages to satisfy the Commission's written notification requirement.** This form outlines common wire fraud schemes, instructs clients to verify wiring instructions by phone using a known number, and warns against relying solely on emailed instructions.

The form must be signed by the receiving party—whether buyer, seller, or both—and retained in the transaction file. Many brokerages require it at the same time as the Buyer Representation Agreement or Listing Agreement, ensuring the advisory reaches clients well before closing funds are in play.

In addition to Form 905, agents should be aware of MAR Form 801 (Transaction Summary) which includes a wire fraud acknowledgment checkbox, and MAR Form 106 (Agency Disclosure), which while not directly related to wire fraud, establishes the scope of duties an agent owes to their client. The interplay matters: if your Form 106 promises to "protect the client's financial interests," a court could interpret that broadly to include fraud warnings.

| Form | Purpose | When to Present | Who Signs |
|------|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| MAR Form 905 | Wire Fraud Advisory | At representation agreement or before wiring instructions shared | Buyer and/or Seller |
| MAR Form 801 | Transaction Summary with fraud acknowledgment | At offer acceptance | All parties |
| Brokerage-specific advisory | Custom wire fraud warning per office policy | Varies by brokerage | Buyer and/or Seller |
| Closing attorney advisory | Title company/attorney wire verification protocol | At or before closing | Buyer |

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance fall into three distinct categories, and none of them are trivial.

**License discipline is the most direct risk.** The Commission can initiate proceedings under 32 M.R.S. Section 13196 for incompetence or failure to exercise reasonable care. Sanctions range from a letter of guidance to fines up to $1,500 per violation, mandatory continuing education, probation, suspension, or full revocation. A 2025 consent agreement published by the Commission involved an agent who failed to provide wire fraud warnings; the agent received a $1,000 fine and 12 hours of mandatory education.

Civil liability is the financial nightmare scenario. When a buyer loses $300,000 to a fraudster who intercepted closing instructions, their attorney will look for every available defendant. If you never provided Form 905 or any equivalent advisory, you become an easy target for a negligence claim. Maine courts apply a reasonable-agent standard—what would a competent licensee have done?

Errors and omissions insurance carriers have increasingly added wire fraud advisory requirements to their coverage conditions. Miss the disclosure, and your carrier may deny coverage entirely, leaving you personally exposed to judgment. This mirrors trends discussed in the context of [wire instructions verification processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) that apply across state lines.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Maine

Five errors show up repeatedly in Commission complaints and brokerage audits. Any one of them can trigger liability.

**First, agents email wiring instructions directly to clients without encryption.** This is the single most dangerous practice in modern real estate. Business email compromise attacks specifically target real estate transactions because agents routinely send PDFs containing bank routing numbers over unsecured channels. The Commission's guidance is unambiguous: never transmit wiring instructions via standard email.

Second, agents present Form 905 at closing instead of at the beginning of the relationship. By the time a client is sitting at the closing table, they may have already received and acted on fraudulent wiring instructions days earlier. The advisory loses its protective value if delivered too late.

Third, agents fail to obtain signatures on the advisory or retain unsigned copies. A verbal warning carries no evidentiary weight when a complaint is filed. The Commission expects documentation in the transaction file.

Fourth, agents assume the closing attorney or title company handles all wire fraud warnings. While Maine's attorney-closing model means lawyers do provide their own advisories, this does not absolve the listing or buyer's agent of their independent duty. The Commission holds each licensee to their own standard of care.

Fifth, agents fail to update clients when closing dates shift. A delayed closing means new wiring instructions may be issued, creating a fresh opportunity for interception. Each time wire details change, the fraud advisory conversation needs to happen again.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokerage supervisors in Maine carry direct liability under 32 M.R.S. Section 13197 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. This means a broker who fails to establish wire fraud prevention policies can face independent discipline even if they personally did nothing wrong.

**Brokers should audit every closed transaction file for a signed wire fraud advisory within the first 90 days of retention.** The Commission's random audit program examines transaction files for exactly this documentation. A pattern of missing advisories suggests systemic supervisory failure rather than isolated agent error.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Action if Missing |
|------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Signed Form 905 or equivalent | Every transaction | Immediate agent counseling; remedial training |
| Email policy compliance (no unencrypted wire instructions) | Quarterly spot-check of agent email | Written warning; policy retraining |
| Timely delivery (advisory before wire instructions shared) | Every transaction | File correction; client re-acknowledgment |
| Updated advisory after closing date change | As applicable | Agent notification; supplemental form |
| Broker policy manual inclusion | Annual review | Policy update and agent distribution |

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy manual section dedicated to wire fraud prevention. The Commission looks favorably on brokerages that can demonstrate proactive training—it mitigates sanctions if an individual agent still makes a mistake. Consider quarterly email security training and annual updates to your wire fraud protocol based on evolving scam tactics.

The supervisory duty extends to teams. If you run a team structure, the team leader and the supervising broker both share accountability for ensuring every team member follows the protocol. This layered responsibility is similar to what we see in [team supervision frameworks](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) in other states, though Maine's attorney-closing model adds another compliance checkpoint.

## Technology and Verification Protocols

Maine's attorney-closing requirement means that funds typically flow through an attorney's IOLTA account, adding a layer of institutional verification. But this does not eliminate risk. Fraudsters increasingly impersonate attorneys, not just agents, sending spoofed emails that appear to come from the closing attorney's domain.

**The gold standard protocol requires clients to verify all wiring instructions by calling a phone number they obtained independently—not from the email containing the instructions.** Agents should coach clients on this during the Form 905 discussion and reinforce it verbally as closing approaches.

Multi-factor authentication on all email accounts used for transaction communication is no longer a best practice—it is a baseline expectation. The Commission's 2024 bulletin specifically referenced email security as part of a licensee's duty of care. Agents using personal Gmail accounts without two-factor authentication are creating unnecessary exposure.
