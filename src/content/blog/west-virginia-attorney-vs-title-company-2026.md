## The Question Every West Virginia Agent Must Answer

The debate over "West Virginia attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" is not an academic exercise. It directly affects how you structure every transaction, who you recommend to clients, and whether the deal you close today survives a challenge tomorrow. West Virginia is one of a handful of states where the answer is clear-cut — and getting it wrong exposes agents, brokers, and their clients to serious legal risk.

Unlike states where title companies independently run closings from start to finish, West Virginia law treats the preparation of closing documents as the practice of law. That single distinction reshapes who sits at the table, who signs off on title, and who bears liability if something goes sideways.

## What West Virginia Law Actually Says

**The statutory foundation begins with W. Va. Code Section 30-2-4, which defines the practice of law broadly enough to encompass deed preparation, title examination, and the drafting of deeds of trust.** The West Virginia State Bar has reinforced this through multiple advisory opinions dating back decades, and the position has not softened heading into 2026.

The West Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals has ruled in cases such as *State ex rel. Frieson v. Isner* that preparing legal instruments for real estate transactions constitutes the practice of law — regardless of whether the person doing so charges a fee or claims to act in a "ministerial" capacity. This means a title company employee who is not a licensed attorney cannot independently prepare deeds, deeds of trust, or render title opinions.

The West Virginia State Bar's Lawyer Disciplinary Board enforces these boundaries. When non-attorneys are found to have prepared closing documents, the Bar can seek injunctive relief through circuit court, and the transactions themselves may face validity challenges.

## Attorney vs. Title Company: How the Roles Break Down

Understanding the division of labor is critical for agents who want to set client expectations correctly and avoid steering parties toward non-compliant closings.

| Function | Attorney | Title Company |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| Title examination and opinion | Required — must be WV-licensed attorney | Cannot independently provide title opinions |
| Deed preparation | Required — attorney drafts or supervises | Cannot prepare without attorney oversight |
| Deed of trust preparation | Required | Cannot prepare independently |
| Settlement statement preparation | May prepare or supervise | May prepare under attorney direction |
| Disbursement of funds | May handle through IOLTA | May handle through escrow account |
| Title insurance issuance | Not required (but may be agent for insurer) | Can issue as authorized agent of underwriter |

The practical reality in West Virginia is that many closings involve both an attorney and a title company working in tandem. The attorney handles document preparation and title opinions while the title company manages escrow, insurance issuance, and administrative coordination. Neither replaces the other entirely.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

West Virginia real estate agents working under the West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC) use standardized forms for transactions. The WV-REPC (West Virginia Residential Real Estate Purchase Contract) governs most residential deals and includes provisions for designating the closing agent. Agents must ensure the contract accurately reflects who will conduct the closing — and that designation must involve an attorney.

**The WV-SPD (Seller's Property Disclosure Statement), required under W. Va. Code 36-6A-1 et seq., must be delivered and reviewed before closing but does not require attorney preparation.** However, any addenda modifying contract terms or resolving title defects found during examination should be prepared by the closing attorney to avoid unauthorized practice issues.

Agents should also be familiar with the HUD-1 replacement — the TRID Closing Disclosure (CD) — which under federal RESPA/TILA rules must be issued at least three business days before closing. In West Virginia, the closing attorney or the attorney's title company partner typically prepares this document. If your transaction involves a [seller disclosure issue](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026), the attorney is the appropriate party to advise on remedies.

The WVREC Form 16 (Agency Disclosure) must be presented at first substantive contact, and agents should review how [agency disclosure obligations](/blog/west-virginia-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) interact with the closing process — particularly when dual agency situations arise and both parties share a closing attorney.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make

Five errors recur across West Virginia transactions, and each carries distinct liability consequences.

**First: Recommending a title company as the sole closing entity.** Agents who tell buyers or sellers that "the title company handles everything" misrepresent how West Virginia closings work. If the title company operates without attorney oversight, the agent may face WVREC disciplinary action for facilitating the unauthorized practice of law. Under W. Va. Code 30-40-1 et seq. (the Real Estate License Act), agents can be sanctioned for conduct that violates state law in connection with a transaction.

Second: Failing to confirm the closing attorney's active bar status. An attorney whose license is suspended or inactive cannot lawfully conduct a closing. Agents should verify status through the West Virginia State Bar's online directory before the transaction progresses past the contract stage. A closing conducted by a non-active attorney is functionally equivalent to a closing with no attorney at all.

Third: Allowing parties to sign documents prepared by unlicensed staff without attorney review. In high-volume offices, paralegals or title company employees sometimes draft deeds using templates. This is permissible only when a supervising attorney reviews and approves each document before execution. Agents who witness signatures on unreviewed documents may become fact witnesses in subsequent malpractice or unauthorized practice claims.

Fourth: Ignoring the distinction between title insurance commitment and title opinion. A title insurance commitment from an underwriter does not substitute for a West Virginia attorney's title opinion. Lenders frequently require both, and agents who tell clients "the title insurance covers you" without ensuring a proper title examination may contribute to closings that fail lender requirements.

Fifth: Misallocating closing costs in the contract without specifying the attorney fee. The WV-REPC allows parties to negotiate who pays for the closing attorney, but agents sometimes leave this ambiguous. Ambiguity leads to last-minute disputes that delay or kill closings — a pattern explored in depth in [where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under W. Va. Code 30-40-19 for the conduct of their affiliated agents. This extends to ensuring agents do not facilitate unauthorized practice of law or misrepresent closing requirements to consumers.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Closing agent designation in contracts | Verify attorney name and bar number on WV-REPC | Every transaction |
| Active bar status of closing attorney | Cross-reference WV State Bar directory | At contract execution |
| Proper disclosure of closing costs | Confirm attorney fees specified and allocated | Every transaction |
| Agency disclosure timing | Confirm Form 16 delivered before substantive contact | Every transaction |
| Document preparation compliance | Verify attorney review of all legal instruments | Random audit quarterly |

Brokers who discover non-compliant closings in their files face several consequences. The WVREC can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under its disciplinary authority. Repeated violations can result in license suspension or revocation for both the agent and the supervising broker. Beyond regulatory action, brokers may face civil liability if a consumer suffers financial harm due to improperly conducted closings.

**A quarterly file audit is the minimum standard brokers should maintain.** The audit should confirm that every closed transaction file contains evidence of attorney involvement — either an engagement letter, a title opinion on attorney letterhead, or a settlement statement bearing the attorney's signature. Files lacking these documents represent unresolved compliance risk.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences extend beyond WVREC fines. When a closing is later challenged as involving the unauthorized practice of law, the deed or deed of trust may be voidable. This does not mean it is automatically void — but it creates a cloud on title that can prevent future sales or refinancing until cured by court order.

Agents involved in such transactions may be named in civil suits by buyers, sellers, or lenders who suffer financial harm. Professional liability (E&O) policies may not cover claims arising from an agent's knowing participation in unauthorized practice. Some E&O carriers specifically exclude coverage for conduct that violates state licensing laws.

The West Virginia State Bar can also seek restitution for affected consumers and referral fees paid to non-attorney closing entities. If an agent received compensation from a title company that was conducting closings without attorney oversight, that fee may be subject to disgorgement.

## 2026 Enforcement Landscape

The WVREC has signaled increased enforcement attention toward closing practices in its 2025-2026 strategic plan. The Commission's investigators have expanded their focus beyond advertising violations and unlicensed activity to include transaction file audits that examine closing agent compliance. Agents should expect that any WVREC investigation triggered by a consumer complaint will now include review of who prepared closing documents.

The [West Virginia Secretary of State's office](https://sos.wv.gov/) maintains recording requirements that indirectly reinforce attorney involvement — documents submitted for recording must meet specific formatting and content standards that unlicensed preparers frequently fail to satisfy. Rejected recordings delay title transfer and create liability for the responsible party.
