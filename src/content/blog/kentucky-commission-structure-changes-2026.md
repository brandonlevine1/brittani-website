## How Kentucky Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026 Reshape Every Transaction

The Kentucky commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have rewritten how agents earn, disclose, and collect compensation across the Commonwealth. Since the settlement's practice changes took full effect, the Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) has issued updated guidance aligning state enforcement with the new national standards. If you hold a Kentucky license and have not yet overhauled your forms and processes, you are operating with material regulatory exposure.

The core mandate is straightforward: buyer agent compensation may no longer be advertised through MLS systems, and all commission terms must be documented in written agreements before substantive brokerage services begin. Kentucky adopted these requirements through KREC's interpretive guidance under KRS Chapter 324, the state's real estate license law. The result is a compliance environment that punishes ambiguity and rewards documentation discipline.

## The Legal Framework Governing Kentucky Commission Disclosure

Kentucky's real estate licensing statute, [KRS 324.160](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/statutes/statute.aspx?id=53960), establishes the supervisory duties of principal brokers and the fiduciary obligations of licensees. Under KRS 324.160(4), a broker must ensure all licensees under their supervision comply with applicable regulations, which now include the NAR settlement's practice changes as incorporated into MLS participation rules statewide.

**KREC has clarified that any failure to provide written disclosure of compensation terms constitutes a potential violation of KRS 324.160 and KRS 324.990, the penalty statute.** Fines under KRS 324.990 can reach $1,000 per violation, and repeated infractions trigger license suspension or revocation proceedings. The commission's investigative authority under 201 KAR 11:121 means complaints from consumers or cooperating agents can trigger a formal audit.

Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) forms have been updated to reflect these changes. The KAR Form 210, the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement, now requires a specific compensation amount or method stated in clear, unambiguous terms. The KAR Form 105, the Listing Agreement, no longer contains a field for blanket offers of buyer agent compensation through the MLS.

## Written Buyer Agreements: Kentucky's Specific Requirements

Every agent representing a buyer must execute a written agreement before touring properties, attending open houses on the buyer's behalf, or submitting offers. This is not optional guidance — it is a condition of MLS participation in Kentucky and a standard KREC expects licensees to meet.

The agreement must include the amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, stated in a way the buyer can understand before signing. It must also disclose whether the buyer agent's compensation is negotiable and identify who is responsible for payment. Vague language like "customary commission" or "to be determined" does not satisfy the requirement.

| Requirement | Pre-Settlement Practice | Post-Settlement (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agreement timing | Optional or at offer stage | Before any touring or services |
| Compensation disclosure | Often verbal or assumed | Written, specific dollar or percentage |
| MLS compensation offers | Standard field in listing | Prohibited in MLS data |
| Seller contribution to buyer costs | Embedded in MLS co-op | Negotiated separately, outside MLS |
| Form used | Informal or KAR 210 (old version) | KAR Form 210 (revised 2025) |

Agents who show property without a signed agreement risk both KREC discipline and forfeiture of their right to collect compensation. Kentucky courts have historically enforced the statute of frauds strictly in commission disputes, meaning oral agreements for brokerage compensation are unenforceable under KRS 371.010.

## Common Mistakes Kentucky Agents Make Under the New Rules

The transition period has exposed recurring errors across Kentucky brokerages. These are not hypotheticals — they are patterns emerging from KREC complaint filings and MLS compliance audits conducted by Greater Louisville, Lexington-Bluegrass, and Northern Kentucky MLS systems.

**First, agents are using outdated KAR forms that still reference MLS-based compensation offers.** If your buyer agreement or listing contract was printed before the 2025 form revisions, it likely contains non-compliant language. Using a superseded form does not shield you from liability; it amplifies it.

Second, some buyer agents are signing agreements with compensation listed as "zero percent" and then verbally agreeing with sellers to a separate fee at closing. This workaround violates the spirit of the settlement and creates a written record that contradicts the actual transaction, which KREC views as potential fraud under KRS 324.160(2).

Third, listing agents are communicating buyer agent compensation through broker-to-broker emails or texts that reference MLS listing numbers. KREC and Kentucky MLS systems have stated that any compensation communication tied to or referencing an MLS listing's data constitutes a policy violation, even if the actual MLS field is blank.

Fourth, agents fail to update buyer agreements when terms change mid-transaction. If a seller agrees to contribute toward buyer agent compensation after the initial agreement is signed, the buyer agreement must be amended to reflect the change. Unamended agreements create discrepancies that trigger audit flags at closing.

Fifth, dual agents are neglecting to disclose how commission flows in [limited agency situations](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026). Kentucky's consent-to-dual-agency requirements under KRS 324.360 demand written disclosure of how compensation is allocated when one licensee represents both parties. The new rules make this even more critical, since compensation can no longer be assumed from the listing side.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in 2026

Principal brokers carry the enforcement burden. Under KRS 324.160, a broker who fails to supervise their agents' compliance with commission disclosure rules faces the same penalties as the offending agent — and potentially greater exposure, since the broker holds the license under which all associated agents operate.

**Brokers should audit every buyer representation agreement for three elements: timing of execution, specificity of compensation terms, and signature completeness.** A file missing a signed buyer agreement before the first showing is a ticking compliance bomb. KREC has indicated that post-hoc execution of buyer agreements — signing them after services have already been rendered — does not cure the violation.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agreement execution date | Must predate first showing or service | License violation, commission forfeiture |
| Compensation specificity | Dollar amount or clear percentage | Unenforceability under statute of frauds |
| MLS listing compensation fields | Must be blank/removed | MLS fine, KREC complaint |
| Closing statement reconciliation | Commission paid must match agreements | Fraud allegation, civil liability |
| Dual agency compensation disclosure | Written consent with allocation detail | KRS 324.360 violation |
| Form version | KAR 210 (rev. 2025) or later | Non-compliant documentation |

Brokers should also reconcile closing statements against signed agreements. If the HUD-1 or closing disclosure shows a buyer agent commission that does not match the executed buyer agreement, that discrepancy is audit-ready evidence of non-compliance. Transaction coordinators and closing departments must be trained to flag mismatches before funding.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for non-compliance operate on three levels: regulatory, civil, and transactional. Regulatory penalties come through KREC under KRS 324.990, which authorizes fines, mandatory education, probation, suspension, and revocation. A single substantiated complaint can trigger a formal hearing before the commission.

Civil liability arises when a buyer or seller claims they were not properly informed of commission terms and suffered financial harm. Kentucky courts apply the standard of reasonable reliance — if a consumer reasonably relied on an agent's representations (or omissions) about compensation, the agent faces damages exposure. The written agreement requirement is your shield, but only if the agreement is complete, signed, and accurate.

Transactional risk means the deal itself can unravel. A buyer who discovers post-closing that their agent's compensation was not properly disclosed can pursue rescission or damages. Title insurance may not cover claims arising from agent misconduct, leaving the brokerage directly exposed. [Missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on documentation only compounds the problem when disputes arise months after closing.

## How Compensation Negotiations Actually Work Now

Sellers in Kentucky can still contribute toward a buyer's agent costs. The mechanism simply moved off-MLS. A listing agent may communicate to a buyer's agent that the seller is willing to pay a portion of buyer-side compensation, but this communication must happen outside MLS fields and cannot be tied to MLS listing data as a blanket offer.

**In practice, buyer agents are now negotiating compensation as part of the offer itself.** The purchase contract — KAR Form 200 — can include a provision requesting that the seller pay a specified amount toward the buyer agent's fee. This makes compensation a negotiated deal term rather than a pre-set assumption.

The shift requires buyer agents to discuss compensation with their clients before writing offers, which changes the dynamic of the initial consultation. Agents who treat the buyer agreement as a formality rather than a genuine negotiation are finding that educated buyers push back. Transparency is now the price of admission, not a differentiator.
