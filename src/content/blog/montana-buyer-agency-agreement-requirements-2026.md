## Why Montana Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026 Matter Now

The practice of buyer representation in Montana has shifted dramatically since the NAR settlement changes took effect nationwide. Montana buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 are no longer optional best practices—they are enforceable obligations that carry real consequences for agents who ignore them. The Montana Board of Realty Regulation has issued updated guidance that aligns state rules with the new national framework while preserving Montana-specific statutory requirements under Title 37, Chapter 51 of the Montana Code Annotated.

Every licensed agent in the state must secure a signed, written buyer representation agreement before engaging in substantive brokerage services for a prospective purchaser. This means before you open a lockbox, before you pull comparable sales at a buyer's request, and before you attend an open house in an agency capacity. The days of informal handshake representation are over.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Buyer Representation

Montana's real estate licensing laws are codified in [MCA Title 37, Chapter 51](https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0370/chapter_0510/parts_index.html), with administrative rules under ARM 24.210. The Board of Realty Regulation, housed within the Montana Department of Labor and Industry, serves as the enforcement body. Section 37-51-313, MCA specifically addresses agency relationships and requires written disclosure of the nature of the relationship between a licensee and any party to a transaction.

**The critical update for 2026 is that written buyer agreements must now include specific compensation disclosures.** The agreement must state, in clear terms, the amount or method of calculating the buyer agent's compensation. It cannot contain open-ended language that allows the agent to receive compensation exceeding what was agreed upon without further written consent.

ARM 24.210.641 further specifies that all agency agreements must identify the parties, describe the scope of services, establish the duration of the agreement, and disclose any potential dual agency scenarios. Failure to meet any one of these elements renders the agreement deficient—and potentially unenforceable in a dispute.

## Specific Forms Montana Agents Must Use

The Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) publishes standardized forms that satisfy the statutory and regulatory requirements. The two primary documents agents will work with are:

| Form | Name | Use Case | Key Feature |
|------|------|----------|-------------|
| MAR Form 112 | Exclusive Buyer Broker Agreement | Single-agent exclusive representation | Buyer commits to one agent/brokerage for a defined period |
| MAR Form 113 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Broker Agreement | Non-exclusive representation | Buyer may work with multiple agents simultaneously |
| MAR Form 102 | Agency Disclosure and Election | Initial agency relationship disclosure | Must be presented at first substantive contact |
| MAR Form 114 | Compensation Agreement Amendment | Modifying agreed compensation mid-engagement | Required when terms change after initial signing |

Form 102, the Agency Disclosure and Election form, is not the same as the buyer agency agreement, but it must be presented alongside it. Many agents conflate these two documents. Form 102 discloses what types of agency exist in Montana; Form 112 or 113 actually establishes the relationship and its terms.

Agents who belong to local boards outside of MAR should confirm their brokerage-approved forms contain identical substantive provisions. Custom forms drafted by brokerages are permissible under Montana law, but they must meet every element prescribed by ARM 24.210.641.

## Compensation Disclosure Rules and the NAR Settlement Impact

Montana did not adopt a separate state-level rule mirroring the NAR settlement verbatim, but the Board of Realty Regulation issued Interpretive Guidance Bulletin 2025-03, which clarified that Montana licensees participating in any MLS must comply with both MLS-specific rules and state law simultaneously. Since Montana's MLSs have removed offers of buyer agent compensation from listings, the buyer agreement is now the sole document establishing how and how much the buyer's agent gets paid.

**The agreement must state compensation as a specific dollar amount or a clear percentage—never as "whatever the listing broker offers."** This prohibition exists because the buyer needs to understand, before touring begins, what their financial obligation might be. If a seller later agrees to pay the buyer agent's fee, the agreement should address how that affects the buyer's obligation—typically through a credit or offset provision.

This structure mirrors what other states have adopted, though Montana's [approach differs from Wisconsin's framework](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) in several procedural details. Wisconsin requires compensation disclosure at a slightly different stage of the transaction, while Montana ties it firmly to the moment before substantive brokerage services begin.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Buyer Agreements in Montana

Errors in this area are not hypothetical. The Board of Realty Regulation's disciplinary records from 2024 and 2025 show a pattern of violations that agents repeat cycle after cycle. Here are the five most frequent failures.

**First, agents present the agreement too late.** The most common violation is showing property—sometimes multiple properties over several weeks—before asking the buyer to sign. Under current rules, "substantive brokerage services" includes pulling property information at a buyer's specific request, scheduling showings, and providing pricing opinions. If you have done any of these things without a signed agreement, you are already out of compliance.

Second, agents leave the compensation field blank or insert vague language like "to be determined." The Board has stated in Bulletin 2025-03 that an incomplete compensation section renders the entire agreement non-compliant. A buyer could later argue they never agreed to pay you, and a court would likely side with them.

Third, agents fail to include a definite termination date. Montana law does not set a maximum duration, but ARM 24.210.641 requires a "stated expiration or termination event." Writing "until buyer purchases a home" without a backstop date has been flagged in multiple audits as deficient. Best practice is a fixed calendar date—typically 90 to 180 days.

Fourth, agents neglect to update the agreement when circumstances change. If a buyer originally agreed to a 2.5% commission and the agent later wants to show a FSBO where no seller-paid compensation exists, the buyer must sign an amendment (Form 114) acknowledging the potential cost difference before that showing occurs.

Fifth, agents operating on teams fail to identify which licensee within the brokerage holds the agency relationship. Montana requires the individual licensee's name on the agreement, not just the brokerage name. If a team member shows property on behalf of a colleague, that substitute arrangement must be documented.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers carry independent liability for their agents' compliance failures under MCA 37-51-321. The Board has made clear that ignorance of an agent's practices is not a defense. Brokers must establish and enforce written policies addressing buyer representation, and they must audit files regularly.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|---------------|----------|
| Agreement timing | Date of agreement vs. date of first showing activity | Showing confirmations pre-dating signed agreement |
| Compensation clarity | Specific dollar or percentage stated | Blank fields, "TBD," or "per MLS" language |
| Termination date | Fixed calendar date present | Open-ended or missing expiration |
| Signature completeness | All parties and licensee signed and dated | Missing buyer signature, electronic signature without proper E-SIGN compliance |
| Form version | Current year MAR form or approved equivalent | Outdated forms from pre-2024 lacking compensation disclosure sections |

Brokers should implement a policy requiring agents to upload signed buyer agreements to the transaction management system before any showing appointment is confirmed. This creates a timestamped record proving compliance. Firms still relying on paper files with no date-stamping mechanism are exposing themselves to audit failures.

Quarterly file reviews are the minimum standard most real estate attorneys recommend for Montana brokerages. During these reviews, pull a random sample of active buyer files and verify each contains a properly executed agreement that predates any substantive activity. Document the review itself—the Board looks favorably on brokerages that can demonstrate proactive compliance efforts when isolated violations occur.

The consequences of broker-level failure are severe. The Board can suspend or revoke a broker's license, impose fines up to $1,000 per violation under MCA 37-51-321, and require additional continuing education. Civil liability compounds these penalties if a buyer brings a claim alleging unauthorized representation or undisclosed fees.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance for Individual Agents

An agent operating without a valid buyer agreement faces exposure on multiple fronts. Regulatory penalties from the Board include formal reprimand, mandatory education, fines, license suspension, or revocation. These actions become part of the agent's public disciplinary record and are searchable by consumers.

On the civil side, an agent without a signed agreement may be unable to enforce any claim for compensation. Montana courts have historically held that oral agreements for real estate brokerage services are unenforceable under the statute of frauds. If a buyer walks away or purchases directly from a seller, the agent has no written basis to claim a fee. This is not theoretical—it happens routinely in cases involving [missed deadlines and procedural gaps](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Beyond individual transactions, an agent's errors and omissions insurance may deny coverage for claims arising from services performed without a written agreement. Most E&O policies contain exclusions for violations of licensing law. If the Board determines you acted without proper authorization, your insurer may decline to defend you in a subsequent civil suit.

## How Montana Differs From Neighboring States

Montana shares borders with states that have adopted slightly different approaches to buyer representation requirements. Understanding these distinctions matters for agents working in border markets or holding licenses in multiple jurisdictions.

Wyoming, for example, has its own [agency disclosure requirements](/blog/wyoming-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) that differ in timing and form from Montana's. Agents licensed in both states cannot assume that compliance in one jurisdiction satisfies the other. Each state's Board evaluates compliance independently based on where the property is located.

The structural requirement is consistent nationwide post-settlement: written agreements before touring, clear compensation terms, defined duration. But the specific forms, regulatory bodies, and enforcement mechanisms vary. Montana agents must follow Montana rules regardless of what they have learned or practiced in other states.
