## Understanding Minnesota Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Minnesota earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real teeth, and agents who treat trust fund handling as an afterthought risk their license, their broker's reputation, and personal financial exposure. The Minnesota Department of Commerce enforces these rules under Minnesota Statutes Chapter 82, and violations trigger consequences that range from fines to license revocation. This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, deadlines, and audit requirements that govern every dollar of earnest money moving through a Minnesota transaction this year.

Earnest money is not a fee or a commission—it is the buyer's good-faith deposit that signals commitment to a purchase agreement. In Minnesota, it becomes trust property the moment the agent accepts it, and every second it sits undeposited creates potential liability. Agents who have handled transactions in states with looser rules often underestimate how strictly Minnesota regulates the custody chain.

## The Statutory Framework: Chapter 82 and Rule 2805

**Minnesota Statutes Section 82.24 is the anchor statute governing earnest money and trust accounts.** It mandates that all money received by a licensee on behalf of others must be deposited into the broker's trust account within three business days of receipt. The only exception is when the purchase agreement expressly states that the deposit will be held by a different party, such as a title company.

Minnesota Administrative Rules Chapter 2805 further details trust account management. Rule 2805.1400 specifies that brokers must maintain records of all trust account transactions for at least six years. It also requires that trust accounts be maintained in a federally insured depository institution located in Minnesota.

> "All trust funds received by a real estate broker must be deposited in a trust account maintained by the broker in a federally insured financial institution in this state." — Minn. Stat. § 82.24, subd. 1

The distinction between "business days" and "calendar days" matters. Weekends and state holidays do not count toward the three-day deposit window. An agent who receives earnest money on a Thursday afternoon has until the following Tuesday—assuming no holidays—to deliver those funds to the broker for deposit.

## Forms That Govern Earnest Money in Minnesota

Minnesota agents primarily use forms published by Minnesota Realtors (MNR) and the Minneapolis Area Realtors (MAR). The most relevant forms for earnest money handling include the MNR Residential Purchase Agreement, which contains the earnest money terms in its opening paragraphs, and the MAR equivalent used in metro-area transactions.

| Form | Publisher | Earnest Money Relevance |
|------|-----------|------------------------|
| Purchase Agreement (PA) | MNR / MAR | Specifies amount, holder, and deposit deadline |
| Cancellation Agreement | MNR / MAR | Governs release of earnest money upon mutual cancellation |
| Addendum to Purchase Agreement | MNR / MAR | Modifies earnest money terms mid-transaction |
| Trust Account Receipt | Broker-specific | Confirms deposit date and amount for compliance records |
| Inspection Response (IR) | MNR / MAR | Can trigger cancellation rights that affect earnest money disposition |

The Purchase Agreement form requires agents to fill in who holds the earnest money, the exact dollar amount, and the form of payment. Leaving the "held by" field blank or ambiguous is a compliance error that invites disputes later. Agents should also note that personal checks, cashier's checks, and wire transfers each create different deposit timing considerations under the three-business-day rule.

When deals fall apart, the Cancellation Agreement is the document that authorizes release. Without it—or a court order—neither party can unilaterally instruct the broker to disburse funds. This is where many agents stumble, particularly when one party demands immediate return of earnest money and the other side has gone silent. The statutes are clear: [mutual written consent or a court order is required](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Liability for Non-Compliance: What Agents Actually Face

**Failing to comply with Minnesota's earnest money statutes exposes agents to a layered set of consequences.** The Minnesota Department of Commerce has authority under Minn. Stat. § 82.197 to impose disciplinary actions including license suspension, revocation, and civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation. Repeated offenses or willful mishandling can trigger criminal referral for theft or conversion.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is lost due to commingling or late deposit can sue both the agent and the broker. Errors and omissions insurance may or may not cover trust account violations depending on policy language—most E&O policies exclude dishonest acts, and a regulator's finding of willful non-compliance can void coverage.

The broker's license is also on the line. Under Minnesota's supervision requirements, the designated broker is ultimately responsible for every trust account transaction conducted under their license. A single agent's carelessness can result in a full trust account audit, operational disruption, and reputational harm across the entire brokerage. For more context on how supervision requirements create broker liability, see how [team supervision structures work in other states](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026).

Deal cancellation is another real-world consequence. When earnest money handling errors create uncertainty about who holds funds or whether funds were properly deposited, buyers lose confidence and invoke cancellation contingencies. Sellers lose time on market. Both sides blame the agent.

## Common Mistakes Minnesota Agents Make With Earnest Money

Five specific errors account for the majority of earnest money complaints filed with the Minnesota Department of Commerce. Each one is preventable with proper systems and awareness.

**First, holding checks instead of delivering them to the broker.** Some agents pocket earnest money checks in their car console or desk drawer, intending to deliver them "soon." The three-business-day clock starts when the agent receives the check, not when the agent gets around to dropping it off at the office. Agents who work remotely or across a large geographic territory are particularly prone to this violation.

Second, depositing earnest money into a personal or operating account. This is commingling, and it is one of the most serious trust account violations under Minnesota law. Even a momentary deposit into the wrong account—followed by an immediate transfer to the trust account—constitutes a violation. The Department of Commerce treats commingling as a per se disciplinary offense.

Third, releasing earnest money without proper written authorization from both parties. When a deal collapses and the buyer calls demanding their money back, some agents instruct the broker to cut a check immediately. Without the seller's written consent via the Cancellation Agreement—or a court order—this release is unauthorized and can expose the agent and broker to liability from the seller.

Fourth, failing to note the receipt date on trust account records. Minnesota requires brokers to maintain records showing when funds were received and when they were deposited. If the agent does not communicate the exact receipt date to the broker's office, the trust account ledger becomes inaccurate and the brokerage becomes non-compliant with [Rule 2805](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2805/) record-keeping requirements.

Fifth, using vague language in the Purchase Agreement about who holds the deposit. Writing "to be held in escrow" without naming the specific entity creates ambiguity that becomes a legal problem if the deal disputes arise. The form provides a clear field for this information—agents must fill it in completely.

## Minnesota Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026: What Brokers Must Audit

Brokers carry the heaviest compliance burden in Minnesota's earnest money framework. The designated broker must maintain oversight of every trust account transaction, and the Department of Commerce can audit trust accounts at any time without prior notice.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly, at minimum | Minn. Admin. Rule 2805.1400 |
| Deposit timing verification | Every transaction | Minn. Stat. § 82.24 |
| Cancellation/release documentation | Every disbursement | Minn. Stat. § 82.24, subd. 5 |
| Record retention (6-year minimum) | Ongoing | Minn. Admin. Rule 2805.1400 |
| Agent receipt-date reporting | Every transaction | Broker policy / Rule 2805 |

**Monthly reconciliation is not optional—it is a regulatory requirement.** The broker or their designated trust account manager must reconcile the trust account ledger against the bank statement every month. Discrepancies must be investigated and resolved immediately. A pattern of unresolved discrepancies is a red flag that the Department of Commerce treats as evidence of systemic non-compliance.

Brokers should also enforce a written office policy requiring agents to submit earnest money—along with a documented receipt date—within 24 hours of receipt, giving the brokerage a buffer before the three-day statutory deadline. This internal policy creates a safety margin and demonstrates good-faith compliance if the Department ever questions timing.

Disbursement authorization is another area brokers must enforce rigidly. No earnest money should leave the trust account without a signed Cancellation Agreement, a closing statement directing disbursement, or a court order. Brokers who allow verbal authorizations or single-party requests are inviting regulatory action. The [risks of relying on memory instead of documented processes](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) apply doubly when trust funds are involved.

## Dispute Resolution and Interpleader Actions

When buyer and seller disagree about who is entitled to earnest money and neither will sign a Cancellation Agreement, the broker holding funds faces a legal standstill. Minnesota law does not require the broker to make a determination about who deserves the money. Instead, the broker's obligation is to hold the funds safely until receiving mutual written consent or a court order.

If the standoff persists, the broker can file an interpleader action in district court. This legal mechanism allows the broker to deposit the disputed funds with the court and ask a judge to determine entitlement. The broker is then released from further liability regarding those funds. Filing an interpleader costs money and time, but it is the legally correct path when parties are deadlocked.

Brokers should not wait indefinitely before pursuing interpleader. While Minnesota statutes do not specify a maximum holding period before filing, holding disputed funds for months without resolution exposes the brokerage to claims from both sides and increases the administrative burden of maintaining accurate trust account records.

## Practical Steps for 2026 Compliance

Agents entering 2026 should confirm three things with their broker before their next transaction closes. First, verify that the brokerage's trust account procedures include a written policy on receipt-date documentation. Second, confirm that the Cancellation Agreement form being used is the current version published by MNR or MAR. Third, confirm that the broker has a defined process for handling disputed funds, including criteria for when interpleader will be filed.
