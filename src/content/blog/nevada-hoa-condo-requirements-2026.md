## Understanding Nevada HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

More than half of all residential sales in Clark County involve a homeowners association. That statistic alone explains why understanding **Nevada HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026** is not optional for any agent working the Las Vegas metro, Reno, or even rural planned communities. The governing statutes carry real teeth—missed deadlines can blow up a deal, expose your brokerage to liability claims, and trigger complaints to the Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED).

Nevada's HOA disclosure framework is anchored primarily in NRS Chapter 116 (the Common-Interest Ownership Act) and, for condominiums specifically, NRS 116B. These statutes dictate what documents sellers must produce, what buyers can demand, and how much time each party gets to act. The regulatory body overseeing HOA compliance is the Office of the Ombudsman for Owners in Common-Interest Communities, housed within the Nevada Real Estate Division.

Agents frequently treat HOA paperwork as "just another form to chase," but the consequences of a casual approach can be severe. This post breaks down the specific obligations, forms, deadlines, and enforcement mechanisms you need to internalize before your next listing appointment.

## The Resale Package: What NRS 116.4109 Actually Requires

**NRS 116.4109 is the single most important statute for agents representing sellers in HOA communities.** It mandates that the seller furnish a resale package to the buyer before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed—or as soon as practicable thereafter. The package must contain a battery of information about the association's financial health, governance, and restrictions.

Here is what the resale package must include under current statute: the declaration, bylaws, and rules of the association; a statement of the monthly assessment and any special assessments; the association's current operating budget and most recent financial statement; a statement of any pending litigation involving the HOA; information about reserve fund balances and any anticipated shortfalls; and the status of the seller's account (including outstanding balances or violations). The association itself is typically the entity that compiles this package at the seller's request.

Most HOAs in Nevada use a third-party vendor—companies like HOA Docs, Homewise, or Super Priority Lien Services—to produce the package. Fees range from $150 to $400 depending on turnaround time. Standard production is 10 business days, though rush fees can cut that to three.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and the Five-Day Window

Once the buyer receives the resale package, NRS 116.4109 grants a five-calendar-day right of cancellation. During this window, the buyer may rescind the purchase agreement for any reason—or no reason at all—without forfeiting their earnest money deposit. The clock does not start until the buyer actually receives the package.

This is where agents on both sides need to be meticulous about documentation. If the resale package is handed off without a signed acknowledgment of receipt, proving delivery becomes nearly impossible in a dispute. The widely used Greater Las Vegas Association of REALTORS (GLVAR) form—the "Receipt of Resale Package and Cancellation Rights" acknowledgment—exists precisely for this purpose.

**If the resale package is never delivered, the buyer's cancellation right never expires.** That means a buyer could theoretically walk away the day before closing, demand their deposit back, and have full statutory backing. Sellers' agents who fail to order the package early in the listing period are gambling with their client's deal.

## Key Forms and Documents Nevada Agents Must Track

The forms ecosystem in Nevada HOA transactions involves both NRED-mandated documents and association-specific forms. Below is a comparison of the primary documents and who bears responsibility for each.

| Document / Form | Responsible Party | Typical Source | Deadline Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resale Package (NRS 116.4109) | Seller | HOA or third-party vendor | Must be delivered to buyer before or at contract execution |
| Receipt and Cancellation Acknowledgment | Buyer (signs) | GLVAR or agent-prepared | Upon receipt of resale package |
| HOA Demand Statement / Payoff | Seller | HOA management company | Ordered during escrow, typically 10 days before closing |
| Lien Estoppel Letter | Title company (requests) | HOA | Required to confirm assessment status for clear title |
| Seller's Real Property Disclosure (SRPD) | Seller | NRED Form 547 | Within specified contract timeframe |

The Seller's Real Property Disclosure (Form 547) is not an HOA-specific document, but it contains questions about association membership, monthly dues, and known violations. Agents who help sellers complete Form 547 must ensure HOA-related sections are answered accurately—leaving them blank invites a post-closing claim.

For condominium-specific transactions, NRS 116B adds requirements around common-element disclosures, insurance coverage held by the association, and any right of first refusal the association may hold. Agents listing condo units should confirm whether the association exercises a right of first refusal, as this can delay or even prevent closing if not addressed early.

## Nevada HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Even experienced agents trip over HOA disclosure requirements. Here are five specific errors that show up repeatedly in NRED complaints and litigation filings.

**First, ordering the resale package too late.** Many listing agents wait until the property goes under contract to request the package from the HOA. With standard turnaround at 10 business days—and some associations taking longer—this delay compresses the buyer's review period against inspection and appraisal deadlines. Best practice is ordering the package at the time of listing or within 48 hours of receiving an offer.

Second, failing to obtain a signed acknowledgment of receipt from the buyer. Without this document, the seller has no proof that the five-day cancellation window ever started. If a dispute arises, the buyer's attorney will argue the window never ran, and courts have sided with buyers on this issue repeatedly.

Third, confusing the resale package with the HOA demand statement. These are two entirely different documents serving different purposes. The resale package is a disclosure to the buyer about the association's condition. The demand statement is a payoff figure showing what the seller owes the HOA at closing. Agents who conflate them often fail to order one or the other, causing last-minute scrambles that [delay closings and break deals](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Fourth, ignoring the super-priority lien issue. Under NRS 116.3116, an HOA's lien for unpaid assessments can take priority over even a first deed of trust—up to nine months of assessments. Agents representing buyers must verify that the title company has obtained a lien estoppel letter confirming the seller's account is current. Failure to catch an outstanding super-priority lien can result in the buyer inheriting the debt.

Fifth, not disclosing known HOA rule violations or pending enforcement actions against the property. If the seller has received violation notices—for unapproved modifications, landscaping issues, or short-term rental infractions—these must appear in the disclosure. Agents who coach sellers to omit this information expose themselves and their brokerage to fraud claims under NRS 645.252.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerage-level compliance is not a suggestion in Nevada—it is a regulatory expectation. NRS 645.252 holds brokers responsible for the acts of their licensees, and NRED has shown willingness to discipline supervising brokers for systematic disclosure failures. Here is what managing brokers should be auditing in every HOA transaction file.

| Audit Item | What to Verify | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Resale package in file | Package present, dated, complete | Missing or dated after buyer cancellation window closed |
| Buyer acknowledgment | Signed, dated, matches delivery date | Unsigned or missing entirely |
| HOA demand statement | Ordered, received, amounts reconcile with closing statement | Not ordered until day before closing |
| Form 547 HOA sections | All HOA-related fields completed | Blank or marked "unknown" without explanation |
| Super-priority lien clearance | Estoppel letter in title file | No verification of assessment status |

**Brokers should build a transaction checklist that flags HOA-related milestones at specific intervals: listing intake, contract acceptance, mid-escrow, and pre-closing.** Waiting until a file review at close of escrow is too late to catch errors that could have been corrected weeks earlier. Many brokerages now [automate deadline tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) to reduce reliance on agent memory.

Training is equally important. Quarterly compliance meetings should include HOA disclosure scenarios, particularly for newer agents who may not understand the distinction between NRS 116 (planned communities) and NRS 116B (condominiums). The Office of the Ombudsman publishes educational materials that brokers can incorporate into their training rotation.

## Liability, Fines, and Deal Cancellation: The Real Costs of Non-Compliance

NRED has authority under NRS 645.633 to discipline licensees who fail to make required disclosures or who engage in conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness. Penalties range from letters of reprimand and mandatory continuing education to license suspension, revocation, and fines up to $10,000 per violation.

Beyond regulatory action, civil liability looms large. A buyer who discovers undisclosed HOA issues post-closing—a special assessment that was pending, litigation that was omitted, or a violation that transfers with the property—can sue the seller and the listing agent for damages. Nevada courts have awarded rescission of sale, compensatory damages, and attorney fees in cases where disclosure obligations under NRS 116.4109 were not met.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. Because the buyer's five-day cancellation right is absolute and non-waivable during the statutory window, an agent who delivers bad news in the resale package without preparing the seller for potential fallout is setting up a blown transaction. Agents should preview the resale package contents before delivering them to the buyer—not to suppress information, but to prepare their client for questions and to address any issues proactively.

> "The right of a purchaser to cancel a purchase agreement pursuant to this section may not be waived." — NRS 116.4109(4)

## Practical Workflow for Agents in HOA Transactions

The most effective agents treat HOA disclosures as a workflow rather than a single task. At listing intake, order the resale package and review Form 547 with the seller, paying close attention to assessment amounts, known violations, and pending special assessments. At contract acceptance, confirm the package is ready for delivery and prepare the buyer acknowledgment form. Within 48 hours, deliver the package and secure the signed receipt.

Mid-escrow, order the HOA demand statement and coordinate with the title company on the lien estoppel letter. Verify that the amounts in the demand statement match what appears in the closing disclosure. If discrepancies exist, resolve them immediately—not the morning of signing.

For agents handling volume, [systematizing this process with checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rather than relying on memory is what separates clean files from audit nightmares. The repetitive nature of HOA paperwork makes it ideal for automation—tracking deadlines, triggering reminders, and flagging missing documents before they become problems.
