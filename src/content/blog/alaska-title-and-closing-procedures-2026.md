Alaska's title and closing process operates under a set of rules that differ from most Lower 48 states in significant ways. If you are licensed in the state and handling transactions in 2026, understanding **Alaska title & closing procedures 2026** updates is not optional — it is the baseline for keeping your deals intact and your license clean. This article breaks down the statutory framework, the exact forms you should be using, the mistakes that get agents into trouble, and the audit responsibilities brokers carry.

## How Alaska Structures the Closing Process

Alaska is an escrow-closing state. Unlike attorney states such as those on the East Coast, residential closings here are handled primarily by title and escrow companies operating under the authority of the Alaska Division of Insurance, which regulates title insurers under Alaska Statute 21.66. The Division of Insurance reviews rate filings and ensures title companies comply with statutory requirements for reserves and claims handling.

The Alaska Real Estate Commission, housed within the Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development, governs agent conduct during the transaction. Commission regulations under 12 AAC 64 spell out duties related to trust accounts, document handling, and disclosure obligations that touch every phase of the closing timeline. Agents who treat the closing as "the title company's problem" misunderstand where their own liability begins and ends.

Recording of deeds and deeds of trust happens through the Alaska Department of Natural Resources, Recorder's Office — not a county recorder, because Alaska has boroughs instead of counties, and much of the state is in the Unorganized Borough with no local recording office at all. This distinction matters for agents working deals outside Anchorage, Fairbanks, or Juneau, because recording delays in remote areas can push closing timelines by weeks.

## Key Forms Alaska Agents Must Know in 2026

The Alaska Association of Realtors (AAR) publishes the standard transaction forms used by most residential agents statewide. The core document is the **AAR Purchase and Sale Agreement**, which was last revised to incorporate updated financing contingency language and property condition disclosure timelines. Agents should confirm they are working from the most current version, as using outdated forms can expose both the agent and the brokerage to liability.

Several additional AAR forms play direct roles in the title and closing phase. The **Addendum to Purchase and Sale Agreement** is used when modifying closing dates, title cure periods, or cost allocations. The **Counter Offer** form governs changes to original terms, including who pays for title insurance and which title company will handle escrow. The **Property Condition Disclosure Statement** — required under AS 34.70.010 — must be delivered to the buyer before closing, and its absence is one of the most common reasons deals collapse during the title review period.

| Form | Purpose | Phase of Transaction |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| AAR Purchase and Sale Agreement | Primary contract | Offer through closing |
| Property Condition Disclosure (AS 34.70) | Seller's mandatory disclosure | Pre-closing, typically within 10 days of acceptance |
| Addendum to Purchase and Sale Agreement | Modifies contract terms | Any time before closing |
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (federal) | Required for pre-1978 homes | With or before purchase agreement |
| Counter Offer | Adjusts offer terms | Negotiation phase |

Failing to provide the Property Condition Disclosure Statement can trigger rescission rights for the buyer under AS 34.70.040, and the Alaska Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action against the listing agent's license. The statute gives the buyer the right to rescind the agreement if the disclosure was never delivered, which means a forgotten form can unwind a deal even after the buyer has moved in.

## Title Insurance and the Search Process in Alaska

**Title insurance in Alaska is issued by a small number of underwriters, and agents should understand the difference between an owner's policy and a lender's policy.** The lender's policy is almost always required by the mortgage company and protects only the lender's interest. The owner's policy protects the buyer and is optional, though strongly recommended — and the party responsible for paying for each policy is a negotiation point in the purchase agreement.

Title searches in Alaska involve pulling records from the DNR Recorder's Office, and in organized boroughs, from local recording districts. Because Alaska was a territory until 1959 and because Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) conveyances created unique chains of title, agents working with properties that touch former native allotment land or ANCSA corporation land should expect extended search timelines and potential exceptions on the preliminary title commitment.

The preliminary title commitment — sometimes called a title binder — is the document that outlines what the title company will and will not insure. Agents need to review Schedule B exceptions carefully with their clients. Missing an easement, a recorded lien, or a prior deed of trust that was never properly reconveyed is the kind of issue that surfaces in Schedule B and requires action before closing can proceed. ## Alaska Title & Closing Procedures 2026: Common Agent Mistakes

Experienced agents still make errors during the title and closing phase that result in delayed closings, dead deals, and disciplinary complaints. Here are five of the most frequent mistakes the Commission and title officers report.

**First, agents fail to calendar the title objection deadline.** The AAR Purchase and Sale Agreement specifies a period during which the buyer must raise title objections. If the buyer's agent misses the deadline, the buyer may be deemed to have accepted the title as-is, including any defects found in the preliminary commitment. This is a [classic example of why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) — the obligation exists in the contract, but no one tracked it.

Second, agents do not verify wire instructions independently before closing. Wire fraud targeting real estate transactions has increased sharply in Alaska, just as it has nationally. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has flagged real estate transactions as a top target for business email compromise scams. Agents who forward wire instructions from an email without verifying directly with the title company by phone are creating liability for themselves and their brokerage.

Third, agents forget that Alaska requires the seller to provide the Property Condition Disclosure even when selling property "as-is." The AS 34.70 disclosure obligation is independent of the property's condition or the terms of sale. An as-is sale does not waive the disclosure requirement, and agents who advise sellers otherwise are providing incorrect legal guidance.

Fourth, listing agents sometimes fail to confirm that the seller has clear authority to convey the property. Properties held in trusts, LLCs, or by prior probate actions require additional documentation that must be in the title company's hands well before closing. Waiting until the week of closing to discover the seller's trust is improperly formed is a preventable disaster.

Fifth, agents neglect to confirm the recording fee and transfer tax situation before drafting the settlement statement. Alaska does not impose a state transfer tax on real estate conveyances, but recording fees through the DNR Recorder's Office still apply, and certain boroughs have their own fee schedules. Misquoting closing costs to a client because you assumed a fee structure from another state is a fast way to lose trust and invite a complaint.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Alaska carry supervisory responsibility under 12 AAC 64.130, which requires them to exercise reasonable supervision over all licensed agents operating under their authority. When it comes to title and closing procedures, that supervision obligation translates into specific audit responsibilities.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Consequence of Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account deposits | Earnest money deposited within contractual and statutory timeframes | Commission fines, license suspension under AS 08.88.401 |
| Disclosure delivery | Property Condition Disclosure provided per AS 34.70 | Buyer rescission rights, disciplinary action |
| Wire instruction verification | Office policy for independent phone verification before every wire | Potential liability for client losses in fraud scenarios |
| Form currency | Agents using current AAR forms, not outdated versions | E&O exposure, deal enforceability issues |
| File completeness at closing | All required documents executed and retained per 12 AAC 64.410 | Commission audit findings, brokerage fines |

**Brokers should conduct file audits at least quarterly, pulling a random sample of closed and pending transaction files.** The Commission has authority to audit brokerage files at any time, and findings of missing disclosures, improperly held earnest money, or incomplete records can result in fines against the broker's license — not just the agent's. Under AS 08.88.401, the Commission can impose civil fines up to $25,000 per violation and can suspend or revoke a broker's license for failure to supervise.

Record retention is another area that catches brokerages off guard. Alaska regulation 12 AAC 64.410 requires that transaction records be maintained for a minimum of three years after closing. Brokers who allow agents to keep files only on personal devices or in disorganized cloud folders are setting themselves up for a compliance failure during a Commission audit. The smarter approach is to [build systems that remove reliance on memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) and instead enforce document management through structured processes.

## ANCSA Land and Native Allotment Considerations

Alaska's unique land ownership history creates title complications that agents in no other state encounter. ANCSA, enacted in 1971, transferred approximately 44 million acres to Alaska Native regional and village corporations. Properties that trace their chain of title through ANCSA conveyances may carry restrictions on alienation, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs may hold a trust or restricted status on certain parcels.

Native allotment land under the Alaska Native Allotment Act of 1906 presents even more complex issues. These allotments are held in trust by the federal government, and any transfer requires Bureau of Indian Affairs approval. Agents who list or show such properties without understanding the federal approval process risk wasting months of their clients' time. Title companies experienced in these transactions — primarily those based in Anchorage and Fairbanks — should be engaged early in the listing process, not after an offer is accepted.

> Alaska Statute 34.15.150 governs the recording requirements for conveyances of real property and requires that all instruments affecting title be recorded in the recording district where the property is located to provide constructive notice.

This statute underpins the entire title search process, and agents should understand that unrecorded interests can still create problems even when they do not appear in the public record. Buyer's agents should always recommend an owner's title insurance policy to protect against these hidden risks.

## Closing Day Logistics and Agent Responsibilities

On closing day in Alaska, the escrow officer at the title company runs the settlement. The agent's role is to ensure their client is prepared, all conditions have been satisfied, and no last-minute issues derail the signing. Agents should confirm at least 48 hours before closing that the title company has received the lender's closing instructions, that all contingencies have been formally released in writing, and that the buyer has wired funds or arranged a cashier's check.

**Alaska does not require both parties to be present at the same table.** Mail-away closings and split closings are common, particularly in rural areas where buyers or sellers may be hundreds of miles from the nearest title office. Agents facilitating remote closings should coordinate with the title company to ensure notarization requirements under AS 09.63 are met and that documents are returned within the timeline specified by the escrow instructions.

After closing, the title company records the deed and deed of trust with the DNR Recorder's Office and disburses funds according to the settlement statement. Agents should verify that recording has occurred and that their clients receive copies of the recorded instruments.
