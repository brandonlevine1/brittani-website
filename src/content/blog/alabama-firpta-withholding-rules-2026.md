## Alabama FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Agent's Liability Primer

The Alabama firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 affect more transactions than most agents realize. Alabama's growing appeal to international investors—particularly in Birmingham, Huntsville, and Gulf Shores—means that listing agents and buyer agents alike encounter foreign-national sellers with increasing regularity. Failing to identify a foreign seller before closing can stick the buyer with a tax bill exceeding six figures and expose every licensee on the transaction to regulatory scrutiny from the Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC).

FIRPTA—the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, codified at 26 U.S.C. Sections 1445 through 1445(e)—requires buyers to withhold a percentage of the gross sales price when acquiring U.S. real property from a "foreign person." The statute defines foreign person broadly: any non-resident alien individual, foreign corporation, foreign partnership, foreign trust, or foreign estate. Alabama does not impose its own separate state-level FIRPTA equivalent, but the federal obligation remains fully operative in every Alabama closing.

## How the Federal Withholding Actually Works in an Alabama Transaction

**The withholding obligation technically falls on the buyer, not the seller.** In practice, however, the closing attorney or title company in Alabama handles remittance. Alabama is an attorney-closing state, meaning a licensed attorney typically conducts closings and prepares settlement statements. That attorney's office collects the withheld funds, files IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests), and submits IRS Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons) to both the IRS and the foreign seller.

The standard withholding rate is 15% of the gross sales price. Two reduced-rate exceptions exist under Section 1445(b)(5): transactions at or below $300,000 where the buyer will use the property as a residence carry a 0% rate, and transactions between $300,001 and $1,000,000 with buyer-residence use carry a 10% rate. These thresholds have not changed for 2026.

| Sales Price Range | Buyer Intends Residence Use | Withholding Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Yes | 0% |
| $300,001–$1,000,000 | Yes | 10% |
| Over $1,000,000 | Yes or No | 15% |
| Any amount | No (investment property) | 15% |

The funds must be remitted to the IRS within 20 days of closing using Forms 8288 and 8288-A. Missing that deadline triggers penalties and interest that accrue against the buyer—creating a post-closing dispute that can blow up an agent's referral relationship.

## The Alabama-Specific Compliance Layer

Alabama does not have a separate state withholding statute comparable to California's or Maryland's. However, the [Alabama Department of Revenue](https://www.revenue.alabama.gov/) requires nonresident sellers—including foreign nationals—to file Alabama Form 40NR or 65 (for partnerships) to report capital gains on Alabama real property. The closing attorney typically addresses this requirement alongside FIRPTA, but agents should confirm it during contract negotiations.

**AREC License Law, specifically Alabama Code Section 34-27-36, obligates agents to disclose material facts and exercise reasonable skill and care.** If you know or should know the seller is a foreign national and fail to flag the withholding requirement, you may be violating your statutory duty. AREC has broad authority to suspend or revoke licenses under Section 34-27-36(a)(12) for incompetence or negligent conduct.

The practical takeaway: even though FIRPTA is federal, your Alabama license is at risk if you ignore it. Agents who [miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on FIRPTA paperwork face the same professional consequences as those who miss inspection or financing contingency dates.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences cascade across multiple parties. The IRS can assess the full 15% withholding amount against the buyer personally, plus penalties under Section 6651 for failure to file and Section 6656 for failure to deposit. Interest accrues from the closing date.

For the agent, liability runs in two directions. First, the buyer may sue the listing agent, buyer's agent, and closing attorney for negligence—arguing that a competent licensee should have identified the seller's foreign status. Alabama courts apply a reasonable-care standard, and ignorance is not a defense if the seller's foreign address, ITIN, or passport information appeared anywhere in the file. Second, AREC can open a complaint investigation under Section 34-27-36, which may result in fines, mandatory education, probation, suspension, or revocation.

Deals can also unwind entirely. If a buyer discovers the withholding obligation at the closing table and the seller refuses to allow funds to be withheld from proceeds, the transaction may collapse. Title insurance companies in Alabama have increasingly added FIRPTA compliance as a closing condition, meaning the title commitment itself may require proof of withholding or a valid affidavit before they will insure.

## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Make with FIRPTA

The first error is assuming the closing attorney will handle everything without agent involvement. Alabama attorneys are thorough, but they depend on information the listing agent gathers during intake. If you never ask the seller about citizenship or residency status, the attorney may not learn the truth until days before closing—too late to apply for a withholding certificate.

The second mistake is conflating residency with citizenship. A seller can live full-time in Tuscaloosa, own property for twenty years, and still be a nonresident alien for tax purposes if they never obtained a green card or passed the substantial presence test. Agents regularly assume that local presence equals U.S. tax status. It does not.

**Third, agents fail to distinguish between the FIRPTA affidavit and the standard seller's certification.** IRS regulations require the seller to provide a Certification of Non-Foreign Status (often called a FIRPTA affidavit) that includes their taxpayer identification number, U.S. address, and a declaration under penalty of perjury. A generic seller disclosure form does not satisfy this requirement. The affidavit must conform to Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-2(b)(2).

Fourth, agents neglect to calendar the 20-day remittance deadline. Unlike many transaction milestones that appear in MLS systems or contract addenda, the IRS remittance window starts at closing and is invisible to most CRM platforms. If the closing attorney misses it, the buyer—and potentially the agent—bears the consequences.

Fifth, agents working with foreign corporate entities fail to recognize that a U.S. LLC owned by a foreign person is still treated as a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes unless it makes a valid election otherwise. An Alabama LLC with a registered agent in Montgomery does not automatically escape withholding if its sole member is a Canadian citizen.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Alabama carry supervisory liability under AREC rules. Section 34-27-35 requires designated brokers to exercise reasonable supervision over affiliated licensees. A pattern of FIRPTA non-compliance across a brokerage—even if no individual agent intended wrongdoing—can trigger AREC disciplinary action against the broker's license.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake forms | Do they include a citizenship/residency question? | Every new listing |
| Transaction file review | Is a FIRPTA affidavit or withholding documentation present? | Before closing |
| Agent training records | Have agents completed FIRPTA training in the last 12 months? | Annually |
| Closing attorney coordination | Is there a documented process for flagging foreign sellers to counsel? | Quarterly review |
| Post-closing confirmation | Has Form 8288 been filed within 20 days? | Within 30 days of closing |

**Brokers who rely solely on memory-based compliance systems are setting themselves up for audit failures.** A structured checklist—or better yet, an automated compliance workflow—catches gaps before they become violations. The difference between a [checklist-driven process](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) and an ad hoc one is often the difference between a clean file and a regulatory complaint.

Brokers should also implement a policy requiring agents to obtain either a completed Certification of Non-Foreign Status or a written acknowledgment from the closing attorney that FIRPTA withholding will be handled. This documentation should be uploaded to the transaction file before the closing date, not after.

## The Withholding Certificate Alternative: IRS Form 8288-B

Foreign sellers often prefer to apply for a withholding certificate rather than have 15% of gross proceeds held at closing. IRS Form 8288-B allows the seller to demonstrate that their actual tax liability on the gain is less than the standard withholding amount. If approved, the IRS issues a certificate authorizing reduced or zero withholding.

The catch: processing times for Form 8288-B applications frequently exceed 90 days. Agents representing foreign sellers should initiate this conversation at listing, not under contract. If the application is still pending at closing, the buyer must withhold the full amount and deposit it with the IRS; the seller receives a refund if the certificate is later approved. Alternatively, the parties can agree to hold funds in escrow pending the IRS determination, but this requires explicit contract language—typically an addendum drafted by the closing attorney.

Alabama-specific contract forms published by the Alabama Association of Realtors do not include a standard FIRPTA addendum. Agents should work with the closing attorney to draft one or use a form approved by their brokerage. The addendum should address the withholding amount, escrow instructions, the 20-day remittance deadline, and responsibility for Form 8288-B filing costs.

## Integrating FIRPTA Compliance Into Your Transaction Workflow

The most effective approach treats FIRPTA identification as a listing-intake task rather than a closing-day scramble. Add a direct question about citizenship and tax residency to your seller questionnaire. If the answer is yes—or if you have reason to suspect foreign status based on the seller's documentation—notify the closing attorney immediately and document that notification in your file.

For buyer-side agents, the obligation is equally real. Your client—the buyer—is the party the IRS will pursue if withholding fails. You owe your buyer a duty to flag this risk and confirm that the closing attorney has addressed it. A simple email asking "Has FIRPTA status been confirmed for this seller?" creates a paper trail that protects you and your client.
