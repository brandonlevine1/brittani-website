## Minnesota Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Rules That Actually Apply

Few questions trip up Minnesota agents faster than "Minnesota attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026." The confusion exists because neighboring states like North Dakota and Wisconsin handle this differently, and misinformation circulates freely in MLS Facebook groups. Minnesota falls squarely in the "title theory" camp, meaning an attorney is not required to conduct closing — but the specifics matter more than the headline.

**Minnesota is not an "attorney state" for residential closings.** Under Minnesota Statutes Chapter 82, specifically Minn. Stat. 82.81, the state licenses "closing agents" who are authorized to conduct real estate closings without attorney supervision. This puts Minnesota in a different regulatory posture than states like Illinois or Massachusetts, where attorney involvement is mandatory.

The Minnesota Department of Commerce oversees closing agent licensure through its Real Estate Division. Agents who assume any warm body can sit at the closing table and hand buyers a pen are exposing their clients — and their own licenses — to real liability.

## Who Can Legally Conduct a Closing in Minnesota

The answer is narrower than most agents think. Three categories of professionals can legally preside over a residential closing in Minnesota.

| Category | Authority Source | Supervision Required | Can Issue Title Insurance |
|----------|-----------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Licensed Closing Agent | Minn. Stat. 82.81 | Must be employed by or contracted with a title company | No (company issues) |
| Minnesota-Licensed Attorney | MN Supreme Court Rules of Professional Conduct | None | No (separate underwriter needed) |
| Title Insurance Company | Minn. Stat. 68A | Self-regulated under Dept. of Commerce | Yes |
| Real Estate Agent | N/A | N/A — **cannot conduct closing** | No |

That last row matters. A real estate agent holding a Minnesota license under Minn. Stat. 82.55 has zero authority to conduct a closing, prepare closing documents, or disburse funds. The line between "attending" closing and "conducting" closing is where agents get into unauthorized practice of law territory.

The Minnesota State Bar Association has repeatedly issued guidance that document preparation — including deeds, affidavits of identity, and eCRV filings — constitutes the practice of law unless performed by a licensed closing agent within the scope of Minn. Stat. 82.81.

## What Agents Actually Need to Do Before and During Closing

Your job at closing in Minnesota is witnessing, not directing. The [Minnesota Association of Realtors](https://www.mnrealtor.com/) Purchase Agreement form (currently form MN-PA-2025, expected to carry forward into 2026 with minor amendments) specifies which party selects the closing agent and title company.

**Paragraph 11 of the standard MN Purchase Agreement designates responsibility for title evidence and closing.** Typically the seller provides the abstract or registered property abstract continuation, and the buyer selects the title company or closing agent. If your buyer's lender has a preferred title company, that preference often dictates the choice — but it cannot override the contractual agreement between the parties without an amendment.

Agents must verify that the person conducting the closing holds an active license. You can check closing agent licenses through the Minnesota Department of Commerce license lookup portal. This takes sixty seconds and prevents a deal from blowing up when a buyer's attorney later challenges the validity of the closing.

## The eCRV Filing and Why It Creates Confusion

Minnesota's electronic Certificate of Real Estate Value (eCRV) system, administered by the Minnesota Department of Revenue, requires filing within 30 days of closing. Many agents mistakenly believe they can file the eCRV themselves, and technically they can — but doing so without proper authority or accurate data creates liability.

The eCRV replaces the old paper CRV and must be filed for any transfer of real property where consideration exceeds $1,000. Under Minn. Stat. 272.115, failure to file results in the county recorder refusing to record the deed. Your deal is effectively dead until the eCRV posts.

Here is where the attorney vs. title company question gets practical: whoever conducts the closing typically handles eCRV submission. If an attorney closes the deal but fails to file the eCRV — or files it with errors — the recording stalls and your commission timeline stretches. Agents who [track deadlines rigorously](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) catch these delays before they cascade.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make at Minnesota Closings

**Mistake 1: Preparing documents they have no authority to prepare.** Filling in a quit claim deed template from the internet is unauthorized practice of law in Minnesota. Even if your client asks you to "just handle it," the answer is no. The Minnesota Supreme Court's Advisory Committee on Rules of Professional Conduct has been clear: document preparation for real estate transfers is legal practice unless performed under Minn. Stat. 82.81 licensure.

**Mistake 2: Directing the closing agent on disbursement order.** Agents sometimes pressure closing agents to disburse commission checks before the deed records. Under Minn. Stat. 82.81, subd. 3(c), the closing agent controls disbursement timing. Pressuring them creates a record of interference that surfaces in complaints to the Department of Commerce.

**Mistake 3: Failing to confirm the closing agent's license status.** If a closing is conducted by an unlicensed person, the transaction is potentially voidable under Minn. Stat. 82.81, subd. 5. Agents who assume "they work at the title company, so they must be licensed" skip a verification step that takes one minute online.

**Mistake 4: Confusing attorney representation with attorney-conducted closing.** A buyer can hire an attorney to review documents without that attorney conducting the closing. These are separate roles. When both a buyer's attorney and the title company's closing agent show up, someone needs to be clearly designated as the closer of record. Ambiguity here creates signature-authority disputes.

**Mistake 5: Scheduling closing without confirming Well Disclosure Certificate status.** Minnesota law (Minn. Stat. 103I.235) requires sellers to disclose the status of all wells on the property before closing. The Well Disclosure Certificate (form MDH Well Disclosure) must be delivered to the buyer and filed with the Minnesota Department of Health. Agents who forget this hold up closings or, worse, close without it — creating post-closing liability.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Minn. Stat. 82.55, subd. 7. If an agent under your supervision engages in unauthorized practice of law or facilitates an unlicensed closing, the broker's license is at risk alongside the agent's.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Risk If Missed |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| Closing agent license verification | Every transaction | Voidable closing, $10,000 fine |
| eCRV filing confirmation | Within 7 days post-close | Deed recording rejection |
| Well Disclosure Certificate in file | Pre-closing review | Minn. Stat. 103I.235 violation |
| Commission disbursement documentation | Monthly reconciliation | Trust account violations |
| Agent attendance at closing (no UPL) | Quarterly file review | MSBA complaint, license action |

**Brokers should implement a pre-closing checklist that requires agents to confirm — in writing — the identity and license number of the person conducting the closing.** This is not bureaucratic overkill. The Minnesota Department of Commerce's enforcement division investigates unauthorized closing agent activity, and brokers who cannot demonstrate supervisory diligence face escalated penalties.

Transaction files should contain a copy of the closing agent's license verification printout. If you manage a team of agents producing volume, the manual overhead of tracking these compliance items adds up fast. ## When an Attorney Actually Should Be at the Table

Minnesota does not require attorneys, but certain transactions practically demand one. Complex title issues — boundary disputes, probate transfers, life estate terminations, contract for deed payoffs — benefit from attorney involvement regardless of what the statute requires.

Commercial transactions almost always involve attorneys for both parties. In residential deals involving trusts, conservatorships, or powers of attorney, the title company's underwriter frequently requires an attorney opinion letter before issuing a clean policy. Agents who recognize these scenarios early and recommend attorney involvement protect their clients and reduce their own E&O exposure.

The cost difference matters to clients. A title company closing in the Twin Cities metro typically runs $400 to $700 for the closing fee alone (separate from title insurance premiums). Attorney-conducted closings range from $800 to $2,000 depending on complexity. Agents should present these numbers neutrally rather than steering clients toward one option or the other — steering creates its own liability under Minn. Stat. 82.68.
