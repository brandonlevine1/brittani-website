## How the New Hampshire Building Code Updates 2026 Reshape Transaction Risk

The New Hampshire building code updates 2026 are not a distant policy concern—they directly affect how you list properties, advise buyers, and manage liability between contract and close. The State Building Code Review Board, operating under RSA 155-A, formally adopted the 2024 International Residential Code (IRC) and 2024 International Building Code (IBC) effective March 1, 2026, replacing the prior 2018 editions that had governed construction standards for years.

For agents and brokers, the practical fallout is immediate. Properties currently under construction, recently renovated, or marketed with additions now sit under a different compliance framework. Buyers and their lenders are already asking questions that agents who haven't studied these changes cannot answer.

This article breaks down what changed, what forms and disclosures apply, and where agents are most likely to make costly errors.

## What Changed: Key Provisions Under the 2024 IRC and IBC Adoption

**The jump from the 2018 codes to the 2024 editions is not incremental—it represents six years of accumulated revisions.** The State Building Code Review Board, which operates under RSA 155-A:10, voted to adopt the updated codes after a public comment period that ended in late 2025. Governor-signed administrative rules (Chapter Bcr 300) formalize the adoption.

The most relevant changes for residential transactions include updated energy efficiency requirements under the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), revised egress window standards, updated deck and balcony load requirements, and new electric vehicle readiness provisions for attached garages. Sprinkler exemptions for single-family dwellings remain intact, but the threshold for triggering fire-separation requirements in duplexes shifted.

For commercial properties, seismic design categories and accessibility provisions received updates that affect mixed-use buildings common in Manchester, Nashua, and the Seacoast region.

## Statutes and Regulatory Bodies Agents Must Know

New Hampshire's building code framework rests on RSA 155-A, the State Building Code statute. The State Building Code Review Board—a nine-member body appointed by the governor—holds authority to adopt, amend, or reject model codes. Local enforcement falls to municipal building officials or, in towns without a building inspector, to the [NH Office of Professional Licensure and Certification](https://www.oplc.nh.gov/).

| Statute/Rule | What It Governs | Agent Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| RSA 155-A:1-11 | State Building Code authority and adoption | Determines which code edition applies to a property |
| RSA 477:4-c | Seller disclosure of property condition | Requires disclosure of known defects, including code violations |
| RSA 331-A:25-b | Licensee duties and prohibited conduct | Prohibits misrepresentation and requires material fact disclosure |
| Bcr 300 (Admin Rules) | Specific code editions adopted | Confirms the 2024 IRC/IBC effective date |
| RSA 674:51 | Local enforcement and building permits | Governs permit issuance and inspections at the municipal level |

Agents don't enforce building codes. But agents do have a statutory duty under RSA 331-A:25-b not to misrepresent property conditions and to disclose material facts they know or reasonably should know. A code violation that affects habitability or safety qualifies.

## Forms and Disclosures That Touch Building Code Compliance

The primary disclosure form in New Hampshire residential transactions is the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure (Form 2359 from the NH Association of Realtors). Section 4 of this form asks about structural issues, renovations, and whether permits were obtained for improvements. Section 7 addresses environmental and safety concerns.

**Agents who gloss over the permit history questions on Form 2359 are inviting post-closing claims.** When a seller checks "unknown" for permit status on a kitchen addition that clearly required a permit, the listing agent needs to document whether they asked follow-up questions. A signed disclosure with "unknown" does not insulate the agent if the agent had actual knowledge of unpermitted work.

The NH Association of Realtors also publishes an Addendum for Property Inspection (Form 2326) and an As-Is Addendum (Form 2089). Neither eliminates the seller's duty under RSA 477:4-c, but the As-Is Addendum does shift repair obligations. Agents must understand that "as-is" does not mean "without disclosure"—a distinction that trips up even experienced licensees.

For new construction, agents should confirm the builder has obtained a Certificate of Occupancy (CO) under the applicable code edition. A CO issued before March 1, 2026 falls under the 2018 codes; one issued after that date must conform to the 2024 editions unless the permit was pulled prior to the effective date and the municipality honors the prior code for in-progress projects.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get This Wrong

Real estate agents in New Hampshire face three tiers of risk when building code issues surface in a transaction. First, the NH Real Estate Commission (operating under RSA 331-A) can impose disciplinary action including license suspension, revocation, or fines up to $3,000 per violation for misrepresentation or failure to disclose material facts.

Second, civil liability under common law fraud or negligent misrepresentation can result in rescission of the purchase and sale agreement, compensatory damages, and in egregious cases, punitive damages. The buyer's cause of action typically names both the seller and the listing agent. Third, the brokerage itself faces vicarious liability under RSA 331-A:25-d, which holds designated brokers responsible for supervising their agents' conduct.

**Deal cancellation is the most common immediate consequence.** When a home inspection reveals unpermitted work that doesn't meet current code, buyers routinely invoke contingency clauses under Form 2326 to terminate. The listing agent who knew about the work—or should have asked—becomes the target of a complaint.

A 2025 NH Real Estate Commission disciplinary case involved a Concord-area agent who failed to disclose a known un-permitted basement conversion. The agent received a $2,500 fine and a six-month license suspension. The seller faced a separate civil suit that settled for the cost of bringing the basement to code plus buyer's attorney fees.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Issues

**Mistake 1: Assuming "grandfathered" means immune from disclosure.** Yes, a 1970s home built under earlier codes does not need to be retrofitted to 2024 standards. But if the seller added a bathroom in 2019 without a permit, that improvement must meet the code in effect in 2019—and the lack of permit is a disclosable fact. Agents conflate "the house is grandfathered" with "nothing needs to be disclosed," and that conflation costs money.

**Mistake 2: Failing to ask about permit history during listing intake.** The listing agent's job is to gather material facts. Walking through a finished basement with drop ceilings and plumbing without asking "Was this permitted?" is a failure of due diligence. You don't need to pull municipal records yourself, but you must ask the seller and document the answer.

**Mistake 3: Misquoting code requirements to buyers during showings.** Agents are not code officials. Telling a buyer "that deck is definitely up to code" or "they changed the egress requirements so this window is fine now" creates liability if those statements are wrong. The correct response is always to recommend a licensed inspector or direct the buyer to the municipal building department.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring code-related inspection findings in repair negotiations.** When a home inspector notes that a renovation doesn't meet code, agents sometimes dismiss it as "the inspector being picky." If the finding relates to safety—improper wiring, inadequate egress, structural deficiency—it's a material fact. Ignoring it in repair negotiations doesn't make the liability disappear.

**Mistake 5: Not verifying the Certificate of Occupancy on new construction.** Representing a new-build without confirming the CO has been issued under the correct code edition is reckless. Buyers who close on a home lacking a valid CO may face insurance and financing complications. The agent who facilitated that closing has exposure.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in New Hampshire carry supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:25-d. The Commission expects brokers to maintain systems that prevent the errors described above. Passive oversight is not a defense.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Review of Form 2359 completion for all listings | Every new listing | Signed copy in transaction file |
| Verification that agents asked about permit history | Spot-check quarterly | Written notes or CRM entries |
| Training on updated code provisions | Within 90 days of adoption | CE credit records or internal training logs |
| Review of marketing materials for code-related claims | Monthly | Screenshots or MLS print-outs |
| Transaction file review for new construction CO verification | Every new-build closing | CO copy in file |

Brokers should issue a written policy memo to all agents within 30 days of the March 2026 effective date. That memo should specify that agents must ask sellers about permit history, must not make representations about code compliance, and must recommend professional inspections for any property with visible additions or renovations.

## How These Updates Interact With Financing and Insurance

Lenders and insurers care about code compliance even when agents don't. FHA and VA appraisals flag safety deficiencies that correlate with code violations—missing handrails, inadequate HVAC, electrical panels that don't meet current standards. A property that "passes" a standard home inspection can still fail an FHA appraisal if the appraiser identifies health and safety issues rooted in code non-compliance.

The 2024 IECC energy provisions also affect properties seeking energy-efficient mortgage (EEM) qualification. Buyers pursuing EEM financing need documentation that the home meets specific energy performance thresholds. Agents representing new construction under the 2024 codes should be prepared to provide HERS rating documentation or equivalent energy performance certificates.

Insurance underwriters in New Hampshire are increasingly requesting permit verification for major renovations before issuing coverage. An unpermitted addition may be excluded from coverage, leaving the buyer exposed. This is another reason agents must document permit status during [the transaction coordination process](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) and flag potential issues early.

## Practical Steps for Agents Between Now and Year-End

The March 2026 effective date has already passed. If you haven't updated your listing intake process, you're behind. Start by adding three questions to your pre-listing consultation: Have any additions or renovations been completed since you purchased the property? Were building permits obtained for that work? Do you have copies of inspection approvals or a Certificate of Occupancy for new work?

Document the answers in writing. If the seller says "I'm not sure," note that on the disclosure form and recommend they contact the municipal building department. Your job is not to verify compliance—it's to ask, document, and disclose.

For buyer-side transactions, advise clients to request permit history from the municipality during due diligence. Many NH towns now offer online permit lookup portals. When the inspection report flags code-related issues, don't dismiss them. Instead, quantify the cost to remediate and factor it into repair requests or price negotiations. Agents who [understand where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) know that unresolved code issues are near the top of that list.

The New Hampshire building code updates 2026 are not optional knowledge—they're the baseline for competent practice. Agents who treat them as someone else's problem will find themselves explaining that position to the Commission, to a judge, or to a client who trusted them to know better. Stay current, document everything, and when you're unsure, refer to the professionals whose job it actually is to interpret the code.
