## Kentucky Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Rule That Trips Up Agents

The question of "Kentucky attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" creates more confusion at settlement tables than almost any other procedural issue in the Commonwealth. Kentucky is not a pure attorney-closing state, nor is it a pure title-company state. It sits in a gray zone defined by overlapping statutes, Kentucky Bar Association opinions, and Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) regulations—and agents who misunderstand the distinction risk deal cancellations, liability exposure, and even allegations of facilitating the unauthorized practice of law.

This post breaks down the specific statutes in play, the forms you fill out, and the audit points brokers should enforce before every closing file leaves the office.

## The Statutory Framework: KRS 524.130 and KRS 382

**Kentucky Revised Statute 524.130 defines the unauthorized practice of law as a Class A misdemeanor**, punishable by up to twelve months in jail and fines up to $500 per occurrence. The statute does not carve out an explicit exception for title companies preparing deeds or legal instruments. What it does allow is for non-attorney settlement agents to process ministerial tasks—escrow management, document recording, fund disbursement—without crossing the line into legal practice.

KRS Chapter 382 governs conveyances and recording requirements. Under KRS 382.135, every deed filed in a Kentucky county clerk's office must meet specific formatting and content requirements. While a title company can coordinate the recording, the actual drafting of the deed—assigning legal descriptions, confirming vesting, selecting tenancy—is considered the practice of law per [Kentucky Bar Association Ethics Opinion KBA E-243](https://www.kybar.org/).

The practical result: a title company can close the deal, but an attorney must either prepare or review the legal documents. Many title companies in Kentucky employ staff attorneys or contract with outside counsel to satisfy this requirement. The agent's job is to confirm this structure exists before directing clients to any closing table.

## What the Contract Forms Actually Say

The Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) Purchase Contract, Form 380, is where this choice gets memorialized. Section 11 of Form 380 designates the closing agent and specifies whether closing will be conducted by an attorney, a title company, or an attorney-supervised title company. Agents fill in the entity name and address, and both parties initial.

**If you leave Section 11 blank or ambiguous, you create a dispute vector that can delay closing by weeks.** KREC has issued guidance that agents must not unilaterally select a closing agent without documented buyer-seller agreement. This is not a formality—it is a compliance requirement tied to KREC's Administrative Regulation 201 KAR 11:121, which governs agency duties and fiduciary obligations.

Additional KAR forms intersect with closing-agent selection. Form 385 (Listing Agreement Addendum) may pre-designate a seller's preferred closing agent, but it cannot override the buyer's contractual rights under Form 380 absent mutual consent. Agents working both sides of a transaction in a disclosed dual-agency arrangement (governed by KRS 324.360) must be especially careful not to steer either party to a particular closing provider without written acknowledgment.

## Attorney State vs. Title-Company State: Where Kentucky Actually Falls

| Factor | Attorney-State Model | Title-Company Model | Kentucky's Hybrid |
|--------|---------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Deed preparation | Attorney required | Title company staff | Attorney required |
| Title search/opinion | Attorney required | Title company | Attorney supervision required |
| Escrow/disbursement | Attorney or paralegal | Title company | Either permitted |
| Physical presence at closing | Attorney required | Not required | Not required by statute |
| Recording | Attorney or agent | Title company | Either permitted |

Kentucky sits in a middle position that demands attorney involvement at the document-preparation stage but does not require an attorney to physically sit at the closing table. This distinction matters. An agent who tells a client "you don't need a lawyer" is making an incomplete statement. The accurate version: "An attorney does not need to attend closing, but one must prepare or supervise the legal documents."

## Common Mistakes Agents Make

**First, agents frequently assume a title company's involvement satisfies all legal requirements.** It does not. If the title company does not employ or contract with a Kentucky-licensed attorney, the deed and title opinion may be improperly prepared. The agent has no legal duty to verify attorney involvement, but if a closing fails or a title defect emerges and the agent recommended that company, [E&O exposure increases significantly](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Second, agents draft or modify deed language themselves. This happens more often than brokers want to admit—a seller asks for a name change on the deed, and the listing agent types in a correction rather than routing it back to counsel. Under KRS 524.130, this constitutes unauthorized practice of law, full stop. Even seemingly minor alterations to vesting or legal descriptions cross the line.

Third, agents fail to disclose the closing-agent selection process to buyers in writing. KREC expects that any recommendation of a closing agent is accompanied by a written disclosure of any affiliated business arrangement, per the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) Section 8 and KRS 324.160's requirement for honest dealing.

Fourth, agents confuse title insurance commitment with a title opinion. A title insurance commitment is a product offered by an insurer. A title opinion is a legal document prepared by an attorney analyzing the chain of title. Kentucky lenders almost universally require both, but agents sometimes present only the commitment to buyers, skipping attorney review of exceptions and objections.

Fifth, agents operating in border counties—particularly along the Ohio, Indiana, or Tennessee lines—apply neighboring states' closing customs to Kentucky transactions. Ohio, for example, is a title-company state with no attorney requirement. [Agents licensed in multiple states must apply Kentucky law to Kentucky property](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026), regardless of where their office is physically located.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers hold vicarious liability for their agents' compliance failures under KRS 324.160 and 201 KAR 11:121. A brokerage's transaction-management protocol should include verification checkpoints before any file reaches closing.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Form 380, Section 11 | Closing agent designated, initialed by both parties | Contract dispute, delayed closing |
| Attorney involvement | Confirm title company has attorney on staff or under contract | UPL exposure, title defect liability |
| AfBA disclosure | Filed if agent has ownership interest in title company | RESPA violation, $10,000+ penalty |
| Deed review | Agent did NOT draft or modify deed language | Criminal misdemeanor, E&O claim |
| Title opinion vs. commitment | Both ordered and received before closing date | Lender rejection, funding delay |

**Brokers should require a signed closing-agent confirmation memo in every file before the transaction coordinator schedules a settlement date.** This one-page document—not mandated by KREC but widely recommended by Kentucky real estate attorneys—confirms that the closing entity has attorney supervision, carries appropriate E&O insurance, and can issue an owner's title policy. Implementing this at the brokerage level eliminates the majority of closing-day surprises.

KREC's disciplinary actions in 2024 and 2025 included multiple sanctions against agents who facilitated closings with non-attorney deed preparation. Sanctions ranged from formal reprimands to license suspensions of 30–90 days. Brokers who fail to [implement systematic compliance checks](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) risk having their own licenses reviewed under KRS 324.160(4), which holds the broker responsible for supervising all licensees under their authority.

## Liability Exposure When Things Go Wrong

A closing that proceeds without proper attorney involvement creates a cascade of liability. The deed may be voidable if a court determines it was prepared by an unlicensed individual. The title insurance policy may exclude coverage for defects arising from improperly prepared instruments. The buyer could sue both the agent and the brokerage for negligent referral.

KREC has enforcement authority under KRS 324.281 to impose fines up to $1,000 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional continuing education. Federal RESPA violations for undisclosed affiliated business arrangements carry penalties up to $10,000 per occurrence. These are not theoretical risks—they are active enforcement priorities.

**The safest posture for any Kentucky agent is to treat every closing as requiring attorney involvement at the document level, regardless of whether the settlement is conducted at a title company's office.** Document your verification. Keep the confirmation memo in the file. Move on.

Agents should not expect a shift toward pure title-company closings anytime soon—the Kentucky Bar Association actively monitors and opposes any legislative effort to dilute attorney involvement in real property transfers.
