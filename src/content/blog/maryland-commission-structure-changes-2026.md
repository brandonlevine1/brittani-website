## How Maryland Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026 Reshape Agent Compensation

The dust from the Sitzer/Burnett verdict has settled into binding practice changes that hit Maryland agents where it matters most: the paycheck. Understanding Maryland commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 is no longer optional reading — it is the difference between a compliant practice and one that faces regulatory action from the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC). Every transaction you enter now carries documentation requirements that did not exist two years ago.

Maryland operates under the Maryland Real Estate Brokers Act (Business Occupations and Professions Article, Title 17) and the Code of Maryland Regulations (COMAR) Title 09, Subtitle 11. These statutes give MREC the authority to discipline agents who fail to meet the new compensation transparency standards that flowed from the August 2024 NAR settlement effective date. The changes are not suggestions from your local association — they carry the force of the consent decree approved by the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Missouri.

## What Actually Changed in Maryland's Commission Framework

**The single biggest structural shift is the elimination of cooperative compensation offers through Bright MLS.** Before the settlement, a listing agent could publish a blanket offer of buyer-agent compensation directly in the MLS. That mechanism is gone. Bright MLS, which serves Maryland and several neighboring states, removed the compensation field from all active listing data in August 2024.

This means buyer-agent compensation must now originate from one of three places: the buyer themselves, a concession negotiated in the purchase contract, or a separate off-MLS agreement between listing broker and buyer's broker. The old model where compensation was automatic and invisible to the buyer has been replaced by a model where every dollar must be disclosed, agreed upon in writing, and signed before touring begins.

Maryland Realtors updated several standard forms to accommodate these requirements. The most critical are Form 1101 (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) and Form 1107 (Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement), both of which now require a specific, definite compensation amount or percentage — not a maximum, not a range.

## The Written Buyer Agreement Mandate: Maryland's Specific Requirements

Under the NAR settlement terms, no agent who is a member of any MLS participating in the settlement may show property to a buyer without a signed written agreement that discloses the agent's compensation. In Maryland, MREC already required agency disclosure through the [Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form](https://www.dllr.state.maryland.gov/license/mrec/), but the compensation piece is new territory.

The written agreement must satisfy three conditions simultaneously. First, it must state a definite amount or rate of compensation — the phrase "up to X%" is non-compliant. Second, it must include a clear statement that broker fees are not set by law and are fully negotiable. Third, the agreement cannot be open-ended; it must specify a duration.

Maryland Code, Business Occupations and Professions, Section 17-532 already required written brokerage agreements for exclusive relationships. The NAR settlement extends this in practice to any showing activity, even if the agent has not yet secured an exclusive commitment from the buyer. Agents relying on verbal understandings or handshake arrangements are operating outside the rules.

## Common Mistakes Maryland Agents Are Making Right Now

**Mistake one: using "up to" language in Form 1101.** The settlement requires a definite amount. Writing "up to 3%" leaves the compensation unspecified and violates the agreement's terms. MREC examiners reviewing transaction files have flagged this as the most frequent deficiency in 2025-2026 audits.

Mistake two: showing property before obtaining a signed buyer agreement. Some agents treat the first showing as a "get to know you" session that falls outside the rule. It does not. The practice change applies to any touring activity conducted by an MLS participant, regardless of how informal the agent considers the interaction.

Mistake three: advertising commission rates on personal websites or social media in ways that imply a standard rate exists. COMAR 09.11.02.05 prohibits misleading advertising by licensees. Stating "I charge 2.5%" without context suggesting negotiability can trigger a complaint.

Mistake four: failing to renegotiate the buyer agreement when the seller offers a different concession amount. If your Form 1101 states your compensation is $15,000 but the seller concession covers only $10,000, you must have a documented understanding with your buyer about the $5,000 gap before contract ratification.

Mistake five: dual agents who neglect to execute separate compensation disclosures for each party. Maryland permits disclosed dual agency under Section 17-530, but the NAR settlement's transparency requirements mean both the seller and buyer must independently understand and agree to the compensation structure. One disclosure form does not satisfy both sides.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences operate on three levels: regulatory, contractual, and civil. At the regulatory level, MREC can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-526. Repeated violations can result in license suspension or revocation following a hearing before the Commission.

At the contractual level, a buyer who discovers their agent lacked a proper written agreement may seek rescission of the purchase contract. Maryland courts have recognized that material non-disclosure of agent compensation can constitute a breach of fiduciary duty, giving the aggrieved party grounds to unwind the transaction or seek damages.

**At the civil level, class-action exposure remains real.** The Sitzer/Burnett plaintiffs recovered $1.78 billion in damages. Individual agents who continue pre-settlement practices create liability not just for themselves but for their brokerages. Maryland's joint and several liability rules mean the broker of record can be held financially responsible for an agent's non-compliance under COMAR 09.11.01.17.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in Their Offices

Brokers in Maryland hold supervisory obligations under Section 17-525 of the Business Occupations and Professions Article. The law requires brokers to maintain adequate supervision of all licensees operating under their authority. In the post-settlement environment, this obligation extends specifically to compensation documentation.

Every brokerage should maintain an audit checklist that covers these verification points for each transaction file:

| Audit Item | Compliant Standard | Red Flag |
|-----------|-------------------|----------|
| Buyer agreement signed date | Before or on date of first showing | Agreement dated after first showing or unsigned |
| Compensation amount | Specific dollar amount or percentage stated | "Up to" language, blank field, or range |
| Duration of agreement | Definite end date included | Open-ended or perpetual terms |
| Negotiability disclosure | Present in agreement language | Missing or buried in addenda |
| Dual agency compensation | Separate disclosures for each party | Single form attempting to cover both sides |
| Seller concession documentation | Matches or addresses any gap with buyer agreement | Mismatch between concession and buyer-agent fee with no amendment |

**Brokers who discover non-compliant files have an affirmative duty to correct the deficiency.** Ignoring a known violation does not protect the brokerage — it compounds liability. MREC's 2025 guidance memorandum specifically states that brokers will be held accountable for systemic compliance failures, not just isolated agent errors.

The practical implementation means brokers should review buyer agreements within 48 hours of execution, not at the end of the transaction when the file is assembled for closing. Waiting until settlement to catch a deficiency means the violation has already occurred and the exposure window has been open for weeks. ## How This Intersects With Maryland's Existing Agency Laws

Maryland's agency framework predates the NAR settlement and remains intact. The state recognizes seller's agents, buyer's agents, dual agents, and intra-company agents under Title 17, Subtitle 5. The settlement did not change who you can represent — it changed how you document compensation for that representation.

The Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form (often called the "agency disclosure" or the "consumer notice") remains mandatory at first scheduled meeting with any party. But this form addresses representation, not compensation. Agents who believe delivering the agency disclosure satisfies the new compensation requirements are conflating two distinct obligations. You need both: the agency disclosure under Maryland statute and the written compensation agreement under the settlement terms.

For agents working across state lines — common in the DC-Maryland-Virginia corridor — the rules differ by jurisdiction. Maryland's specific form numbers and COMAR requirements do not transfer to Virginia or DC transactions. Understanding [how commission and licensing rules vary across jurisdictions](/blog/washington-commission-and-licensing-rules-2026) can prevent costly assumptions when your client's search spans multiple states.

## Practical Steps for Agents Entering Transactions in 2026

Start every buyer relationship with a direct conversation about your compensation. Before pulling up listings, before scheduling the first showing, have the Form 1101 or 1107 completed with a specific dollar amount or percentage. Keep a signed copy in your transaction file from day one.

When writing offers, address buyer-agent compensation explicitly in the contract. Maryland Realtors Form 1405 (Residential Contract of Sale) includes provisions for seller concessions. If you intend to request that the seller cover your fee, draft that request clearly and ensure your buyer understands what happens if the seller refuses or counters at a lower amount.

Document every compensation negotiation in writing. If a listing agent contacts you off-MLS to discuss their seller's willingness to contribute toward your fee, confirm that conversation in email. Verbal agreements about compensation create he-said-she-said disputes that MREC has no patience for. Transaction management that tracks [where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) can help you identify the exact moments where compensation documentation tends to slip through the cracks.
