## Understanding Hawaii Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Hawaii's regulatory framework for dual agency and designated agency carries real teeth, and agents who treat disclosure as a formality risk losing their licenses. The **Hawaii dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** landscape is governed primarily by Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467, the Hawaii Administrative Rules Title 16, Chapter 99, and enforcement by the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC). Every agent practicing on the islands needs to understand these rules at the statutory level, not just through office training slides.

The distinction between dual agency and designated agency in Hawaii is not academic. It determines what fiduciary duties you owe, what information you can share, and how deeply your broker is exposed to liability. Getting this wrong on even one transaction can cascade into commission disputes, deal cancellations, and formal complaints to HIREC.

## Statutory Framework: HRS 467 and HAR 16-99

Hawaii Revised Statutes Section 467-14(18) identifies the failure to make required agency disclosures as grounds for disciplinary action. This statute doesn't merely suggest disclosure; it makes non-compliance a licensable offense. The [Hawaii Real Estate Commission](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) has consistently interpreted this provision to require written consent before dual agency commences.

**Hawaii Administrative Rules Section 16-99-3 defines the types of agency relationships permitted in the state.** Under HAR 16-99-3(c), a licensee may act as a dual agent only with the informed written consent of all parties. HAR 16-99-3(f) addresses designated agency (called "assigned agency" in Hawaii's regulatory language), permitting a principal broker to assign different licensees within the same firm to represent each party independently.

The practical effect is clear: a single agent representing both sides triggers dual agency, while two agents in the same brokerage representing opposite sides triggers designated agency. Both require distinct disclosure protocols and different forms.

## Required Forms and Timing of Disclosure

Hawaii uses specific Hawaii Association of Realtors (HAR) standard forms to document agency relationships. Agents must deliver form **RR401 (Agency Disclosure)** at the earliest practicable time, defined as first substantive contact with a prospective buyer or seller. This form outlines the types of agency available and the duties owed under each arrangement.

When a dual agency situation arises, agents must obtain signatures on form **RR403 (Dual Agency Consent)** before proceeding. This form requires both buyer and seller to acknowledge that the dual agent cannot advocate for one party over the other, cannot disclose confidential pricing strategy, and cannot provide undivided loyalty to either side. The consent must be knowing and voluntary, which means the agent needs to actually explain the limitations, not just hand over a form for signature.

For designated agency, the principal broker documents the assignment using internal brokerage records and ensures each assigned agent understands their fiduciary scope. There is no single statewide form mandated for designated agency assignment, but HIREC expects brokers to maintain clear written records demonstrating the designation occurred before substantive negotiation began.

| Scenario | Required Form(s) | Timing | Who Signs |
|----------|------------------|--------|-----------|
| Single agent, one client | RR401 | First substantive contact | Client |
| Dual agency (one agent, both sides) | RR401 + RR403 | Before dual representation begins | Both parties |
| Designated agency (two agents, same brokerage) | RR401 for each client + broker designation record | Before substantive negotiation | Each client + broker documentation |
| Referral or transaction brokerage | RR401 (modified selection) | First substantive contact | Client |

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

Agents who skip or delay agency disclosures in Hawaii face a multi-layered penalty structure. HIREC can impose fines of up to $500 per violation under HRS 467-14, and repeat offenders face license suspension or revocation. These penalties apply independently of any civil liability the agent may incur.

**On the civil side, undisclosed dual agency can be grounds for rescission of the purchase contract.** If a buyer or seller can demonstrate they were not informed of the dual agency relationship before entering the agreement, Hawaii courts have recognized claims for actual damages, return of deposits, and in egregious cases, punitive damages. The agent's errors and omissions insurance may not cover intentional disclosure failures, leaving the licensee personally exposed.

Beyond individual penalties, the brokerage's principal broker faces supervisory liability under HRS 467-1.6. HIREC can discipline brokers who fail to establish adequate systems for ensuring agency disclosure compliance, even if the broker was not personally involved in the transaction. This makes dual agency compliance a firm-wide concern, not just an individual agent problem.

The relationship between [proper disclosure and deal stability](/blog/where-deals-break-down) cannot be overstated. Transactions that unravel over undisclosed dual agency often result in extended litigation that dwarfs the original commission.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Hawaii

**First, agents routinely confuse the timing of disclosure with the timing of consent.** The RR401 agency disclosure must happen at first substantive contact, which Hawaii defines as any discussion that could reasonably be expected to lead to a transaction. Waiting until the offer stage is too late. The RR403 dual agency consent is a separate obligation that arises only when dual agency actually begins, but agents sometimes combine both steps or skip one entirely.

Second, agents assume that because their brokerage has two licensees on opposite sides of a deal, they automatically have designated agency protections. This is incorrect. The principal broker must affirmatively designate the agents and document that designation. Without the broker's written assignment, the default relationship is dual agency for the entire firm, exposing both agents and the broker to dual agency restrictions and liabilities.

Third, agents fail to explain the material limitations of dual agency in plain language. HIREC has disciplined licensees who obtained signatures on RR403 without actually discussing what the form means. The consent must be "informed," which requires a verbal explanation of how dual agency limits the agent's ability to share pricing intelligence, negotiate aggressively, or recommend acceptance or rejection of offers.

Fourth, agents operating on neighbor islands sometimes treat informal relationships differently from Oahu transactions, assuming smaller markets have different standards. HIREC applies the same rules statewide. A Maui listing agent who also writes an offer for an unrepresented buyer is in a dual agency situation regardless of market size.

Fifth, agents forget to document mid-transaction agency changes. If a buyer's agent within a brokerage lists a property that their buyer-client wants to purchase, the agency relationship has changed. That change requires new disclosure and new consent before the next substantive step occurs.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry direct responsibility for agency compliance under HRS 467-1.6 and HAR 16-99-5. HIREC audits have specifically examined whether brokerages maintain systems for tracking agency disclosures across active files. A broker who cannot produce signed RR401 forms for every active client relationship is already non-compliant.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| RR401 on file | Signed copy in every transaction file, dated before or at first substantive contact | Every new file |
| RR403 on file (if dual agency) | Signed by both parties, dated before dual representation began | Upon dual agency identification |
| Designated agency documentation | Written broker designation memo with date and agent names | Upon in-house match |
| Agent training records | Annual training on agency disclosure, documented attendance | Annually |
| Transaction timeline review | Confirm disclosure dates precede negotiation dates | Quarterly sample audit |

**Brokers should implement a file-opening checklist that flags agency disclosure status before any offer is drafted.** This is not optional best practice; it is the minimum standard HIREC expects during regulatory audits. The commission has publicly stated that "lack of a supervision system" is itself a disciplinable offense, separate from any individual agent violation.

Quarterly file audits should sample at least 10-15 percent of closed transactions and verify that disclosure dates are consistent with transaction timelines. If an RR401 is dated the same day as the purchase contract, that is a red flag requiring further investigation. Agents who consistently produce same-day disclosures likely need retraining on the "first substantive contact" standard.

Brokers managing teams face additional complexity. The [supervision obligations for teams](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) vary by state, but Hawaii's framework places ultimate responsibility on the principal broker regardless of team structure. A team lead cannot satisfy the broker's supervisory obligation unless they hold a broker's license themselves.

## Designated Agency: Operational Boundaries

Designated agency in Hawaii offers a meaningful alternative to dual agency when structured correctly. Under HAR 16-99-3(f), the principal broker designates one licensee to represent the buyer and another to represent the seller, and each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties to their assigned client. The broker, however, becomes a neutral supervisor who cannot share confidential information between the two sides.

This creates an information wall within the brokerage. The listing agent cannot tell the buyer's agent about the seller's bottom-line price, and the buyer's agent cannot disclose the buyer's maximum willingness to pay. If either agent breaches this wall, the entire brokerage may lose its designated agency protection and default to dual agency status, which requires retroactive consent that is almost impossible to obtain cleanly.

Operationally, brokers should maintain separate digital file access for designated agency transactions. Shared transaction management platforms that allow both agents to view all notes and communications can inadvertently destroy the confidentiality wall. Tools that permit role-based access controls become essential for maintaining compliance, and automated [deadline tracking systems](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) can help brokers monitor whether disclosure timing obligations are met without manual intervention.
