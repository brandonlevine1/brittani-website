## South Dakota Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026: The Statutes, Forms, and Pitfalls You Cannot Afford to Ignore

Handling earnest money incorrectly is one of the fastest ways to lose your license in South Dakota. The rules are specific, the deadlines are tight, and the South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) does not treat violations as technicalities. If you are actively listing or writing offers in 2026, you need a working command of South Dakota earnest money & escrow laws 2026 — not a vague memory from your pre-licensing course.

This post breaks down the exact statutes, the forms you should be using, the penalties that follow noncompliance, and the operational mistakes that trip up even experienced agents. Brokers will also find a dedicated section on what they should be auditing inside their own offices right now.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Earnest Money in South Dakota

South Dakota's trust account and earnest money obligations are codified primarily in SDCL Chapter 36-21A, with SDCL 36-21A-72 serving as the backbone provision. That statute requires every licensed broker to maintain a trust account at a financial institution located in South Dakota, and to deposit all earnest money into that account within two business days of receipt. There is no wiggle room on the two-day timeline — weekends and state holidays do not count as business days, but every other day does.

**The trust account must be entirely separate from the broker's personal or operating accounts.** Commingling is a standalone violation regardless of whether the funds are ultimately accounted for. The SDREC has repeatedly emphasized in its disciplinary actions that good intentions do not excuse commingling.

Administrative rules under ARSD 20:69:03 further clarify trust account recordkeeping standards. Brokers must reconcile their trust accounts monthly and retain records for a minimum of three years. The Commission can audit these records at any time without prior notice, a power codified in SDCL 36-21A-56.

## Which Forms South Dakota Agents Should Be Using

The South Dakota Association of REALTORS (SDAR) publishes standardized forms that are the industry default across most MLS boards in the state. For residential purchase transactions, the key form is the **SDAR Purchase Agreement**, which includes specific blanks for the earnest money amount, the identity of the holder, and the deposit deadline. Agents should also be familiar with the SDAR Addendum to Purchase Agreement form when modifying earnest money terms after the initial offer.

When a transaction falls apart and both sides agree on how to handle the deposit, the SDAR Mutual Release form governs the disbursement of funds. This form requires signatures from both buyer and seller before the broker can release money from the trust account. Without it, the broker holds the funds — there is no unilateral release mechanism built into the standard forms.

Agents who draft their own language instead of using SDAR forms take on significant liability. Custom clauses that contradict SDCL 36-21A-72 or omit required disclosures can expose both the agent and their brokerage to disciplinary action. The SDREC does not regulate the forms themselves, but it absolutely regulates the conduct those forms are supposed to document.

## Deposit Timelines, Holding, and Disbursement: A Side-by-Side View

Understanding who holds what, and when, prevents the majority of earnest money disputes. The following table summarizes the key obligations and timelines under current South Dakota law.

| Obligation | Requirement | Governing Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit deadline | Two business days after receipt | SDCL 36-21A-72 |
| Account type | Dedicated trust account at a SD financial institution | SDCL 36-21A-72 |
| Monthly reconciliation | Required, with documentation retained | ARSD 20:69:03 |
| Record retention | Minimum three years | ARSD 20:69:03 |
| Disbursement on mutual release | Only after both parties sign the SDAR Mutual Release | SDAR form / SDCL 36-21A-72 |
| Disbursement on dispute | Funds held until court order or written agreement | SDCL 36-21A-72 |

One issue that confuses agents: the purchase agreement may specify a different entity as the escrow holder, such as a title company or an attorney. When a third-party holder is named, the listing or selling broker is still responsible for delivering the funds to that holder within the two-business-day window. The obligation to deposit promptly does not transfer just because someone else is named on the form.

## What Happens When an Agent Gets It Wrong

The consequences for mishandling earnest money in South Dakota are not hypothetical. The SDREC publishes its [disciplinary actions and enforcement orders](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/commission.aspx) publicly, and a review of recent cases reveals that trust account violations are among the most common grounds for discipline.

**Penalties range from formal reprimands and mandatory continuing education to license suspension and outright revocation.** The Commission can also impose civil fines. Under SDCL 36-21A-57, the SDREC has authority to deny, suspend, or revoke a license for any violation of the trust account provisions, and it has exercised that authority repeatedly.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face personal civil liability. If a buyer loses earnest money because an agent failed to deposit it, misrouted it, or released it without proper authorization, the agent and the brokerage can be sued for the lost funds plus damages. Errors and omissions insurance may cover some of these claims, but many E&O policies exclude intentional acts or knowing violations of state law — and a failure to follow deposit timelines can be characterized as exactly that.

Deal cancellation is also a practical risk. When buyers or sellers discover that their funds were not handled according to the purchase agreement, they may invoke breach provisions and walk away from the transaction entirely. The resulting fallout — lost commissions, damaged client relationships, potential complaints to the SDREC — compounds the original mistake many times over.

## Five Common Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make With Earnest Money

**First, holding checks instead of depositing them.** Some agents keep the buyer's earnest money check in their desk drawer or car until the inspection contingency passes, thinking they are doing the buyer a favor. This directly violates the two-business-day deposit requirement. The deposit obligation begins when the agent receives the funds, not when contingencies are satisfied.

Second, failing to name the escrow holder on the purchase agreement. The SDAR Purchase Agreement has a specific blank for identifying who will hold the earnest money. Leaving it blank or writing something ambiguous like "TBD" creates confusion at best and a compliance gap at worst. Every offer should clearly state the holder's name and the account into which funds will be deposited.

Third, releasing funds based on a verbal agreement between the parties. Even if the buyer calls and says "just send it back," the broker cannot disburse without a signed mutual release or a court order. Verbal authorizations are not sufficient under SDCL 36-21A-72. Agents who shortcut this step expose their broker to serious liability.

Fourth, poor communication about receipt. Agents sometimes accept earnest money at a showing or an open house and fail to notify the broker or office manager promptly. If the broker does not know the funds exist, they cannot deposit them within two business days. This is a systemic failure, and the SDREC will hold both the agent and the supervising broker accountable.

Fifth, miscalculating business days around holidays. South Dakota observes state holidays that may not align with federal banking holidays. Agents who assume they have an extra day because of a holiday they incorrectly identified have missed the deposit deadline. When in doubt, deposit sooner rather than later.

If you find yourself regularly [missing transaction deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), earnest money deposit windows are the single highest-risk item to get wrong — more so than inspection responses or financing contingencies.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce in 2026

Brokers bear ultimate responsibility for trust account compliance. The SDREC does not accept "my agent handled it" as a defense. Here is what every managing broker in South Dakota should be doing at minimum.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | Discrepancies between ledger balance and bank statement |
| Deposit timing review | Per transaction | Gap between agent receipt date and deposit date exceeding two business days |
| Mutual release documentation | Per closed or canceled file | Signed SDAR Mutual Release on file before any disbursement |
| Agent receipt logs | Weekly | Unreported funds, missing receipt dates, incomplete entries |
| Record retention compliance | Quarterly | Files older than three years may be archived; files younger than three years must be readily accessible |

**Brokers should also conduct at least one unannounced spot-check of agent files per quarter.** This is not about distrust — it is about catching process failures before the SDREC finds them during a random audit. The Commission's authority to audit without notice under SDCL 36-21A-56 means that a broker's first warning of a problem may be an investigator walking through the office door.

Training is the other half of enforcement. Brokers who hold an annual or semi-annual meeting specifically on trust account procedures — not bundled into a generic compliance session — see fewer violations. Documenting that training took place also creates an affirmative defense if an individual agent later violates policy: the brokerage can demonstrate that it set and communicated clear standards.

For brokers managing teams or multiple offices, the operational complexity multiplies. A breakdown in [how checklists and systems are maintained](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is often the root cause of trust account errors, not a lack of legal knowledge.

## Disputed Earnest Money: When Neither Party Will Sign the Release

Disputed earnest money is one of the most uncomfortable situations in real estate, and South Dakota law does not give the broker a shortcut. When the buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the deposit, SDCL 36-21A-72 requires the broker to hold the funds in the trust account until receiving either a signed mutual release or a court order.

Some brokers attempt to resolve disputes informally by negotiating between the parties. This is risky. If a broker applies pressure — even subtly — to get one party to sign a release, that broker may be accused of acting outside their licensed role or favoring one client over another. The safest practice is to notify both parties in writing that the funds will remain in trust, advise them to consult their own attorneys, and wait for proper authorization before moving a dollar.

**Interpleader actions — where the broker files a court petition asking a judge to decide — are an option but not a common one.** The cost and time involved usually exceed the amount in dispute for a typical residential transaction. Still, brokers should know the procedure exists and have a relationship with an attorney who can file on short notice if the dispute escalates or drags on for months.

Missing any step in that chain creates a gap that is visible during an audit.
