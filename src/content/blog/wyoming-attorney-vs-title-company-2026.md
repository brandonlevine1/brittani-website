## Who Actually Needs to Show Up at a Wyoming Closing?

The question of **Wyoming attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026** trips up more agents than you would expect, especially those relocating from attorney-mandatory states like New York or Georgia. Wyoming falls firmly in the "title company state" category, meaning no statute forces either party to hire an attorney for a residential real estate closing. But that classification hides a surprising amount of nuance that can burn agents who assume the title company handles everything.

Wyoming's real estate closing framework is governed by a patchwork of statutes under Title 26 (Insurance Code) and Title 33 (Professions and Occupations), along with rules enforced by the Wyoming Real Estate Commission (WREC). Understanding what each party can and cannot do at the closing table is not optional knowledge — it is the difference between a clean deal and a licensing complaint. This post breaks down the statutory requirements, the form numbers you need to know, and the specific mistakes that land agents in disciplinary trouble.

## Wyoming's Statutory Framework for Closings

**Wyoming does not require attorney involvement at any stage of a residential closing.** This is not a gray area. The state has no statute equivalent to, say, South Carolina's requirement that an attorney supervise every closing. Instead, Wyoming Statute 26-23-301 through 26-23-315 governs who may act as a title insurance agent and, by extension, who may conduct the settlement.

Under W.S. 26-23-301, a "title insurance agent" must be licensed by the Wyoming Department of Insurance and must maintain errors and omissions coverage. These agents — typically employees of title companies — prepare the closing disclosure, disburse funds, and record documents with the county clerk. They are authorized to perform these tasks without attorney oversight.

Attorneys, of course, may also conduct closings under their general authority to practice law in Wyoming. Some buyers or sellers hire attorneys for complex transactions involving trusts, estates, or commercial elements. The key statutory distinction is that attorney presence is elective, not mandatory.

## What Title Companies Handle (and What They Cannot)

Title companies in Wyoming perform the bulk of closing work for residential transactions. Their responsibilities include the title search, title commitment issuance, preparation of the settlement statement, funds escrow, and recording of the deed and mortgage with the county clerk's office.

However, title companies and their agents face a hard boundary: they cannot provide legal advice. Wyoming's unauthorized practice of law statutes, enforced by the Wyoming State Bar under the Rules of the Supreme Court of Wyoming, prohibit anyone other than a licensed attorney from advising parties on the legal effect of documents they are signing. A title agent who explains what a warranty deed "means for your rights" has crossed the line.

This distinction matters enormously for agents sitting at the closing table. When a buyer turns to the title agent and asks whether a specific easement clause could affect their planned addition, the title agent must decline to answer — and so must you. Agents who attempt to fill that gap risk a complaint under [W.S. 33-28-303](https://wyoleg.gov/NXT/gateway.dll?f=templates&fn=default.htm), which governs prohibited acts by licensees and specifically addresses unauthorized practice of law.

## Attorney vs. Title Company: A Side-by-Side Comparison

The following table clarifies the practical differences between using an attorney and a title company to close a residential transaction in Wyoming.

| Function | Title Company | Attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Title search and commitment | Yes — standard service | Yes — but often outsources to a title company |
| Prepare closing disclosure (TRID) | Yes — required for lender transactions | Yes — if acting as settlement agent |

For the vast majority of residential deals in Wyoming, a title company handles every necessary function. Attorneys enter the picture when a party wants legal counsel on document interpretation, when litigation risk exists, or when estate and trust complications arise.

## Forms Every Wyoming Agent Should Know

WREC mandates the use of specific Commission-approved forms for most residential transactions. Agents cannot draft their own contracts from scratch — doing so constitutes unauthorized practice of law. The primary forms relevant to closing include the following.

The **WREC Purchase and Sale Agreement (Form 1)** is the backbone of every residential deal. It includes standard closing provisions, designates who selects the title company, and establishes the closing date framework. Agents must use the most current version; outdated forms generate Commission complaints more often than you might think.

**WREC Form 2 (Counter Offer)** and **WREC Form 7 (Amendment to Purchase and Sale Agreement)** handle modifications that arise before closing. If closing must be extended because of title defects discovered late, Form 7 is the correct vehicle — not an email exchange between agents. The Wyoming Real Estate Commission has been clear in its guidance that informal written modifications outside approved forms create liability exposure for the agent.

**WREC Form 26 (Agency Disclosure)** must be delivered before any substantive discussion about a transaction. This form is relevant to the closing context because failure to disclose agency relationships early can unravel a deal at the closing table when a party claims they were not properly represented. For more detail on Wyoming's agency rules, see our breakdown of [Wyoming agency disclosure requirements](/blog/wyoming-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make at or Before Closing

**Mistake one: advising clients on whether they "need" an attorney.** Telling a buyer "you don't need a lawyer for this" is not your call. WREC rules require agents to recommend that parties seek independent legal counsel when legal questions arise. Failing to do so — or worse, affirmatively steering clients away from attorneys — creates disciplinary exposure under W.S. 33-28-303(a)(xxiii), which addresses conduct that is "dishonest or unprofessional."

Mistake two: using outdated or unapproved contract forms. WREC periodically updates its approved forms, and agents who pull a 2023-era Form 1 out of habit risk having the Commission treat that as an unauthorized form. The Commission's website publishes the current approved versions, and ignorance of updates is not a defense. Every brokerage should have a system — automated or otherwise — that flags form version mismatches before documents reach the closing table. If your current workflow relies on memory, you already know [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in high-stakes environments.

Mistake three: failing to verify earnest money deposit timelines. Wyoming's Purchase and Sale Agreement specifies exact deadlines for earnest money delivery to the title company or escrow holder. Agents who let the deposit deadline slide without a formal amendment expose their clients to potential contract termination by the other party. Our guide on [Wyoming earnest money rules](/blog/wyoming-earnest-money-rules-2026) covers the deposit mechanics in detail.

Mistake four: assuming the title company will catch every contract error. Title companies review title, not your contract. If you wrote the wrong legal description into Form 1, used an incorrect vesting format, or left a contingency date blank, the title company may not flag it until the day of closing — or may not flag it at all. The agent owns the contract accuracy.

Mistake five: providing opinions on title defects. When a title commitment comes back with exceptions, agents sometimes tell clients those exceptions are "standard" or "nothing to worry about." That is a legal opinion. Direct the client to ask the title officer for clarification, or suggest they consult an attorney.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under W.S. 33-28-303 for the acts of their affiliated agents. This is not abstract risk. WREC can and does sanction brokers for systemic failures in oversight, even when the broker had no direct involvement in the transaction that triggered the complaint.

**Every Wyoming brokerage should audit three areas on a quarterly basis at minimum.** First, verify that all agents are using the current WREC-approved form versions. A single outdated form in a closed file is enough for a Commission citation. Second, review closing files for evidence that agency disclosure (Form 26) was delivered and signed before substantive negotiations began. Third, confirm that earnest money deposit receipts match the timelines stated in the Purchase and Sale Agreement.

Brokers also need to enforce a clear policy on what agents can and cannot say at the closing table. Put it in writing, include it in your office policy manual, and require agents to acknowledge it annually. The policy should state unambiguously that agents may not interpret legal documents, advise on title exceptions, or recommend for or against attorney representation.

|---|---|---|
| WREC form version check | Quarterly | Commission citation, deal rescission |
| Agency disclosure (Form 26) timing | Every transaction | License suspension, client lawsuit |
| Earnest money deposit verification | Every transaction | Contract termination, E&O claim |
| Closing table conduct review | Annually (policy) | Unauthorized practice of law complaint |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | License revocation under W.S. 33-28-303 |

## How Liability Breaks Down When Things Go Wrong

If an agent gives legal advice at closing, the consequences stack. WREC can impose fines, require additional continuing education, suspend, or revoke the license under W.S. 33-28-303. Simultaneously, the Wyoming State Bar can refer the matter as unauthorized practice of law, which is a separate proceeding. The agent's errors and omissions insurance may deny coverage for UPL claims, leaving the agent personally exposed to civil liability from the harmed party.

Deal cancellation is another real outcome. If a buyer discovers post-closing that the agent misrepresented a title exception or improperly advised them that attorney review was unnecessary, rescission claims follow. Wyoming courts have held that material misrepresentation by a licensee can support rescission even after recording, particularly when the buyer can demonstrate reliance.

Fines from WREC for form violations or supervision failures typically range from $750 to $5,000 per violation, though repeat offenders face steeper penalties. The Commission publishes disciplinary actions on its website, which means every fine is also a reputational hit that shows up in internet searches.
