## Understanding South Carolina Teams & Supervision Rules 2026

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission has sharpened its focus on team operations, and the South Carolina teams & supervision rules 2026 landscape demands more from agents and brokers than ever before. Whether you run a two-person partnership or a mega team with fifteen licensees, the regulatory framework governing how you advertise, supervise, and transact has real teeth. Agents who treat team compliance as an afterthought are discovering that fines, license suspensions, and voided transactions are not theoretical.

South Carolina's team regulations stem primarily from SC Code Title 40, Chapter 57, with additional guidance in SC Regulation 105-1 through 105-9. The [SC Real Estate Commission](https://llr.sc.gov/rec/) (a division of the Department of Labor, Licensing, and Regulation) enforces these rules and publishes periodic guidance bulletins that clarify expectations for team advertising, supervision hierarchies, and unlicensed assistant activities.

This post breaks down exactly what licensed agents and their brokers need to understand to stay compliant through the end of 2026.

## Team Formation and Registration Requirements

**Every real estate team in South Carolina must operate under the direct supervision of a broker-in-charge (BIC), and the team's existence must be documented at the brokerage level.** South Carolina does not require teams to register separately with the Commission as a distinct entity, but the BIC must maintain records showing team membership, advertising approvals, and supervisory protocols.

SC Code Section 40-57-137 establishes that all licensees must be affiliated with a licensed real estate broker and that the BIC holds responsibility for supervising all real estate activities conducted under the brokerage. This means team leaders cannot operate as de facto brokers without holding proper licensure. A team leader who is only licensed as a salesperson cannot independently supervise other agents, sign off on trust account disbursements, or approve advertising without BIC authorization.

The practical implication is straightforward: if you are forming a team, your broker-in-charge must sign off on the arrangement, and the BIC's supervisory role cannot be delegated to an unlicensed team leader or administrative manager.

## Advertising Rules That Trip Up Teams

Advertising violations account for a disproportionate share of disciplinary actions against South Carolina teams. SC Regulation 105-5 specifies that all advertising must include the name of the brokerage under which the licensee operates, and team names must not mislead the public into believing the team is a separate brokerage entity.

Here is how team advertising requirements compare to individual agent advertising in South Carolina:

| Requirement | Individual Agent | Team |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name required | Yes, prominently displayed | Yes, equal or greater prominence than team name |
| Team name alone permitted | N/A | No — must appear with brokerage name |
| Personal phone number allowed | Yes | Yes, but BIC must approve all ad content |
| "Realty" or "Real Estate" in team name | N/A | Prohibited unless team name IS the brokerage name |
| Social media compliance | Brokerage name in profile | Brokerage name in profile and team name subordinate |

The rule about using "Realty" or "Real Estate" in a team name catches many agents off guard. If your team is called "Palmetto Real Estate Group" but you operate under a different brokerage name, the Commission considers this misleading to consumers. The team name implies a separate brokerage, which violates SC Regulation 105-5.

All advertising — including yard signs, social media posts, business cards, and email signatures — must comply. The Commission does not differentiate between digital and print media for enforcement purposes.

## Supervision Standards the BIC Must Meet

**The broker-in-charge cannot passively oversee team operations; SC Regulation 105-1 requires active, meaningful supervision.** This means reviewing transactions, auditing files, and ensuring team members follow proper disclosure and agency procedures. A BIC who simply collects fees without engaging in actual oversight faces personal liability.

Specific BIC obligations for team supervision include reviewing all contracts within a reasonable timeframe (the Commission has informally interpreted this as within 48 hours of execution), ensuring proper use of SC Real Estate Commission forms, and verifying that all team advertising complies with state regulations before publication.

For transactions, agents in South Carolina must use Commission-approved forms or attorney-prepared documents. The [SC Residential Property Condition Disclosure Statement](https://llr.sc.gov/rec/) (required under SC Code Section 27-50-40) must be provided to buyers, and the team's internal workflow cannot override this statutory obligation. The BIC must verify that this form — along with agency disclosure forms — is properly executed in every transaction file.

## Unlicensed Assistants and the Boundaries They Cannot Cross

South Carolina draws a firm line between administrative tasks and activities that require licensure. Under SC Code Section 40-57-20, practicing real estate without a license is a misdemeanor. Teams that allow unlicensed assistants to cross this line expose themselves to criminal liability, not just administrative discipline.

Unlicensed assistants on a team may perform the following: scheduling appointments, assembling documents for closing, placing signs on property with permission, and answering factual questions about listing data already published in MLS. They may not discuss pricing strategy, negotiate terms, show properties independently, explain contract provisions to clients, or hold themselves out as licensed agents.

The distinction matters enormously for teams that rely on transaction coordinators or administrative support staff. If your [transaction coordinator is unlicensed](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator), their role must remain strictly administrative within South Carolina's regulatory framework. Even a single instance of an unlicensed person advising a client on contract terms can trigger Commission investigation.

## Common Mistakes South Carolina Teams Make

**Three to five recurring errors surface in Commission disciplinary records year after year, and most are entirely preventable.** Understanding these patterns can save your team thousands of dollars and months of license jeopardy.

First, team leaders operating as unsupervised quasi-brokers. When a team leader — licensed only as a salesperson — begins approving advertising, handling trust account questions, or signing off on other agents' transactions, the BIC has effectively abdicated their statutory duty. The Commission has disciplined both the team leader (for exceeding their license authority) and the BIC (for failure to supervise) in these cases.

Second, failing to update team membership changes with the brokerage. When agents join or leave a team, the BIC must adjust supervisory records, update advertising materials, and ensure departing agents do not continue using the team name. SC Code Section 40-57-110 requires license transfer paperwork within ten days of affiliation changes, yet teams regularly miss this window.

Third, commingling team operating expenses with the brokerage trust account. Team leaders who deposit personal marketing funds into the trust account — or who direct earnest money to a team-specific account not held by the brokerage — violate SC Code Section 40-57-135(B). Trust account violations carry fines of up to $5,000 and potential license revocation.

Fourth, inconsistent agency disclosure at the team level. When multiple team members interact with the same client, confusion about who disclosed what — and when — creates liability gaps. The Commission expects agency disclosure (using the SC Agency Disclosure Form) at first substantive contact, regardless of which team member initiates that contact.

Fifth, using out-of-state forms or generic templates instead of Commission-approved or attorney-prepared documents. South Carolina is an attorney-closing state, and contract forms must comply with state law. Teams pulling forms from national franchises without verifying South Carolina compliance risk deal cancellation and malpractice claims.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers who oversee teams should implement a systematic audit program rather than relying on spot checks or complaints to surface problems. The following areas demand regular review.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | Monthly | All active ads include brokerage name, team name not misleading |
| Transaction files | Every closing | Agency disclosure signed at first contact, property condition disclosure present, proper forms used |
| Trust account | Weekly | No commingling, proper deposits within 48 hours per SC Code 40-57-135 |
| License status | Quarterly | All team members active, CE current, no pending disciplinary actions |
| Unlicensed assistant activities | Ongoing | No negotiation, no contract advice, no independent showings |

The BIC should maintain a written supervision policy specific to each team operating under the brokerage. This document should define what the team leader is authorized to do, what requires BIC approval, and how files will be reviewed. When the Commission investigates a complaint, the existence (or absence) of a written supervision policy weighs heavily in determining whether the BIC exercised reasonable oversight.

Documentation also protects the brokerage in civil litigation. If a team member's error leads to a buyer or seller lawsuit, the broker's defense depends on showing that supervisory systems existed and were actively enforced. A policy that sits in a drawer unreviewed is worse than having no policy — it demonstrates awareness of the duty without follow-through.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The penalties for violating team supervision rules in South Carolina range from informal warnings to criminal prosecution, depending on severity. The Commission can impose fines up to $1,000 per violation for advertising infractions under SC Code Section 40-57-145. Trust account violations carry fines up to $5,000. License suspension or revocation is available for repeated offenses or egregious conduct.

Beyond administrative penalties, agents face civil liability. If a team's failure to properly disclose agency relationships causes financial harm to a consumer, the injured party can pursue damages against the individual agent, the team leader, and the brokerage jointly. South Carolina courts have upheld vicarious liability claims against brokerages for team member misconduct where supervision was inadequate.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a buyer discovers that the person who "negotiated" their purchase was actually an unlicensed team assistant, the contract may be voidable. The buyer could rescind the agreement, demand return of earnest money, and pursue damages for costs incurred during the transaction. These situations are precisely [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) — not from market forces, but from avoidable compliance failures.
