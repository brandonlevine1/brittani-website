## Why Vermont Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026 Deserve Your Full Attention

Vermont is one of a handful of states where environmental history can tank a deal faster than a failed septic inspection. Understanding **Vermont property condition & environmental disclosures 2026** requirements is not optional knowledge — it is the baseline for practicing competently in this state. Missing a single disclosure obligation can expose you, your seller, and your brokerage to rescission claims, civil liability, and disciplinary action from the Vermont Real Estate Commission.

The state's disclosure framework sits primarily within Title 27, Chapter 5, Subchapter 3 of the Vermont Statutes (27 V.S.A. Sections 301-304). These statutes require sellers of residential real property to provide written disclosures about the physical condition of the property and known environmental hazards. Federal overlay adds lead-based paint requirements for pre-1978 homes and FIRPTA considerations for foreign sellers, but Vermont's own statutory scheme carries the heavier day-to-day compliance burden for practicing agents.

This post covers the specific forms, statutes, and liability risks that Vermont agents and brokers must understand heading into 2026. It also addresses the mistakes that keep showing up in commission complaints and the audit steps brokers should already have in place.

## The Statutory Framework: What Vermont Law Actually Requires

**Vermont's seller disclosure statute, 27 V.S.A. Section 302, mandates that sellers of residential property provide a written property disclosure report to prospective buyers before the buyer becomes obligated under a purchase and sale agreement.** The disclosure must cover known material defects affecting the property's value or desirability. Vermont law does not require sellers to perform inspections — only to disclose what they actually know.

The statute enumerates specific categories that must be addressed, including structural systems, heating and plumbing, water supply, sewage disposal, environmental hazards, and boundary disputes. Sellers must also disclose the presence of underground storage tanks, known contamination, radon test results, and any hazardous materials on or near the property. Vermont's emphasis on environmental categories is stronger than in many neighboring states, reflecting the state's regulatory posture toward land use and ecological protection.

Exemptions under the statute are narrow. Court-ordered transfers, foreclosure sales, transfers between spouses or co-owners, and sales by estates where the personal representative has no occupancy knowledge are generally exempt. Every other residential sale triggers the disclosure obligation.

## Forms in Play: What Vermont Agents Must Use

The Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) publishes the standard Property Disclosure Report that most agents use statewide. This form tracks the statutory requirements of 27 V.S.A. Section 302 and covers roughly two dozen categories of property condition. Agents should verify they are using the most current version of this form, as VAR periodically updates it to reflect legislative changes or commission guidance.

| Form / Document | Source | When It's Required |
|---|---|---|
| VAR Property Disclosure Report | Vermont Association of Realtors | All residential sales (unless exempt) |
| EPA Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (EPA Form) | U.S. EPA / HUD | Pre-1978 residential properties |
| Seller's Disclosure of Lead Paint Knowledge | Federal (42 U.S.C. 4852d) | Pre-1978 residential properties |
| Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Disclosure | State / Municipal | Properties with on-site septic or private wells |
| Act 250 Land Use Permit Disclosure | Vermont Natural Resources Board | Properties subject to Act 250 permits |

For properties built before 1978, agents must also ensure compliance with the federal [Lead-Based Paint Disclosure Rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/sellers-disclosure-rule-background). This means providing the EPA pamphlet ("Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home"), obtaining the seller's written disclosure of known lead hazards, and giving the buyer a 10-day opportunity to conduct a lead inspection. Vermont agents sometimes treat this as a formality. It is not — it carries its own penalty structure entirely separate from state law.

Act 250 disclosures deserve special attention. Vermont's Act 250 (10 V.S.A. Chapter 151) imposes land use permits on developments meeting certain thresholds. If a property is subject to an Act 250 permit, the seller must disclose the permit's conditions because they run with the land. Failure to disclose permit conditions — which may restrict everything from commercial use to the number of allowable residential units — is a frequent source of post-closing disputes.

## Vermont Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026: Liability When You Get It Wrong

**The consequences of non-disclosure in Vermont split into three lanes: civil liability for the seller, disciplinary exposure for the agent, and deal-level remedies for the buyer.** Understanding all three is essential because agents often assume the seller carries all the risk. That assumption is wrong.

Under 27 V.S.A. Section 304, a buyer who does not receive the required disclosure before becoming obligated may rescind the purchase agreement. This right of rescission is statutory — it does not depend on proving the seller acted in bad faith. If the disclosure simply was not provided on time, the buyer has grounds to walk. In practice, this means a missed disclosure can kill a deal even when the property has no actual defects.

For agents, the Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC) has authority under 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41 to investigate complaints and impose sanctions ranging from fines to license suspension or revocation. VREC treats disclosure failures as potential violations of the agent's duty to conduct transactions with competence and in compliance with applicable law. An agent who knew or should have known that a disclosure was missing — and failed to act — faces personal disciplinary exposure regardless of what the seller did or did not do.

Civil liability can also extend to agents under common law fraud or negligent misrepresentation theories. If an agent actively conceals a known defect or discourages a seller from disclosing, courts in Vermont have recognized agent liability to the buyer. The financial exposure is not limited to the purchase price — consequential damages, remediation costs, and attorney's fees can all enter the picture.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make on Disclosures

Patterns emerge from VREC complaint files and closing disputes. The same errors appear year after year, and most of them are entirely preventable with basic process discipline.

**First, agents routinely deliver the Property Disclosure Report too late in the transaction.** The statute requires disclosure before the buyer becomes obligated. Handing the form over at the closing table — or even a day before closing — does not satisfy the statutory timing requirement and preserves the buyer's rescission right. Agents should obtain the completed disclosure before listing or, at the latest, before executing the purchase and sale agreement.

Second, agents fail to flag incomplete disclosures. Sellers frequently leave blanks on the VAR Property Disclosure Report, particularly in environmental categories like underground storage tanks or known contamination. A blank is not a "no." It is an unanswered question that creates ambiguity and potential liability. Agents have a duty to review the form, identify gaps, and push the seller to complete every field — even if the answer is "unknown."

Third, agents overlook Act 250 permit conditions. Vermont properties subject to Act 250 permits carry deed-recorded conditions that may restrict use, subdivision, or development. Agents who do not check whether an Act 250 permit exists — and whether its conditions are disclosed — leave their buyers exposed to restrictions that may fundamentally change the property's value. A title search alone does not always surface these conditions in a way that buyers understand.

Fourth, agents treat lead paint disclosure as a checkbox exercise and forget to document the buyer's 10-day inspection opportunity. The federal rule requires written acknowledgment that the buyer received the opportunity — not just the pamphlet. Missing this documentation creates liability even if the buyer never intended to inspect. Similar documentation issues arise when agents [miss deadlines on contingency-related items](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), which remains one of the most common process failures across all states.

Fifth, agents neglect to disclose known environmental conditions on adjacent properties. Vermont's disclosure form asks about conditions that may affect the property, which can include contamination on neighboring parcels. If an agent knows a property sits near a state-listed contaminated site — searchable through the Vermont Agency of Natural Resources database — silence is not an acceptable posture.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Vermont carry supervisory obligations under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296, which requires them to exercise reasonable supervision over the activities of their licensees. Disclosure compliance is one of the highest-risk areas for brokerage liability, and brokers who treat it as an agent-level problem are making a mistake.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Property Disclosure Report completion | All fields answered; form is current version | Every listing, before MLS entry |
| Lead paint disclosure (pre-1978) | EPA form signed; buyer's 10-day acknowledgment documented | Every applicable transaction |
| Act 250 permit status | Permit existence checked; conditions disclosed if applicable | Every transaction involving land or development |
| Timing of disclosure delivery | Disclosure delivered before buyer obligation | Every transaction, verified at contract execution |
| Environmental database cross-reference | Vermont ANR site check for adjacent contamination | Recommended for all transactions |

**Brokers should build disclosure review into their transaction file audit at two checkpoints: listing intake and contract execution.** Waiting until the pre-closing file review is too late to catch a missing or incomplete disclosure. At that point, the statutory timing requirement has already been blown, and the buyer's rescission right is live.

Transaction coordinators play a significant role here. A well-run TC process catches disclosure gaps before they become legal problems. If your brokerage lacks a systematic approach to [tracking documents and deadlines](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), disclosure compliance will remain inconsistent no matter how many training sessions you hold.

Brokers should also maintain a written policy specifying that no property goes active on MLS without a completed Property Disclosure Report in the file. This single policy change eliminates the most common timing violation and creates an auditable standard that protects the brokerage in commission investigations.

## Environmental Disclosures: Vermont's Unique Exposure

Vermont's environmental regulatory framework creates disclosure obligations that agents in other states rarely encounter. The state's emphasis on groundwater protection, Act 250 land use permitting, and wetland regulation means that environmental conditions frequently affect residential transactions — not just commercial or agricultural deals.

Underground storage tanks (USTs) are a recurring issue. Vermont's UST program, administered by the Department of Environmental Conservation, maintains a registry of known tanks. Sellers must disclose known USTs, but agents should go further and check the state's database proactively. A disclosed but leaking UST is a negotiation issue. An undisclosed UST discovered after closing is a lawsuit.

Septic and water supply disclosures carry extra weight in Vermont because so many properties rely on private wells and on-site wastewater systems. Vermont's Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Rules (effective under 10 V.S.A. Chapter 64) impose permitting requirements that run with the property. Agents must confirm whether a valid wastewater permit exists and whether the system has been modified without a permit. These are not edge cases — they arise in a significant percentage of rural Vermont transactions.

Radon is another area where Vermont pushes beyond the national baseline. While federal law does not require radon disclosure, Vermont's Property Disclosure Report includes radon as a specific category. If the seller has radon test results, they must disclose them. Agents who advise sellers to omit known radon data are creating liability for themselves and their brokerage.
