# North Dakota Title & Closing Procedures 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Understanding **North Dakota title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional knowledge for agents operating in the state — it is the backbone of every deal that reaches the finish line. North Dakota's closing process is shaped by a distinct combination of state statutes, title industry customs that vary county by county, and regulatory oversight that carries real teeth. Agents who treat the title-to-close phase as someone else's problem are the ones who end up explaining to their clients why a deal fell apart three days before the moving truck arrived.

This post breaks down what licensed agents and their brokers need to know right now about title commitments, closing mechanics, regulatory exposure, and the specific errors that keep tanking otherwise solid transactions across the state.

## How North Dakota Structures the Closing Process

North Dakota is not an attorney-required state for real estate closings. Most residential transactions close through a title company or an abstractor working alongside a closing agent. The [North Dakota Insurance Department](https://www.insurance.nd.gov/) regulates title insurance companies and agents under North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 26.1-20, which governs title insurance practices, rates, and agent licensing.

The standard flow moves from executed purchase agreement to title commitment to closing disclosure to the settlement table. Title companies issue a commitment for title insurance — sometimes still called a "title binder" locally — that reveals the condition of title, any encumbrances, and the requirements the company sets before it will insure. Agents need to review this document, not just forward it to the buyer's attorney or lender.

**North Dakota is also an abstract state, meaning many counties still rely on abstracts of title rather than a pure title insurance model.** In western counties especially, an updated abstract may be required before a title opinion is issued by an attorney. This dual-track system catches out-of-state agents and even some in-state agents who have only worked in Fargo or Bismarck markets, where title insurance commitments are more standard.

## Key Statutes and Forms Agents Must Know

Several NDCC provisions directly affect how agents handle the title and closing phase. NDCC 47-10 governs recording of real property instruments, setting out the requirements for deeds, mortgages, and other conveyances to be properly recorded with the county recorder. NDCC 47-19-01 through 47-19-46 detail the recording statutes, including the "race-notice" system North Dakota follows — meaning a subsequent good-faith purchaser who records first takes priority.

For the purchase agreement itself, most agents use the North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR) Purchase Agreement form. The 2026 edition includes updated language on title objection deadlines and the buyer's right to review the title commitment within a specified number of days after receipt. Agents should also be familiar with NDAR Form 101 (Residential Purchase Agreement), NDAR Form 201 (Counter Proposal), and NDAR Form 301 (Amendment to Purchase Agreement), as errors in these forms directly affect title and closing timelines.

At closing, the federal Closing Disclosure (CD) under TRID rules applies to all federally related mortgage loans. North Dakota does not impose a separate state-specific closing statement requirement, but agents need to verify that the CD matches the terms of the purchase agreement. Discrepancies between the CD and the contract — especially on closing cost allocations and prorations — are a leading source of last-minute delays.

| Document | Who Prepares It | Agent's Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Title Commitment | Title company or abstractor | Review for defects; relay objections within deadline |
| Abstract of Title | County abstractor | Ensure it is ordered promptly; confirm seller provides updated abstract if required |
| NDAR Form 101 (Purchase Agreement) | Listing or buyer's agent | Accurate completion of all title and closing terms |
| Closing Disclosure | Lender or closing agent | Verify consistency with purchase agreement terms |
| Warranty Deed | Title company or attorney | Confirm names, legal description, and recording information |

## Title Objections and Curing Defects

**The title objection period is one of the most mismanaged phases of any North Dakota transaction.** Under the standard NDAR purchase agreement, the buyer typically has a set number of calendar days after receiving the title commitment or abstract to submit written objections. If the buyer fails to object within that window, they are generally deemed to have accepted the condition of title.

Common defects include unreleased mortgages, mineral rights reservations that were not disclosed, judgment liens, and boundary disputes revealed by surveys. In western North Dakota, mineral rights are a particularly loaded issue. NDCC 47-10-24 addresses dormant mineral interests, and the state's Mineral Resources Act (NDCC 38-18.1) affects whether severed mineral rights have been properly maintained. Agents who do not flag mineral reservations on a title commitment risk blindsiding their buyer clients.

When a defect is identified, the seller is given a reasonable period to cure it. If the defect cannot be cured — say, a cloud on title from a decades-old estate that was never probated — the buyer can typically terminate the agreement and recover their earnest money. Agents should document every objection and cure response in writing, ideally using NDAR Form 301 to amend the agreement with any resolution terms. ## North Dakota Title & Closing Procedures 2026: Common Agent Mistakes

Five errors account for the majority of agent-caused closing problems in North Dakota. Each one is preventable.

**First, agents fail to order the title commitment early enough.** The purchase agreement is signed, and the agent waits a week before contacting the title company. In rural counties where the abstractor may serve multiple jurisdictions, that delay can push the title review past the objection deadline, leaving the buyer with no contractual remedy for defects discovered late.

Second, agents misidentify who pays for what at closing. North Dakota custom varies by market. In Fargo-Moorhead, the seller customarily pays for the owner's title insurance policy. In other markets, the split differs. When agents assume rather than specify in the purchase agreement, it creates disputes at the closing table that could have been resolved weeks earlier.

Third, agents neglect to verify legal descriptions. A legal description on the purchase agreement that does not match the deed of record or the county GIS can delay recording, invalidate the conveyance, or trigger a title company's refusal to insure. Copying and pasting from an MLS listing is not the same as confirming the legal description with the county recorder's records.

Fourth, agents forget to confirm earnest money deposit timelines. Under NDCC 43-23-08, brokers must deposit earnest money into a trust account in accordance with the terms of the purchase agreement. Late deposits — or deposits that never make it to the trust account — expose the broker to disciplinary action by the North Dakota Real Estate Commission and can give the opposing party grounds to cancel the contract.

Fifth, agents do not review the Closing Disclosure against the purchase agreement before closing day. This is not a task that should be delegated entirely to the lender or closing agent. Discrepancies in prorations, seller concessions, or closing cost credits that conflict with the executed agreement will delay closing and damage client trust. If [missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are a recurring issue in your practice, the CD review step is often where the breakdown begins.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in North Dakota carry supervisory responsibility under NDCC 43-23 and the rules of the North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC). This is not abstract liability. If an agent under your supervision mishandles trust funds, misrepresents the title status, or fails to disclose a known defect, the Commission can pursue action against the broker's license — not just the agent's.

| Broker Audit Item | Why It Matters | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Earnest money deposit verification | NDCC 43-23-08 compliance; trust account integrity | Every transaction |
| Title commitment review documentation | Confirms agent reviewed and relayed objections within deadline | Every transaction |
| Purchase agreement accuracy | Legal description, closing cost allocations, contingency dates | Every transaction |
| Closing Disclosure reconciliation | Catches discrepancies before closing day | Every financed transaction |
| File completion after closing | Ensures recorded deed, final settlement statement, and disclosures are archived | Within 30 days of closing |

**Brokers should implement a transaction file checklist that specifically includes a title commitment review timestamp and a CD comparison sign-off.** Relying on agents to self-audit is how compliance gaps develop. The NDREC has the authority under NDCC 43-23-11.1 to investigate complaints, conduct audits, and impose penalties ranging from fines to license revocation. Post-closing file retention is required for a minimum of three years, though many firms retain records longer as a defensive measure.

If your brokerage lacks a consistent system for tracking these items across agents, that is a structural risk — not just an operational inconvenience. The difference between a clean audit and a Commission investigation often comes down to whether someone verified the file before it was closed and shelved. ## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

Agents who skip steps in the title and closing process face more than a scolding from their broker. The NDREC can impose civil penalties, require additional education, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely. Under NDCC 43-23-11.1, grounds for disciplinary action include failure to account for or remit funds, making misleading statements in a transaction, and demonstrating unworthiness or incompetence.

Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face civil liability. A buyer who purchases a property with an undisclosed lien because the agent failed to review or communicate the title commitment findings may have a negligence claim. Errors and omissions insurance covers many of these scenarios, but carriers can deny coverage if the agent's conduct was grossly negligent or fell outside the scope of their duties.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate consequence. When title issues surface late because the agent did not manage the timeline, the transaction often dies. Sellers lose market time. Buyers lose their rate lock. Both sides lose confidence in the agent. None of that is recoverable.
