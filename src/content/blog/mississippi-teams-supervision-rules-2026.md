# Mississippi Teams & Supervision Rules 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Know

The phrase **Mississippi teams & supervision rules 2026** carries more weight this year than it has in the past decade. The Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) has sharpened its enforcement posture on team advertising, delegation of supervisory duties, and the line between licensed and unlicensed activity within teams. Agents who formed teams casually—picking a name, printing business cards, splitting leads—are now finding that the regulatory framework demands far more structure than a handshake and a shared CRM.

This matters because Mississippi treats the supervising broker as the single point of accountability for every licensee operating under that brokerage, including team members who may feel semi-autonomous. The consequences of getting this wrong range from fines to license revocation to transaction rescission.

## The Statutory Foundation: Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-1 et seq.

Mississippi's licensing law is codified in Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-1 through 73-35-39. The MREC derives its rulemaking authority from Section 73-35-35, which grants the Commission power to adopt rules governing licensee conduct, advertising, and supervisory obligations. Teams are not separately licensed entities in Mississippi—they exist only as marketing arrangements under a responsible broker's umbrella.

**Section 73-35-3 defines who qualifies as a "broker" with supervisory authority.** A salesperson licensee, regardless of production volume or years of experience, cannot independently supervise other licensees. This distinction trips up high-producing team leaders who assume that writing checks and assigning leads equals supervisory control.

The MREC's [Rules and Regulations](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/rules-regulations/) elaborate on these statutory requirements, particularly Rule 3.2 (Advertising) and Rule 3.1 (Supervisory Responsibilities). Both were updated in late 2025 with language specifically addressing team structures.

## Advertising Rules: Where Most Teams First Go Wrong

Rule 3.2 requires that all advertising by a licensee include the name of the responsible broker or brokerage firm. For teams, this creates a layered disclosure obligation. The team name may appear, but it cannot be presented in a way that suggests the team is a standalone brokerage. The brokerage name must be at least as prominent as—or more prominent than—the team name in all media.

This rule applies to yard signs, social media profiles, email signatures, printed materials, and any digital presence. The MREC has issued cease-and-desist letters to teams whose Instagram bios listed only a team name without the brokerage identifier. Fines under Rule 3.2 violations can reach $1,000 per occurrence, and repeated violations trigger a formal hearing process that can result in license suspension.

**Mississippi Form MREC-7 (Advertising Disclosure Certification) must be signed by the responsible broker acknowledging review and approval of all team advertising materials.** Teams that change their branding, add social media accounts, or launch new websites must obtain updated broker approval each time. This form sits in the broker's file, not the team's file.

## Supervision Duties: What the Broker Actually Owes

The responsible broker's supervisory obligation in Mississippi is not a passive checkbox. Under Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21 and MREC Rule 3.1, the broker must maintain "direct supervision" over all licensees, which the Commission has interpreted to include reviewing contracts, monitoring trust account activity, and verifying that team members are not engaging in unlicensed activity.

For teams specifically, this means the broker must know what unlicensed assistants are doing on a daily basis. Mississippi draws a hard line: unlicensed team members may perform purely administrative tasks (scheduling appointments, placing signs, assembling documents at a licensee's direction) but may not negotiate, advise on pricing, show property independently, or draft contract terms.

| Activity | Licensed Required? | Form/Statute Reference |
|----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Showing property to a buyer | Yes | Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-3 |
| Negotiating price or terms | Yes | Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-3 |
| Scheduling appointments | No | MREC Rule 3.1 Commentary |
| Placing/removing yard signs | No | MREC Rule 3.1 Commentary |
| Drafting contract addenda | Yes | Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-3 |
| Hosting an open house alone | Yes | MREC Advisory Opinion 2024-3 |
| Distributing pre-printed materials | No | MREC Rule 3.1 Commentary |

When a broker delegates day-to-day oversight to a team leader (who holds a broker associate license), that delegation must be documented in writing using MREC Form 8 (Delegation of Supervisory Authority). Without this form on file, the responsible broker retains full personal liability for any team misconduct, and the team leader has no legal authority to supervise.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Teams Make

Mistakes in this area rarely stem from bad intent. They stem from agents importing practices they saw work in other states, or from teams growing faster than their compliance infrastructure. Here are the five errors the MREC flags most frequently in disciplinary proceedings.

**First, team leaders with salesperson licenses acting as de facto supervisors.** This happens when a high-producing agent recruits buyer's agents, assigns them leads, reviews their contracts, and provides coaching—all without holding a broker's license or having written delegation from the responsible broker. The MREC views this as unlicensed brokerage activity by the team leader.

Second, failing to use the correct agency disclosure form (MREC Form 1, Consumer Agency Disclosure) at the team level. When a team has both listing-side and buyer-side agents, consumers sometimes interact with multiple team members without receiving proper disclosure about who represents whom. Each consumer must receive Form 1 at first substantive contact, regardless of whether another team member previously interacted with them in a different capacity.

Third, commingling team "operating accounts" with trust funds. Some teams maintain a shared bank account for marketing expenses and mistakenly route earnest money or security deposits through it. Mississippi's trust account rules under Section 73-35-21(c) are strict: all client funds must go directly into the broker's trust account. A team-level bank account is never an acceptable substitute. [Understanding where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with these kinds of trust account errors.

Fourth, teams advertising under a DBA or LLC that is not registered with the MREC. Mississippi requires that any trade name used in real estate advertising be registered with the Commission. Teams operating under catchy brand names without filing the appropriate paperwork face advertising violations.

Fifth, inconsistent record retention. MREC Rule 4.1 requires transaction records to be retained for three years. Teams that maintain their own filing systems—separate from the broker's files—create gaps that surface during MREC audits. The broker is ultimately responsible for record retention, and teams that keep independent systems make it nearly impossible for brokers to fulfill this duty.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers who allow teams to operate under their license accept heightened liability. The MREC has made clear through enforcement actions in 2024 and 2025 that "I didn't know what my team was doing" is not a viable defense. Proactive auditing is the only protection.

**Every quarter, brokers should review all team advertising across every platform.** This includes checking that MREC Form 7 is current, that brokerage names appear correctly, and that no unlicensed team members are identified in ways that suggest licensure. A single Instagram story that omits the brokerage name can trigger a complaint.

Brokers must also verify that MREC Form 8 is on file if they have delegated any supervisory authority to a team leader holding a broker associate license. This form should specify exactly which duties are delegated—contract review, trust account oversight, training—and which remain with the responsible broker. Vague delegation is treated as no delegation at all.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Key Document |
|------------|-----------|-------------|
| Advertising compliance | Quarterly minimum | MREC Form 7 |
| Supervisory delegation | Annually or upon team changes | MREC Form 8 |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Broker trust account ledger |
| Agency disclosure delivery | Per transaction | MREC Form 1 |
| Unlicensed activity review | Quarterly | Team roster vs. MREC license lookup |
| Record retention verification | Semi-annually | Transaction files (3-year check) |

Trust account reconciliation deserves special attention. Brokers should confirm monthly that no earnest money or client funds have been routed to team-level accounts. Mississippi's disciplinary records show that trust account violations result in some of the harshest penalties—immediate license suspension pending hearing, with potential revocation. ## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The penalty structure in Mississippi escalates quickly. A first-time advertising violation may result in a warning letter or a fine up to $1,000. Repeated violations, particularly those involving consumer harm or trust account mishandling, can result in fines up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory continuing education, license suspension (typically 30-180 days), or outright revocation.

Beyond MREC discipline, non-compliance creates civil liability. A buyer or seller who discovers that their "agent" was actually an unlicensed team assistant performing licensed activities may have grounds to void the contract entirely. Mississippi courts have recognized rescission as an available remedy when licensure requirements are violated, creating enormous financial exposure for the team, the broker, and potentially the other party's brokerage.

**Errors and omissions insurance policies frequently exclude coverage for regulatory violations and unlicensed activity.** This means that when a team member crosses the line, neither the team leader nor the broker may have insurance to cover resulting claims. The financial exposure is personal and direct.

## Building a Compliant Team Structure in 2026

The path forward is not complicated—it simply requires intentionality. Start with a written team agreement that defines each member's role, specifies whether they hold a salesperson or broker associate license, and identifies exactly which activities unlicensed staff may perform. Have the responsible broker sign it.

File all required forms with the MREC before the team begins operating. Register any trade names. Complete Form 7 for advertising approval and Form 8 if supervisory delegation applies.
