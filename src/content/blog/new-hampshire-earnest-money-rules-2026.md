## Understanding New Hampshire Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

New Hampshire earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry specific statutory requirements that trip up even experienced agents. The state's regulatory framework, governed primarily by RSA 331-A and the administrative rules issued by the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), imposes strict timelines, recordkeeping standards, and fiduciary duties around the handling of client funds. Failing to comply doesn't just jeopardize a deal — it puts your license at risk.

The stakes are high because New Hampshire treats escrow violations as a serious breach of fiduciary duty. Unlike some states where a slap on the wrist is common for first offenses, NHREC has consistently demonstrated willingness to suspend licenses over deposit mishandling. This article breaks down the statutes, forms, common pitfalls, and broker-level responsibilities that matter most in 2026.

## The Statutory Framework: RSA 331-A and NHREC Rules

**New Hampshire's escrow requirements are rooted in RSA 331-A:25-b, which mandates that all earnest money deposits received by a licensee be placed in an escrow account maintained by the principal broker.** The statute does not permit individual agents to hold funds in personal accounts or office operating accounts under any circumstances. Violations constitute grounds for disciplinary action under RSA 331-A:28.

The NHREC administrative rules provide the operational details. Rule Rea 701.01 establishes the five-business-day deposit window, meaning brokers must deposit earnest money within five business days of the licensee receiving the funds. Rule Rea 701.02 outlines the recordkeeping requirements, including transaction-by-transaction ledger entries and monthly reconciliation of all escrow accounts.

Rule Rea 701.03 governs the disbursement side. Funds cannot be released from escrow until one of three conditions is met: closing occurs, all parties provide written authorization for release, or a court of competent jurisdiction orders disbursement. There is no unilateral right for either buyer or seller to demand release without the other's consent — a point that generates disputes regularly.

## Forms Agents Must Know

New Hampshire agents working under the New Hampshire Association of Realtors (NHAR) typically use standardized transaction forms. The key forms related to earnest money include:

| Form Number | Form Name | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| NHAR Form 51 | Purchase and Sale Agreement | Defines earnest money amount, deposit deadline, and escrow holder |
| NHAR Form 52 | Earnest Money Addendum | Modifies deposit terms after initial agreement |
| NHAR Form 68 | Release of Earnest Money | Authorizes disbursement when a deal falls through |
| NHAR Form 90 | Escrow Account Disclosure | Notifies parties of where funds are held |

**Form 68 is the one agents most frequently need — and most frequently mishandle.** When a transaction falls apart, both buyer and seller must sign Form 68 before the broker can release the deposit. Agents who verbally agree to release funds without obtaining dual signatures expose their broker to liability.

The purchase and sale agreement (Form 51) includes specific provisions for the earnest money amount, the timeframe for delivery, and the identity of the escrow holder. Agents must ensure these fields are completed accurately because ambiguity in the contract creates disputes that NHREC ultimately has to mediate — and the agent who drafted the unclear terms is the one who draws scrutiny.

## Deposit Timelines and Compliance Triggers

The five-business-day deposit rule under Rea 701.01 starts ticking when the licensee (not the broker) receives the check, wire, or other payment instrument. If a buyer's agent receives a check on Monday, the broker has until the following Monday to deposit it — assuming no holidays intervene. The clock does not pause over weekends for counting purposes, but only business days count.

A common source of confusion: the five-day window applies to receipt by any licensee in the brokerage, not just the principal broker. If a showing agent accepts a check at an open house on Saturday, that Saturday is day one (assuming the brokerage is open). Brokers who rely on the excuse that "the check sat on the agent's desk" will not find sympathy from NHREC investigators.

Wire transfers and electronic payments have their own wrinkles. The [NHREC's position](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) is that electronic funds must be deposited into escrow within the same five-day window, even though wires technically clear faster. The commission has not issued separate guidance for cryptocurrency or stablecoin payments, so agents should default to traditional payment methods until formal rulemaking addresses digital assets.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

RSA 331-A:28 grants NHREC broad authority to discipline licensees for escrow violations. Penalties include license suspension, license revocation, fines of up to $2,000 per violation, and mandatory completion of additional education. The commission can also require restitution to harmed parties.

Beyond administrative penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money was improperly disbursed can sue the agent and broker for conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, or negligence. New Hampshire courts have held that brokers are vicariously liable for their agents' escrow mishandling, even when the broker had no direct knowledge of the violation — a principle that makes broker oversight essential.

Criminal exposure is rare but possible. Deliberate misappropriation of escrow funds constitutes theft under RSA 637:3. Prosecutors in New Hampshire have pursued criminal charges in cases involving agents who diverted client deposits to personal use. Even a single incident can result in felony charges if the amount exceeds $1,500.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Earnest Money

Three to five errors appear repeatedly in NHREC disciplinary records and broker audits. Understanding these patterns is the fastest way to avoid them.

**First, agents accept earnest money checks made payable to themselves rather than the escrow account or brokerage.** This creates a deposit-path problem and raises red flags during audits. All checks should be made payable to the brokerage's escrow account by name, exactly as it appears on the bank records. Agents who accept improperly addressed checks must immediately notify their broker and arrange correct handling.

Second, agents release deposits based on verbal authorization from one party. When a deal collapses, the listing agent sometimes tells the buyer's agent that "the seller said to release the deposit." Without a signed Form 68 from both parties, the broker cannot lawfully disburse. Agents who pressure their broker to release funds based on informal communications put the entire office at risk.

Third, agents fail to document the date of receipt. NHREC investigators count backward from the deposit date shown on bank records. If the deposit occurred on day seven but the agent claims they received the check on day four, the absence of a documented receipt date creates an inference of late deposit. Smart agents photograph or scan checks immediately upon receipt with a timestamp, similar to best practices around [deadline tracking in other states](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Fourth, agents commingle earnest money with option fees or other payments in their notes to the broker, creating confusion about which funds belong in escrow versus operating accounts. Each payment type must be identified separately in the transmittal to the broker.

Fifth, agents forget to update escrow holders when transactions move from one title company or attorney to another. If the purchase agreement names Attorney A as escrow holder but funds remain in the brokerage account because Attorney A never received them, the broker is holding funds beyond the authorized period.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in New Hampshire bear ultimate responsibility for escrow compliance. NHREC Rule Rea 701.04 requires monthly reconciliation of all escrow accounts, and brokers must retain these reconciliation records for a minimum of three years. An incomplete or missing reconciliation is itself a violation, regardless of whether any funds were actually mishandled.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Escrow account reconciliation | Monthly | Rea 701.04 |
| Individual transaction ledger review | Per transaction | Rea 701.02 |
| Agent receipt-date documentation | Per deposit | Rea 701.01 |
| Form 68 verification before disbursement | Per cancellation | RSA 331-A:25-b |
| Escrow account bank statement retention | 3 years minimum | Rea 701.04 |

**Brokers should treat escrow compliance as a non-delegable duty.** While a transaction coordinator or office manager may perform the physical reconciliation, the principal broker is personally liable for errors. NHREC has sanctioned brokers who claimed ignorance of their agents' conduct, holding that the duty to supervise is absolute under RSA 331-A:25-a.

One enforcement trend worth watching: NHREC has increased random audit frequency since 2024. Brokers who previously went years without examination are now seeing desk audits with less than 30 days' notice. Having a system that tracks receipt dates, deposit confirmations, and disbursement authorizations in real time is no longer optional — it's a survival requirement. ## Disputed Deposits and Interpleader Actions

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the earnest money, New Hampshire brokers face a dilemma. They cannot disburse to either party without dual consent, but they also cannot hold funds indefinitely without a resolution path. The standard remedy is an interpleader action — a court filing that asks a judge to determine rightful ownership.

RSA 331-A:25-b implicitly authorizes brokers to file interpleader by prohibiting disbursement absent mutual consent or court order. The broker deposits the disputed funds with the court, names both buyer and seller as defendants, and steps out of the dispute. Filing fees and attorney costs for the interpleader are typically recoverable from the deposit itself, though the purchase agreement should address this explicitly.

Agents should know that prolonged disputes trigger additional obligations. If funds sit in escrow for more than six months without resolution, NHREC expects the broker to either obtain a written extension agreement from both parties or initiate interpleader. Sitting passively with disputed funds is not a defensible position during an audit.
