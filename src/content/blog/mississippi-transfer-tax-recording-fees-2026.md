## Understanding Mississippi Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Mississippi stands alone among southern states in one critical respect: it has no state-imposed real estate transfer tax. That single fact trips up agents relocating from Tennessee, Alabama, or Louisiana, where documentary stamp taxes or transfer fees are baked into every closing estimate. If you are preparing a settlement statement for a Mississippi transaction in 2026, the **Mississippi transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026** framework is defined almost entirely by county-level recording fees set in state statute, not by a percentage-based conveyance tax.

Misquoting these fees—or worse, omitting legally required filings—can delay recordings, expose your brokerage to liability, and leave your client's ownership interest unprotected. The sections below break down the exact statutory authorities, the forms you need, what goes wrong when agents get sloppy, and what brokers should be checking on every file.

## The Statutory Framework: Miss. Code Ann. Sections 25-7-9 and 89-5-24

**Mississippi's recording fee structure is governed primarily by Miss. Code Ann. 25-7-9, which sets uniform fees that chancery clerks may charge across all 82 counties.** This statute was last amended during the 2024 legislative session, and the fee schedule remains in effect for 2026 filings. The relevant charges for real estate closings include fees for recording deeds, deeds of trust, releases, and assignments.

Miss. Code Ann. 89-5-24 addresses the requirements for a deed to be eligible for recording at all. A deed must be acknowledged before a notary, must contain a legal description sufficient to identify the property, and must include the full name and address of the preparer. If any element is missing, the chancery clerk has authority to reject the instrument, which means your client's deed sits in limbo while competitors file subsequent instruments.

There is no companion statute imposing a transfer tax, excise tax, or documentary stamp obligation. The [Mississippi Secretary of State's office](https://www.sos.ms.gov/) confirms this through its land records guidance: the state does not collect a percentage-based fee tied to the sales price at the time of deed recording.

## Recording Fee Breakdown for 2026

The following table summarizes the fees agents should quote on preliminary closing estimates and HUD-1 or ALTA settlement statements in Mississippi:

| Document Type | First Page Fee | Each Additional Page | Indexing (per extra name) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warranty Deed | $25 | $3 | $5 |
| Deed of Trust | $25 | $3 | $5 |
| Release / Satisfaction | $25 | $3 | $5 |
| Assignment of Deed of Trust | $25 | $3 | $5 |
| UCC Fixture Filing | $25 | $3 | $5 |

These fees are per instrument. A transaction with a warranty deed (3 pages), a deed of trust (15 pages), and a UCC filing (2 pages) would generate recording costs of $31 + $67 + $28 = $126 before any additional indexing charges. Agents who estimate "around $50 for recording" on a buyer's net sheet are systematically under-quoting, especially on transactions with lengthy legal descriptions or multiple parcels.

County clerks may also charge a separate fee for certified copies, typically $5 for the first page and $1 per additional page. If your buyer's lender requires a certified recorded copy returned, add that line item.

## Who Pays What: Custom vs. Contract

Mississippi law does not dictate which party bears recording costs. Unlike states where statute assigns the documentary transfer tax to the seller, Mississippi leaves allocation entirely to the parties' agreement. In practice, the custom across most of the state follows a predictable split:

| Fee Item | Customary Payor | Statutory Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Deed recording | Buyer | None—contractual |
| Deed of trust recording | Buyer | None—contractual |
| Satisfaction/release recording | Seller | None—contractual |
| Land roll change filing | Buyer (or title company) | Miss. Code 27-3-55 |
| Certified copy of deed | Buyer | None—contractual |

**Agents must ensure the purchase agreement explicitly states who pays recording fees.** The Mississippi Association of Realtors (MAR) Form 110—the standard Residential Purchase Agreement—contains a section on closing costs allocation. If you leave that section blank or rely on "customary," you are inviting a dispute at the closing table when one party claims the other should absorb a $150 charge.

## Required Forms and Filings Beyond the Deed

Recording the deed is not the only filing obligation triggered by a Mississippi property transfer. Agents and their transaction coordinators must track several ancillary documents:

The Land Roll Change Form mandated by Miss. Code Ann. 27-3-55 must be submitted to the county tax assessor's office to update the ad valorem tax rolls. Failure to file does not void the transfer, but it can result in tax bills being sent to the prior owner, which creates collection complications and occasionally triggers tax sale proceedings on accounts that appear delinquent.

The Homestead Exemption Application (Form 72-020) should be presented to any buyer who will occupy the property as a primary residence. While this is technically the buyer's responsibility, the listing agent or buyer's agent who reminds them at closing provides genuine post-sale value.

If the property is subject to a FIRPTA withholding (foreign seller), the transferee must file IRS Form 8288 and 8288-A within 20 days of closing. Mississippi does not impose a separate state withholding, but the federal obligation still applies. Agents working with foreign sellers should coordinate with the closing attorney early.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Mississippi Recordings

Five errors surface repeatedly in Mississippi transaction files, and each one carries real consequences.

**First, agents quote transfer tax amounts that do not exist.** Coming from other states, some agents include a "transfer tax" line on the seller's net sheet. This confuses clients, raises questions about agent competence, and—if left uncorrected on the settlement statement—could constitute a RESPA violation for collecting fees not actually owed.

Second, agents fail to verify page counts before estimating recording fees. A deed of trust from a national lender can run 25 to 40 pages. At $3 per additional page beyond the first, that is $72 to $117 just for the excess pages—yet many agents estimate the entire instrument at $25.

Third, agents neglect the land roll change filing. The chancery clerk records the deed, but the tax assessor does not automatically update the rolls. If nobody submits the change form, the buyer may not receive a property tax bill until the account goes delinquent, at which point penalties and interest have already accrued.

Fourth, deeds are submitted with an insufficient legal description. Mississippi chancery clerks have become more rigorous about rejecting instruments that reference only a street address without a metes-and-bounds or recorded plat reference. Agents who draft their own deeds—rather than using a closing attorney—are the most frequent offenders.

Fifth, the preparer's name and address are omitted from the deed face. Miss. Code Ann. 89-5-24 requires this information. Without it, the clerk rejects the instrument, and the recording date slips. A one-day delay may seem minor, but in competitive transactions with simultaneous closes, it can cascade into a chain of broken closings.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) Rule 3.2 for the acts of their licensees. Recording fee errors may seem trivial compared to agency disclosure violations, but they expose the brokerage to claims of negligence, breach of fiduciary duty, and RESPA violations when inflated or fabricated fees appear on settlement statements.

**Every broker should audit at least 10 percent of closed files each quarter for three specific recording-related items.** First, confirm that the settlement statement matches the actual county recording fees charged. Second, verify that the land roll change form was submitted within 30 days of closing—request a stamped copy or filing receipt. Third, ensure no "transfer tax" line item appeared on files where no such tax exists.

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy stating which forms the agent or TC is responsible for submitting post-closing. The MREC does not mandate a specific checklist, but having one in writing shifts liability away from the broker if an agent fails to comply. If an audit reveals repeated errors, the broker must document remedial training. MREC's disciplinary guidelines (published in their 2025 Enforcement Manual) consider documented supervision efforts as mitigating factors when evaluating brokerage-level sanctions.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

An agent who consistently misstates fees or fails to ensure proper recording faces layered consequences. At the transaction level, a rejected recording means the buyer's lender cannot confirm their lien position, which can trigger a loan funding freeze. The deal does not technically cancel, but it does not close on time, and the buyer may lose a rate lock worth thousands of dollars.

At the regulatory level, the MREC can initiate disciplinary proceedings under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-21 for conduct demonstrating incompetence or negligence. Sanctions range from a letter of reprimand to license suspension. The Commission's public disciplinary database shows multiple cases where agents were disciplined for closing-related documentation failures.

At the civil level, a buyer who suffers financial harm because the agent failed to record the deed promptly—or failed to advise on the land roll change—can sue for negligence. Mississippi courts have recognized that real estate agents owe a duty of care that extends to ensuring instruments are properly prepared for recording, even when a closing attorney is involved. The agent does not get to hide behind the attorney's role if the agent controlled the timeline or prepared the documents.

## Staying Current as Fee Schedules Evolve

Mississippi's legislature reviews fee schedules periodically, and the 2024 amendment to Section 25-7-9 raised the per-page additional fee from $2 to $3. Agents who built their closing cost templates before that change are now under-quoting every single file. Staying current on **Mississippi transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026** is not a one-time exercise—it requires annual verification against the statute and confirmation with your local chancery clerk's office.

Brokers should distribute an updated fee reference sheet at the start of each calendar year and require agents to acknowledge receipt. The Mississippi Association of Realtors publishes periodic practice updates, but those updates are not a substitute for reading the statute yourself. Agents who rely on outdated MLS closing cost fields or recycled net sheet templates from 2022 are quoting numbers that no longer reflect reality, and that puts both the agent's reputation and the brokerage's license at risk.
