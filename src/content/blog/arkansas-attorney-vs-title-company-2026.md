## Arkansas Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of "Arkansas attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" lands on every agent's desk eventually — usually when a deal hits a snag three days before the closing date. Arkansas occupies a unique middle ground among states: it does not require an attorney at closing by statute, but it aggressively restricts what non-attorneys can do during the closing process. Misunderstanding that distinction has cost agents both deals and license complaints.

This post breaks down the legal framework, the practical roles each party fills, which Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) forms matter, and where agents routinely trip over compliance lines they did not know existed.

## The Legal Framework: What Arkansas Law Actually Says

**Arkansas does not have a blanket closing-attorney mandate.** Unlike states such as Georgia, South Carolina, or New York, there is no statute requiring a licensed attorney to physically attend or supervise every residential closing. The relevant statutes are Ark. Code Ann. Section 16-22-211 (unauthorized practice of law), Ark. Code Ann. Section 23-103-101 et seq. (Title Insurance Act), and Arkansas Supreme Court Rule XIV.

Here is the critical nuance: while no attorney must "be at" the table, any act that constitutes the practice of law must be performed by a licensed Arkansas attorney. The Arkansas Supreme Court has repeatedly defined "practice of law" to include drafting deeds, preparing mortgages, interpreting title exceptions, and advising parties on their legal rights. A title company employee who crosses those lines exposes the transaction — and potentially the referring agent — to liability.

The [Arkansas Supreme Court's Committee on the Unauthorized Practice of Law](https://www.arcourts.gov/courts/supreme-court/committees/unauthorized-practice-of-law) actively investigates complaints and has issued advisory opinions clarifying that title agents may handle ministerial functions (collecting signatures, disbursing funds, recording documents) but cannot exercise legal judgment on behalf of a party.

## Attorney Role vs. Title Company Role: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

Understanding who does what prevents agents from making promises they cannot keep and from inadvertently participating in unauthorized practice.

| Function | Licensed Attorney | Title Company |
|----------|------------------|---------------|
| Draft deeds and legal instruments | Yes — exclusive authority | No — unless attorney-supervised |
| Render title opinions | Yes — exclusive authority | No — may issue title commitments only |
| Conduct title search | Yes | Yes — through licensed title agents |
| Handle escrow and disbursement | Yes | Yes — under Ark. Code Ann. 23-103 |
| Advise parties on contract terms | Yes | No — ministerial role only |
| Record documents at county level | Yes | Yes |
| Issue title insurance policies | No (unless also licensed agent) | Yes — must hold ADIS license |

This table matters because agents are often asked by clients, "Do I need to hire an attorney?" The answer depends on the complexity of the transaction. A straightforward single-family sale with clean title can close through a title company without attorney involvement. A transaction involving boundary disputes, mineral rights reservations, or estate conveyances almost always requires attorney preparation of instruments.

## How AREC Forms Shape Your Compliance Obligations

Arkansas real estate agents work primarily with forms approved or promulgated by the Arkansas Real Estate Commission. The most relevant forms for closing-related compliance include AREC Standard Form 1 (Purchase Contract), Standard Form 2 (Counter Offer), and Standard Form 4 (Addendum). Agents must not modify these forms beyond filling in blanks — doing so may constitute unauthorized practice of law under Arkansas Supreme Court precedent.

**AREC Rule 10.7 specifically prohibits licensees from drafting legal documents or advising clients on legal matters.** If a buyer asks an agent to explain a title exception or draft a quitclaim deed, the agent must decline and refer the client to an attorney. The distinction between filling in AREC-approved blanks and drafting custom language is the line between permissible conduct and a [violation that could break a deal](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Agents should also be aware of AREC Rule 10.15 regarding escrow trust accounts. If an agent holds earnest money and the transaction falls through, the disbursement of those funds involves legal determinations that may require attorney involvement or interpleader action. Mishandling earnest money disputes without proper guidance is one of the fastest paths to an AREC complaint.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make at Arkansas Closings

Five errors surface repeatedly in AREC disciplinary actions and title insurance claims.

**First, agents draft or modify deed language.** Even small changes — adding a survivorship clause, adjusting the legal description, or inserting an easement reservation — constitute unauthorized practice of law. An agent who does this faces potential AREC disciplinary action under Rule 10.7, and the deed itself could be challenged in court. The remedy is simple: refer all instrument preparation to a licensed attorney.

Second, agents tell clients they do not need an attorney because "the title company handles everything." This statement creates liability when the title company employee cannot legally advise the client on a title exception that later causes loss. The agent may be named in a subsequent negligence or misrepresentation claim. A better approach: inform clients that a title company can facilitate closing, but an attorney should review any transaction involving unusual circumstances.

Third, agents fail to verify that the closing agent holds proper licensing. Under the Arkansas Title Insurance Act (Ark. Code Ann. 23-103-401), any person or entity issuing title insurance or acting as an escrow agent must be licensed with the Arkansas Department of Insurance and registered with a title underwriter. An unlicensed closer can void the title insurance policy entirely — leaving the buyer uninsured and the agent potentially liable for the referral.

Fourth, agents provide opinions on title commitment exceptions. When a commitment shows a Schedule B-II exception for an unrecorded easement, the agent who says "that's nothing to worry about" has arguably practiced law. That exception must be explained by an attorney or, at minimum, by the title company's underwriting counsel.

Fifth, agents miss [critical compliance deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) because they assume the title company or attorney is tracking them. Closing date extensions, financing contingency expirations, and title objection periods are the agent's responsibility to monitor, regardless of who conducts the closing.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under Ark. Code Ann. Section 17-42-311. A broker who permits or ignores unauthorized practice within their office risks license suspension, civil liability, and potential referral to the Committee on the Unauthorized Practice of Law.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|--------------|----------|
| Form usage | Only AREC-approved forms in files | Custom addenda drafted by agents |
| Closing agent licensing | ADIS license number on file | No verification in transaction folder |
| Attorney referrals | Documentation when agent declined legal questions | Agent emails containing legal advice |
| Earnest money disputes | Interpleader or written attorney guidance | Agent disbursing without consent/court order |
| Deed preparation | Attorney name on all instruments | Agent-prepared deeds in closed files |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits focusing specifically on closing documents.** Pull ten random closed files per quarter and verify that no agent-prepared legal instruments exist, that all closing agents were properly licensed, and that any earnest money disputes followed proper procedures. Document these audits — AREC investigators view documented compliance programs favorably during examinations.

Brokers also need a written office policy addressing when agents must recommend attorney involvement. At minimum, transactions involving estate sales, boundary disputes, mineral rights, commercial property, or [complex multi-party structures](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) should trigger a mandatory attorney referral in the office policy manual.

## The 2026 Enforcement Climate in Arkansas

AREC has signaled increased enforcement attention toward unauthorized practice issues in recent years. The Commission's 2025-2026 strategic priorities include enhanced continuing education requirements around legal boundaries and closer coordination with the Arkansas Supreme Court's unauthorized practice committee. Agents should expect that violations which may have been overlooked a decade ago will draw formal complaints today.

The Arkansas Department of Insurance has similarly tightened oversight of title companies, particularly regarding escrow account management and proper segregation of duties. A title company that loses its license mid-transaction can derail a closing with no warning — another reason agents should verify licensing proactively rather than assume good standing.

Fines for AREC violations range from reprimand to license revocation, with monetary penalties up to $1,000 per violation under Ark. Code Ann. Section 17-42-310. Unauthorized practice findings by the Supreme Court can result in contempt proceedings, injunctions, and separate civil liability to harmed parties.

## Practical Guidance for Every Arkansas Closing

The safest approach is simple: use a licensed title company for ministerial closing functions and a licensed attorney for anything requiring legal judgment. Most closings in Arkansas proceed through title companies without incident because the transactions are straightforward. Problems arise when agents or title company employees exceed their authority, usually under time pressure.
