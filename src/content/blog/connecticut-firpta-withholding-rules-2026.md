## Understanding Connecticut FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every Connecticut agent who lists property owned by a non-U.S. person faces a dual-layer withholding obligation that can derail closings and trigger personal liability. The Connecticut firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 require compliance with both the federal Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (IRC Sections 1445-1446) and Connecticut's own nonresident withholding statute, Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 12-725. Failing to address either layer before the closing date exposes agents, their brokers, and the buyer to penalties that start at thousands and scale to six figures.

The federal rule is straightforward in theory: when a foreign person disposes of a U.S. real property interest, the buyer must withhold 15% of the amount realized and remit it to the IRS using Form 8288 and Form 8288-A within 20 days of closing. Connecticut adds its own requirement through the Department of Revenue Services (DRS), demanding withholding on gains realized by nonresidents—foreign or domestic—at a rate that currently stands at 6.99% of the gain or, in many cases, a flat percentage applied to the gross sales price as a safe harbor.

**Agents do not personally remit these funds, but they carry an affirmative duty to disclose foreign ownership status to the closing attorney and title company.** The Connecticut Real Estate Commission has made clear in its licensee guidance that material nondisclosure—including failure to flag a seller's foreign status—can result in license discipline under Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-320.

## Federal vs. State Withholding: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

Agents frequently confuse what the IRS demands with what the Connecticut DRS demands. They are separate obligations with separate forms, separate deadlines, and separate penalties.

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | Connecticut (DRS) |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory Authority | IRC Section 1445 | Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 12-725 |
| Withholding Rate | 15% of amount realized | 6.99% of CT-source gain (or safe harbor on gross) |
| Key Forms | IRS Form 8288, 8288-A, 8288-B | CT-1040NR/PY, Form CT-8288 equivalent via myconneCT |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after closing | 30 days after closing |
| Primary Liable Party | Buyer (transferee) | Buyer (transferee) or withholding agent |
| Exemption Threshold | $300,000 residence use exemption | Withholding certificate from DRS |

The most common scenario Connecticut agents encounter is a foreign national who purchased a Fairfield County home a decade ago, has since returned overseas, and now wants to sell remotely. Both federal and state withholding must be addressed, and the closing attorney cannot release proceeds without confirming compliance.

**If you represent a foreign seller, you should verify their taxpayer identification status at listing—not at the closing table.** An Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) does not change a seller's foreign status for FIRPTA purposes, though it is required for filing Form 8288-A.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The liability chain in a FIRPTA failure runs primarily to the buyer, but it does not stop there. Under IRC Section 1445(e), a transferee who fails to withhold becomes personally liable for the tax, plus interest at the federal rate and a penalty of up to 20% of the amount that should have been withheld. In a $1.2 million Connecticut sale, that is $180,000 in base withholding, with potential penalties pushing exposure above $215,000.

For agents, the consequences are professional rather than directly financial—but no less severe. The Connecticut Real Estate Commission can suspend or revoke a license under Section 20-320(a)(5) for conduct that demonstrates incompetency or untrustworthiness. A pattern of ignoring FIRPTA disclosures or failing to communicate known foreign seller status to the closing team meets that threshold.

Brokerage-level exposure compounds the problem. Under Connecticut's vicarious liability framework, the supervising broker bears responsibility for acts within the scope of an agent's licensed activity. If a buyer later discovers they owe the IRS $180,000 because their agent and the seller's agent both ignored the foreign seller's status, the resulting malpractice claim names the brokerage.

> "The transferee's obligation to withhold is not conditioned on receiving notice from the transferor. The buyer is liable regardless of actual knowledge." — IRS Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(b)

That regulation means your buyer client cannot later claim ignorance. Agents who let closings proceed without addressing FIRPTA are setting their clients up for a surprise IRS assessment, which almost always triggers a professional liability claim.

## Common Mistakes Connecticut Agents Make with FIRPTA

**Mistake one: assuming "resident alien" means "not foreign."** A green card holder who has abandoned residency or failed the substantial presence test may still be classified as a nonresident alien for tax purposes. The determination is fact-specific, and agents are not qualified to make it—but they are responsible for raising the question and directing the seller to a tax advisor or the closing attorney.

Mistake two: ignoring Connecticut's separate withholding requirement entirely. Many agents who understand FIRPTA at the federal level have no idea that Connecticut imposes an additional nonresident withholding. The DRS enforces this independently, and the state can file a lien against the property or the proceeds if withholding is not remitted.

Mistake three: waiting until the week before closing to raise the issue. IRS Form 8288-B, the application for a reduced withholding certificate, takes 90 days or more to process. If a seller qualifies for an exemption or reduction, that application must be filed months before the anticipated closing date. Agents who discover the issue late force their sellers into escrowing the full 15% federally, plus the state amount, with no guarantee of a timely refund.

Mistake four: confusing the $300,000 buyer-residence exemption with a blanket rule. The exemption under IRC 1445(b)(5) only applies when the amount realized is $300,000 or less and the buyer signs an affidavit stating they intend to occupy the property as a principal residence for at least 50% of the time during the first two years. In Connecticut's market, where median home prices in many towns exceed this threshold, the exemption rarely applies.

Mistake five: failing to document the seller's citizenship or residency status at all. If you never ask, you cannot claim you acted reasonably. The listing agreement should prompt a discussion, and a signed [Affidavit of Non-Foreign Status](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026) (or its absence) should be in the file before you go under contract.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers operating in Connecticut carry supervisory obligations under Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-312a that extend to ensuring agents handle FIRPTA-related disclosures properly. This is not optional guidance—it is a licensure requirement.

First, every brokerage should maintain a written policy requiring agents to document seller citizenship or residency status at the listing intake stage. The policy should specify what form or questionnaire is used and where it is stored in the transaction file. Without this, the broker has no evidence of systemic compliance during a DRS or Commission audit.

Second, brokers must verify that the closing instructions transmitted to the title company or closing attorney reference the seller's foreign status when applicable. A simple line in the agent's closing checklist—confirmed foreign/non-foreign status with closing attorney on [date]—creates an audit trail. Brokers reviewing files after closing should look for this confirmation the same way they check for [lead paint disclosures](/blog/west-virginia-lead-paint-disclosure-2026) or agency agreements.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Look For | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Seller status questionnaire | Signed form in listing file | No proof agent raised FIRPTA |
| Closing attorney notification | Email or memo referencing status | Liability if withholding missed |
| Form 8288-B timeline tracking | Calendar entry if exemption sought | Delayed closing or escrowed funds |
| State withholding confirmation | DRS filing receipt in post-close file | State lien or penalty |
| ITIN verification | Copy of ITIN or SS-4 application | IRS rejection of Form 8288-A |

Third, continuing education on FIRPTA should be mandatory at the brokerage level. Connecticut's 12-hour CE cycle (administered by the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection) does not always include FIRPTA-specific content, which means brokers must supplement with internal training. A 30-minute quarterly review during a team meeting is sufficient to keep agents alert to the issue.

## The Role of Closing Attorneys in Connecticut Transactions

Connecticut is an attorney-close state. Unlike jurisdictions where title companies handle disbursement independently, Connecticut closings require an attorney to oversee the transaction. This provides a built-in safeguard for FIRPTA compliance—but only if the agent has communicated the seller's status in advance.

The closing attorney prepares or reviews Form 8288 and coordinates the withholding escrow. They also handle the Connecticut DRS filing through the [myconneCT portal](https://portal.ct.gov/drs), which is the state's online tax filing system. However, attorneys rely on information provided by the parties and their agents. If nobody flags the seller's foreign status, the attorney may not discover it until the closing table—or worse, after funds have already been disbursed.

**Agents who assume "the attorney will catch it" are gambling with their license and their client's financial exposure.** The safest practice is to treat every seller's citizenship status as a mandatory intake item, no different from mortgage payoff verification or HOA transfer documentation.

## Practical Steps for Agents Handling Foreign Seller Listings

Start at the listing appointment. Ask directly whether the seller is a U.S. citizen, a U.S. resident alien meeting the substantial presence test, or a nonresident alien. If the seller hesitates or provides an ambiguous answer, refer them to a tax professional before the property hits the MLS.

Once a contract is ratified, notify the closing attorney in writing that the seller is a foreign person subject to FIRPTA withholding. Include any ITIN documentation the seller has provided and note whether the seller intends to apply for a withholding certificate via Form 8288-B. If they do, the attorney needs 90-plus days of lead time, which means the closing date in the contract must account for this.

Track deadlines aggressively. The 20-day federal filing window and 30-day state window after closing are hard deadlines with no grace period.
