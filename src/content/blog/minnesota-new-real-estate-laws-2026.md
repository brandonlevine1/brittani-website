The 2026 legislative session and subsequent rulemaking by the Minnesota Department of Commerce have produced a batch of statutory changes that directly affect how licensed agents conduct business. Understanding Minnesota new real estate laws 2026 is not optional — several provisions carry immediate enforcement risk, and the compliance deadlines have already passed or are approaching fast. This article breaks down what changed, what forms are affected, and where agents and brokers are most likely to trip up.

## Buyer Representation Agreements Now Mandatory Before Touring

Minnesota Statutes Section 82.67 was amended during the 2025 legislative session (SF 1142) and took effect January 1, 2026. The statute now requires a written buyer representation agreement to be executed before any property touring, open house attendance on behalf of a client, or substantive negotiation activity. This aligns Minnesota with post-NAR settlement standards that other states adopted throughout 2025.

The Minnesota Association of Realtors updated its standard Buyer Representation Exclusive Right to Represent form (MAR Form 101B, revised 01/2026) to comply. The form now mandates a specific, non-open-ended compensation figure — either a flat fee, a percentage, or an hourly rate — and prohibits language that references MLS cooperative compensation as a guaranteed source. Agents who use older versions of this form are operating on an outdated document that may not satisfy the statutory requirements.

**Failing to secure this agreement before touring exposes the agent to license discipline and makes any subsequent commission claim legally unenforceable.** The Minnesota Department of Commerce has already issued guidance bulletins (Commerce Bulletin 2026-02) stating it will treat violations as unlicensed activity when compensation is later sought without a signed agreement.

## Revised Seller Disclosure Requirements Under Minnesota Statutes Section 513.55

The Minnesota Seller's Property Disclosure Statement received its most significant overhaul since 2012. The legislature expanded the scope of required disclosures to include PFAS contamination awareness, known flooding history beyond FEMA zones, and prior insurance claims related to water intrusion. These additions appear in the revised statutory form referenced in Minnesota Statutes Section 513.55, Subdivision 1.

Agents must use the 2026 version of the disclosure form. The prior version omitted questions about environmental contaminants that are now mandatory. The Department of Commerce issued Administrative Rules Chapter 2805.1500 clarifying that the listing agent is responsible for ensuring the seller receives, completes, and delivers this form to prospective buyers before an offer is accepted.

One critical distinction: Minnesota remains a "disclose what you know" state, not an inspection mandate state. Sellers are not required to hire inspectors, but they cannot leave a field blank without written explanation. Agents who coach sellers to leave fields blank or write "unknown" on items the seller clearly has knowledge about are exposing themselves to fraud liability under Minnesota Statutes Section 82.81.

## Minnesota New Real Estate Laws 2026: Escrow and Earnest Money Changes

**The earnest money deposit timeline has been shortened from three business days to two business days following contract acceptance.** This change, codified in the amendment to Minnesota Statutes Section 82.24, Subdivision 3, applies to all residential purchase agreements executed on or after March 1, 2026.

| Requirement | Previous Rule (Pre-2026) | New Rule (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit deadline | 3 business days after acceptance | 2 business days after acceptance |
| Broker trust account documentation | Quarterly reconciliation | Monthly reconciliation |
| Buyer notification of deposit | Not required | Written confirmation within 24 hours |
| Disbursement after cancellation | 5 business days | 3 business days |
| Electronic deposit permitted | At broker discretion | Explicitly authorized by statute |

The shift to monthly trust account reconciliation creates new administrative burdens on brokerages. Managing brokers who previously reconciled quarterly now face potential audit findings if they miss even one monthly cycle. The Department of Commerce has signaled that trust account violations will be a 2026 enforcement priority, referencing a pattern of consumer complaints from 2024-2025 involving delayed earnest money returns.

Agents working with buyers need to communicate this compressed timeline clearly. A buyer who assumes they have until Monday to wire funds after a Friday acceptance is already in violation territory. This is exactly the type of [deadline agents commonly miss](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and the consequences now arrive faster.

## Agency Disclosure Timing Gets Stricter Enforcement

Minnesota has required the "Agency Relationships in Real Estate Transactions" disclosure since 1994, but enforcement was historically complaint-driven. Under revised Administrative Rules Chapter 2805.2100, the Department of Commerce now requires affirmative documentation that the disclosure was provided at "first substantive contact" — and the definition of substantive contact has been tightened.

The new rule clarifies that substantive contact includes any discussion of specific properties, financing options, or the consumer's motivations for buying or selling. A casual conversation at a community event does not trigger the requirement, but the moment an agent says "I saw a listing that might work for you," the clock starts. The disclosure must be delivered before that conversation continues.

**Agents must retain a signed acknowledgment of receipt or documented electronic delivery confirmation.** The MAR Form 201 (Agency Disclosure, revised 2026) now includes a timestamp field specifically designed for electronic delivery tracking. Agents using older versions without this field will have difficulty proving compliance in any dispute.

This matters because [where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often traces back to unclear agency relationships. A buyer who later claims they thought the listing agent was representing them can seek rescission if the disclosure wasn't properly delivered and documented.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

The first three months of 2026 enforcement have already revealed patterns. Based on Department of Commerce complaint data and brokerage audit findings, here are the errors showing up most frequently.

**Mistake one: using outdated MAR forms.** The January 2026 form revisions affected the Buyer Representation Agreement (101B), the Seller's Disclosure Statement, the Agency Disclosure (201), and the Purchase Agreement addendum for compensation disclosure. Agents pulling forms from saved templates instead of the current MAR form library are submitting non-compliant documents. This is not a minor formatting issue — missing statutory language renders a form potentially unenforceable.

Mistake two: treating the two-business-day earnest money deadline as a suggestion. Multiple brokerage audits have flagged agents who collected earnest money checks but held them in their car, desk, or briefcase for days before delivering to the brokerage trust account. Under Section 82.24, the agent must deliver funds to the broker within the statutory window. Personal possession of trust funds beyond that period constitutes a trust account violation carrying fines up to $10,000 per occurrence.

Mistake three: delivering the buyer representation agreement at the offer stage instead of before touring. Some agents have interpreted "before substantive negotiation" as meaning "before we write an offer." The statute is explicit — touring qualifies as substantive activity requiring a signed agreement. An agent who shows twelve homes without an agreement and then asks the buyer to sign before writing an offer has been in violation since home number one.

Mistake four: failing to disclose the compensation structure to all parties in writing. The revised Purchase Agreement addendum (MAR Form 301A, 2026) requires that buyer agent compensation — regardless of source — be disclosed to the seller as part of any offer package. Agents submitting offers without this addendum are creating incomplete contracts that title companies are increasingly rejecting.

Mistake five: omitting the PFAS disclosure question on the seller's disclosure form. This is specific to agents in the east metro, particularly communities near the 3M Superfund sites, but the requirement applies statewide. Even in areas with no known PFAS contamination, the question must appear on the form and the seller must respond.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce Immediately

Managing brokers carry direct supervisory liability under [Minnesota Statutes Section 82.22](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/82.22). The 2026 amendments expanded what constitutes a broker's "failure to supervise" to include specific documentation audits.

| Audit Item | Required Frequency | Consequence of Non-Compliance |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer representation agreements on file | Per transaction, before closing | Broker fine up to $5,000 per missing agreement |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | License suspension after 2 missed periods |
| Agency disclosure acknowledgments | Per transaction | Written warning, escalating to fine |
| Updated form version verification | Quarterly spot-check (minimum) | Administrative action if pattern found |
| Compensation disclosure addendum (301A) | Per transaction with buyer agent involvement | Transaction rescission risk |

Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint at three stages: listing agreement execution, offer acceptance, and pre-closing. Waiting until closing to audit for missing documents creates a scenario where the violation has already occurred, the consumer's rights have potentially been impaired, and the remediation options are limited. This is where [structured checklists outperform memory-based compliance](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Monthly trust account reconciliation deserves specific attention. The Department of Commerce's 2025 enforcement report cited trust account irregularities as the number one reason for license revocation proceedings. The move from quarterly to monthly reconciliation was directly motivated by cases where funds sat unaccounted for more than sixty days.

## Liability Exposure and Enforcement Mechanisms

The Minnesota Department of Commerce has three primary enforcement tools for 2026 violations: administrative fines, license discipline (suspension or revocation), and referral to the Attorney General's office for consumer fraud claims.

Administrative fines for first-time form violations typically range from $1,000 to $5,000 per occurrence. Trust account violations carry higher penalties — up to $10,000 per occurrence with cumulative liability if multiple transactions are affected. Repeat offenders face license suspension without the option for a consent order.

Consumer rescission rights also expanded. Under the amended Section 513.55, a buyer who did not receive the updated seller's disclosure form before offer acceptance now has a seven-calendar-day right of rescission after receiving the form — up from the previous statutory period of three days. This creates a scenario where deals can unravel deep into the transaction timeline if disclosure delivery was late.

The practical effect: a listing agent who forgets to deliver the updated disclosure at listing, then scrambles to get it to the buyer after mutual acceptance, has just given the buyer a seven-day free look at the deal. During that window, the buyer can walk for any reason or no reason. Title companies and lenders are increasingly flagging these gaps during their own compliance reviews.
