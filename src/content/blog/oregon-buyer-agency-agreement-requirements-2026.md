## Oregon Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: The Rules Have Teeth Now

The phrase "Oregon buyer agency agreement requirements 2026" is not just a compliance checkbox anymore — it represents one of the most enforcement-heavy obligations facing agents licensed in this state. Following the NAR settlement implementation and subsequent rule updates from the Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA), the written buyer representation requirement has moved from best practice to hard mandate. Agents who skip this step face real consequences, including license discipline and lost commissions.

Oregon's regulatory framework sits at the intersection of state administrative rules, MLS policies adopted by RMLS and other regional boards, and the broader national settlement terms that went into effect in August 2024. The result is a layered compliance obligation that touches every buyer interaction, from first contact through closing. This article breaks down the specific statutes, form requirements, liability exposures, and broker oversight duties that define the 2026 landscape in Oregon.

## The Statutory and Regulatory Foundation

Oregon's agency relationship requirements are governed primarily by ORS 696.800 through 696.890 and the administrative rules under OAR 863-015-0215. The Oregon Real Estate Agency — the state's licensing and enforcement body — issued updated guidance in 2025 clarifying that written buyer agency agreements must be executed before an agent provides any substantive brokerage services to a prospective buyer. This mirrors the [NAR settlement practice changes](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) but carries Oregon-specific enforcement mechanisms.

OAR 863-015-0215 requires that the agreement disclose the nature and scope of the agent's duties, the duration of representation, and the compensation structure. The rule explicitly mandates that compensation terms be stated in an objective, specific manner — no open-ended "whatever the seller offers" language. Oregon goes further than some states by requiring the agreement to identify whether the licensee represents the buyer exclusively or if disclosed limited agency applies.

**The OREA has made clear in its 2025-2026 audit bulletins that verbal agreements do not satisfy the written requirement, even if memorialized later.** The agreement must exist in writing before services begin, full stop.

## Form Numbers and Documentation Standards

Oregon REALTORS (the state association) publishes Form 801, titled the Buyer Representation Agreement, as the standard template that satisfies OAR requirements. This form underwent significant revision in late 2024 and again in early 2026 to align with settlement mandates. Most brokerages operating through RMLS use Form 801 or a brokerage-approved equivalent that has been reviewed by legal counsel.

| Form | Title | Use Case | Key Section |
|------|-------|----------|-------------|
| Form 801 | Buyer Representation Agreement | Full exclusive buyer representation | Compensation, duration, duties |
| Form 802 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Limited-term, non-exclusive showing arrangement | Scope limitations, termination |
| Form 840 | Consent to Disclosed Limited Agency | Dual/limited agency situations | Conflict disclosure, reduced duties |
| Form 800 | Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet | First substantive contact with any party | Agency types, consumer rights |

Form 800 (the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet) must be provided at first substantive contact, before or simultaneously with the execution of Form 801 or 802. The OREA has issued administrative warnings to agents who present Form 801 without first providing Form 800. These are not interchangeable steps — they are sequential obligations under ORS 696.810.

Brokerages using proprietary forms must ensure those documents include every element specified in OAR 863-015-0215, including the specific dollar amount or percentage of buyer-agent compensation and the conditions under which termination may occur. Custom forms that omit the termination date or fail to specify compensation in objective terms have already triggered OREA enforcement actions in early 2026.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Non-compliance with Oregon's buyer agency agreement requirements carries consequences across three distinct channels: regulatory, contractual, and civil.** Each one can independently derail an agent's career or a transaction in progress.

On the regulatory side, OREA can impose civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation under ORS 696.990. Repeated violations or willful disregard can result in license suspension or revocation. The Agency has publicly stated that buyer agency agreement violations are a 2026 audit priority, meaning investigators are actively reviewing transaction files for missing or deficient agreements.

Contractually, a buyer who never signed a representation agreement can walk away from any obligation to pay the agent's commission. If a dispute arises and the agent has no signed agreement, Oregon courts have consistently ruled that the agent has no enforceable claim to compensation. This is true even if the agent spent weeks showing property and submitting offers.

Civil liability exposure is the third rail. An agent who acts without a signed agreement but behaves as though an agency relationship exists may face claims for breach of fiduciary duty, negligent misrepresentation, or unauthorized practice. The buyer can argue they never consented to representation, shifting all risk onto the agent. Errors and omissions insurance carriers have increasingly added buyer agency documentation as an underwriting condition, meaning gaps in compliance could void coverage.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Oregon

Five recurring errors show up in OREA disciplinary files and brokerage audit reports. Any one of them can cost an agent their commission, their license, or both.

**First, agents present the buyer agency agreement at the offer stage rather than before showing property.** This violates the timing requirement under OAR 863-015-0215 and creates an argument that all prior services were provided without consent. The agreement must predate substantive services, not memorialize them after the fact.

Second, agents use outdated versions of Form 801 that lack the post-settlement compensation specificity language. The 2026 version requires compensation to be expressed as a fixed dollar amount, a percentage of purchase price, or a combination — never as "to be determined" or "as offered by the listing broker." Using a pre-2025 form is functionally equivalent to having no compliant agreement at all.

Third, agents fail to execute Form 840 when a disclosed limited agency situation arises mid-transaction. If an agent's own listing attracts a buyer they already represent, the transition to disclosed limited agency requires a new signed consent. Proceeding without Form 840 violates ORS 696.890 and creates dual-agency exposure without informed consent.

Fourth, agents neglect to provide Form 800 (the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet) before executing the buyer representation agreement. Oregon law treats these as separate disclosure obligations with independent timing requirements. Skipping Form 800 does not invalidate Form 801, but it creates an independent violation that compounds regulatory exposure.

Fifth, agents allow agreements to expire without renewal and continue providing services. An expired agreement means no agency relationship exists under Oregon law. Services provided after expiration are unauthorized and uncompensated as a matter of law.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Oregon carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.026 and OAR 863-015-0080 for their affiliated agents' compliance failures. This is not a theoretical risk — OREA has imposed [fines on brokers](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) whose agents lacked proper documentation, even when the broker had no direct involvement in the transaction.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Red Flag |
|------------|-----------|----------|
| Form 801/802 execution date vs. first showing date | Every transaction | Showing date precedes agreement date |
| Compensation specificity language | Every transaction | Vague or open-ended compensation terms |
| Form 800 delivery confirmation | Every transaction | Missing signature/acknowledgment |
| Agreement expiration date | Monthly review | Active services beyond termination date |
| Form 840 presence in dual-agency files | As applicable | Dual agency without signed consent |

Brokers should implement a transaction file review at three milestones: agreement execution, offer submission, and closing. The first checkpoint catches timing violations before they become embedded in a transaction file. The offer submission review confirms that compensation terms in the purchase agreement align with the buyer agency agreement — a mismatch creates confusion and potential disputes at closing.

**Every Oregon brokerage should maintain a centralized log of buyer agency agreement execution dates, expiration dates, and associated agent names.** This log becomes the broker's primary defense in an OREA audit. Without it, the broker is left arguing from memory, which regulators do not find persuasive.

Training is not optional either. OAR 863-015-0080 requires principal brokers to provide supervision "appropriate to the experience of the licensee." New agents must receive specific instruction on form selection, timing requirements, and the compensation disclosure rules before handling buyer clients independently. Documenting this training creates an affirmative defense if an individual agent violates protocol despite adequate oversight.

## Duration, Termination, and Renewal Protocols

Oregon does not impose a statutory maximum on buyer agency agreement duration, but the OREA expects terms to be "reasonable and negotiated." Industry practice in the state has settled around 90 to 180 days for most residential transactions. Agreements extending beyond six months attract scrutiny, particularly if the buyer is unsophisticated or claims they did not understand the commitment.

Termination clauses must be clear and actionable. Form 801 includes a mutual termination provision and a protection period (often 60-90 days post-termination) during which the agent retains a commission claim on properties previously shown. Agents who draft overly broad protection periods — covering any property the buyer purchases regardless of the agent's involvement — risk having those clauses deemed unenforceable under Oregon's unfair trade practices statutes.

Renewal requires a new signature. Oregon does not recognize automatic renewal of buyer agency agreements absent explicit written consent at the time of renewal. An agent who continues working past the termination date without a new signed agreement has no enforceable right to compensation and no authorized agency relationship. This is one of the most common sources of [commission disputes and transaction breakdowns](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the state.
