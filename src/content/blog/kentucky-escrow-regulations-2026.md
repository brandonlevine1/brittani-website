## The Statutory Framework Behind Kentucky Escrow Regulations 2026

Kentucky escrow regulations 2026 are not new law—they are the accumulated weight of KRS Chapter 324, KREC administrative regulations (201 KAR Chapter 11), and evolving commission guidance that agents must follow without exception. The Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC) enforces these rules under its authority granted by KRS 324.281 through KRS 324.310. Misunderstanding the framework costs agents deals, money, and sometimes their license.

**The foundational statute is KRS 324.111, which mandates that every principal broker maintain a separate escrow or trust account.** This account must be held at a federally insured financial institution located in Kentucky. The statute explicitly prohibits commingling—mixing escrow funds with business operating funds or personal accounts is a per-se violation regardless of intent.

KREC's administrative regulations under 201 KAR 11:121 add specificity to the statutory requirements. They dictate deposit timelines, recordkeeping standards, and the documentation brokers must maintain for each escrow transaction. These requirements apply regardless of transaction size, property type, or whether the deal ultimately closes.

## Deposit Timelines and the Forms That Govern Them

The Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) publishes standardized forms that most agents use statewide. Form 200—the Purchase Agreement—contains the earnest money provisions that trigger escrow obligations. When a buyer executes Form 200 and the seller accepts, the deposit clock starts ticking.

Under standard form language and KREC guidance, earnest money must reach the broker's escrow account by the close of the next business day following mutual acceptance. If the contract specifies a different timeline, that written agreement controls—but agents should be cautious about extending the window without clear client instructions. The deposit acknowledgment on Form 200 serves as the paper trail proving compliance.

**Form 215—the Disbursement of Earnest Money Agreement—governs how funds are released when a transaction terminates.** Both parties must sign Form 215 before the broker can release escrowed funds to either side. Without dual signatures, the broker must hold the funds and cannot unilaterally decide who gets the money, even when the contract language seems obvious.

| Form Number | Purpose | When It Triggers Escrow Action |
|---|---|---|
| Form 200 | Purchase Agreement | Mutual acceptance starts deposit clock |
| Form 215 | Disbursement of Earnest Money | Required before any release of held funds |
| Form 220 | Counter Offer | May modify escrow terms or deposit amounts |
| Form 230 | Amendment to Contract | Can change escrow holder or deposit deadline |
| Form 270 | Notice of Termination | Begins the dispute or release process |

Agents sometimes treat Form 215 as a formality. It is not. When one party refuses to sign, the broker faces a holding obligation that can last until a court orders release or both parties eventually agree. KREC has repeatedly disciplined brokers who released funds based on one party's verbal claim without proper documentation.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Escrow in Kentucky

Mistakes with escrow are not hypothetical war stories—they are the most frequent basis for KREC disciplinary actions against licensees. Here are the errors that keep appearing in commission hearing transcripts.

**First, late deposits remain the single most common violation.** Agents receive a check from the buyer, stick it in a desk drawer over a weekend, and deliver it to the office Monday afternoon. If mutual acceptance occurred Friday, that Monday deposit is already late. The "next business day" requirement is strict, and KREC auditors count calendar days from the contract timestamp.

Second, agents deposit earnest money into the wrong account. This happens when a team member deposits the check into the team lead's personal account, or when an agent working under one broker deposits into an account held by a different brokerage. Both scenarios constitute commingling under KRS 324.111, and both expose the broker to sanctions regardless of the agent's intent.

Third, agents verbally promise buyers a refund of earnest money without executing Form 215. The buyer's agent tells the buyer "you'll get your money back" after a failed inspection, but never secures the seller's signature on the disbursement form. The broker then sits on disputed funds for months while the buyer grows furious and files a KREC complaint.

Fourth, agents fail to disclose escrow disputes to their clients in writing. When a seller refuses to sign Form 215 and the money is frozen, agents sometimes avoid the uncomfortable conversation. KRS 324.160(4) requires licensees to disclose material facts affecting the transaction—a frozen earnest money deposit qualifies.

Fifth, agents accept escrow instructions that conflict with the purchase agreement. A title company sends over its own escrow instructions with different disbursement conditions than Form 200 specifies. The agent signs without comparing the documents, creating a contradiction that surfaces at closing and delays the deal by weeks.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, License Actions, and Civil Liability

KREC's enforcement authority is not theoretical. The commission [publishes disciplinary actions](https://krec.ky.gov/) quarterly, and escrow violations rank consistently among the top three categories. Understanding the penalty structure helps agents appreciate why compliance is non-negotiable.

Under KRS 324.160, KREC can impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation. License suspension ranges from 30 days to one year depending on severity. License revocation is reserved for repeat offenders or cases involving deliberate misappropriation of client funds. A single commingling violation—even an accidental one—can trigger a mandatory audit of the broker's entire escrow account history.

| Violation Type | Potential KREC Penalty | Civil Exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Late deposit (first offense) | Written reprimand, $500-$1,000 fine | Buyer may cancel contract |
| Commingling funds | $2,500-$5,000 fine, possible suspension | Conversion claim, treble damages |
| Unauthorized disbursement | Suspension or revocation | Breach of fiduciary duty lawsuit |
| Failure to maintain records | $1,000-$2,500 fine, mandatory audit | Discovery sanctions in litigation |
| Escrow shortage (any amount) | Immediate suspension pending hearing | Criminal referral if intentional |

Beyond KREC sanctions, agents and brokers face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue for conversion—and Kentucky courts have awarded treble damages in cases involving willful misconduct. The broker's errors and omissions insurance may not cover intentional acts, leaving the broker personally exposed.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical consequence. Under the standard Form 200 terms, a buyer who delivers earnest money that is not properly deposited within the stated timeline may argue the contract is voidable. Sellers can walk away from a deal if escrow conditions are not met, and they occasionally do—especially in multiple-offer situations where the next buyer is waiting.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

The principal broker bears ultimate responsibility for escrow compliance under Kentucky law. KRS 324.111 does not allow delegation of this duty to office managers, team leads, or transaction coordinators without the broker's ongoing oversight. The buck stops at the broker's license.

**Brokers should conduct monthly reconciliations of their escrow accounts at minimum.** 201 KAR 11:121 requires that records show the source, amount, and disposition of every dollar entering and leaving the trust account. Each deposit must tie back to a specific transaction file with the corresponding Form 200 and any amendments. Each disbursement must correspond to a signed Form 215 or a closing statement from a title company.

Brokers also need to audit agent behavior around deposit timing. Requiring agents to submit a photo or scan of the earnest money check with a timestamp—before physically delivering it to the office—creates a verifiable chain of custody. Some Kentucky brokerages now require agents to deposit checks via mobile banking apps linked directly to the escrow account, eliminating the desk-drawer problem entirely.

KREC can initiate a [random audit of any broker's escrow account](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) at any time without cause. These audits examine three years of records. Brokers who discover discrepancies should self-report to KREC immediately—the commission has historically treated voluntary disclosure more favorably than violations uncovered during a surprise audit.

Training documentation matters. Brokers who can demonstrate they provided annual escrow compliance training to all agents—with signed acknowledgment forms—have a stronger defense if an individual agent violates policy. KREC considers broker supervision standards when determining whether to sanction the broker for an agent's mistake.

## Escrow Disputes: The Interpleader Option and Practical Realities

When a buyer and seller cannot agree on earnest money disbursement and neither will sign Form 215, the broker faces a practical dilemma. Kentucky law does not permit the broker to hold funds indefinitely without taking some action, nor does it allow unilateral release.

The interpleader action—filing a petition in circuit court asking a judge to determine who receives the funds—is the broker's legal escape valve. KRS 324.111 implicitly authorizes this by requiring brokers to protect all parties' interests. The broker deposits the disputed funds with the court, names both parties as respondents, and asks to be dismissed from the dispute. Filing fees and attorney costs for the interpleader can sometimes be deducted from the escrowed amount if the purchase agreement permits it.

Most disputes never reach interpleader. In practice, the listing agent and buyer's agent negotiate a resolution—often a split—and both parties sign Form 215. Agents who understand [where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) can often anticipate escrow disputes before they escalate and draft contract language that addresses common termination scenarios.

Brokers should establish a written office policy specifying the timeline for escalating unresolved escrow disputes. A common standard: if both signatures on Form 215 are not obtained within 30 days of termination notice, the broker initiates interpleader proceedings. Having this policy in writing protects the broker from accusations of favoritism or inaction.

## Recordkeeping Standards Under 201 KAR 11:121

The administrative regulations governing escrow recordkeeping are precise. 201 KAR 11:121 requires brokers to maintain, at minimum, a ledger showing each transaction's escrow activity, copies of all deposit slips and bank statements, copies of all disbursement authorizations, and monthly reconciliation worksheets. These records must be retained for five years from the date of the last activity in each transaction file.

**Electronic records are acceptable under current KREC guidance, but they must be readily producible in printed form within 72 hours of an audit request.** Cloud-based transaction management systems satisfy this requirement as long as the broker maintains administrative access independent of any individual agent's account. If an agent leaves the brokerage, their transaction records must remain accessible to the broker.

Bank statements must be reconciled against the transaction ledger monthly. The reconciliation must account for every dollar—any unexplained variance, even a $5 bank fee, must be documented with an explanation. KREC auditors flag unexplained variances as potential indicators of deeper problems, and a single unexplained discrepancy can trigger a full forensic review.

Agents who handle their own [transaction coordination without systems](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are particularly vulnerable to recordkeeping failures. The volume of documents per file—checks, forms, amendments, notices—overwhelms manual tracking after a handful of concurrent transactions.

Mobile deposit capabilities linked directly to the escrow account eliminate the most common violation—late deposits. Automated reminders for Form 215 signatures prevent disputes from festering into interpleader territory. Digital transaction files with timestamp verification give brokers the audit trail KREC demands without requiring manual spreadsheet updates.
