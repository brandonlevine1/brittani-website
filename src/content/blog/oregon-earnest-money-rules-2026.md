## Understanding Oregon Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Oregon treats earnest money as trust funds the moment a buyer hands over a check or wire. That single fact drives almost every compliance obligation agents face under Oregon earnest money & escrow laws 2026. Mishandle the deposit—even by a day—and you expose yourself, your broker, and your client to regulatory sanctions, contract cancellation, and civil liability.

The governing framework sits across several statutes and administrative rules: ORS Chapter 696 (real estate licensing and trust accounts), Oregon Administrative Rules Chapter 863 (Real Estate Agency rules), and the Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) library that provides the contract language agents actually use in practice. The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA), a division of the Real Estate Board, enforces compliance and investigates complaints.

This post breaks down the deposit timeline, escrow holder obligations, dispute resolution protocols, common agent errors, and what principal brokers must audit to keep their firms out of trouble.

## Deposit Deadlines and Trust Account Rules

**Oregon's deposit window is one of the shortest in the country.** Under OAR 863-015-0135, a principal broker—or a licensed agent acting under that broker's authority—must place earnest money into the clients' trust account no later than the end of the next business day following receipt. "Receipt" means the moment the check, cashier's check, or wire confirmation lands in the agent's hands or the brokerage's office, whichever comes first.

The trust account itself must be a demand deposit account at a federally insured financial institution located in Oregon. Commingling personal or brokerage operating funds with clients' trust money is a per-se violation under ORS 696.241(1). Even a small infraction—say, depositing an office expense reimbursement into the trust account temporarily—can trigger an investigation.

Wire transfers add another wrinkle. If a buyer wires earnest money to an escrow or title company directly, the listing broker never physically holds the funds, and the next-business-day clock does not apply to the brokerage. But the agent still must confirm receipt with the escrow holder and document it in the transaction file. ## OREF Forms That Govern Earnest Money

Oregon agents overwhelmingly rely on OREF forms for residential transactions. The two forms most relevant to earnest money handling in 2026 are OREF 001 (Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement) and OREF 049 (Earnest Money Promissory Note), though several addenda also come into play.

| Form | Function | Key Earnest Money Clause |
|------|----------|--------------------------|
| OREF 001 | Sale Agreement | Section 2 defines deposit amount, holder, and delivery deadline |
| OREF 049 | Earnest Money Promissory Note | Used when buyer provides a note instead of immediate funds |
| OREF 038 | Mutual Release of Earnest Money | Requires both parties' signatures before disbursement |
| OREF 044 | Dispute Resolution Addendum | Outlines mediation/arbitration steps for deposit disputes |

Section 2 of OREF 001 names the escrow holder and specifies whether the listing broker's trust account or a title/escrow company will hold the deposit. If the parties fail to fill in this field—or if the named holder changes after mutual acceptance—an addendum or written agreement must document the change. Agents who verbally agree to redirect funds without written authorization create an unenforceable arrangement and a compliance gap their broker will have to explain to OREA.

OREF 049 deserves special attention. It allows a buyer to provide a promissory note for all or part of the earnest money when immediate funds aren't available. The note must state a due date, and if the buyer fails to pay by that date, the seller may treat it as a default under the contract. Agents sometimes confuse a promissory note with an IOU scrawled on a napkin—OREA has made clear that only the approved form, fully executed, qualifies.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

Violations fall into three categories: administrative penalties from OREA, civil liability to the parties, and potential criminal exposure in egregious cases.

**OREA can revoke, suspend, or refuse to renew any license for trust account violations under ORS 696.301.** The Board can also impose civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation under ORS 696.990. In practice, first-time deposit-timing errors often result in a formal reprimand and mandatory continuing education, but repeat offenders face suspension.

Civil liability is where the real financial pain lives. If a seller loses a backup buyer because the agent's failure to deposit earnest money caused the deal to collapse, the seller can sue the agent and the broker for damages. Oregon courts have held that agents owe a fiduciary duty to their own client and a statutory duty of honest dealing to all parties under ORS 696.810. Breach of either duty in the context of earnest money mishandling is actionable.

Criminal liability is rare but possible. Conversion of trust funds—spending a buyer's deposit or diverting it—constitutes theft under ORS 164.045 (Theft in the Second Degree for amounts under $1,000) or ORS 164.055 (Theft in the First Degree for amounts over $1,000). A felony conviction would obviously end a real estate career, but it also exposes the principal broker to vicarious liability if supervision was lacking.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Earnest Money in Oregon

Five errors show up repeatedly in OREA disciplinary files and brokerage audits. All are preventable.

**First, holding a check "per buyer's request" past the deposit deadline.** Buyers sometimes ask agents not to cash the check until a certain date. Oregon law does not care what the buyer wants—the broker has a statutory duty to deposit by end of next business day. The correct move is to inform the buyer that the funds will be deposited on schedule and that they should not write a check they cannot cover.

Second, failing to document a change in escrow holder. If the parties agree mid-transaction to move funds from the broker's trust account to a title company (or vice versa), the change must be reflected in a written addendum signed by all parties. Verbal agreements are unenforceable and leave the agent exposed if a dispute arises about who holds the money.

Third, releasing earnest money to the seller before closing without mutual written consent. Even when the buyer has clearly defaulted—blown a contingency deadline, failed to perform—the listing agent cannot unilaterally hand the deposit to the seller. OREF 038 exists precisely for this scenario. Both signatures, or a court order, must precede disbursement. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) helps agents anticipate these pressure points before they become compliance disasters.

Fourth, treating a personal check and a wire transfer identically in the file. Agents must note the form of payment, the date received, and the date deposited. Wire confirmations should be printed or saved digitally. Check images should be captured before deposit. The file must tell a complete story to any auditor.

Fifth, ignoring the interest question. Oregon permits—but does not require—interest-bearing trust accounts. If the account earns interest, the disposition of that interest must be disclosed and agreed upon. Most brokerages use non-interest-bearing accounts to sideline the issue entirely, but agents who work for boutique firms with interest-bearing accounts need to ensure the OREF 001 addresses interest allocation.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry personal liability for trust account compliance under OAR 863-015-0130. That is not a delegation you can hand to an office manager and forget. OREA expects the principal broker—or a licensed associate broker with formal written authority—to perform monthly reconciliations of the clients' trust account.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | OAR 863-015-0140 |
| Deposit timing verification | Per transaction | OAR 863-015-0135 |
| File documentation of earnest money receipt | Per transaction | OAR 863-015-0135 |
| Mutual release or court order before disbursement | Per dispute | ORS 696.581 |
| Agent training on trust account rules | Annually (recommended) | Best practice; not statutory |

**A broker who discovers a deposit timing error must self-report if the error resulted in harm to a party.** Self-reporting does not guarantee immunity, but OREA has historically treated proactive disclosure as a mitigating factor in penalty determinations. Covering up a violation—or worse, instructing an agent to backdate a deposit slip—escalates the matter into potential criminal territory.

Brokerages with multiple agents should maintain a centralized tracking system for deposit deadlines. A spreadsheet works, but it depends on manual entry and human memory—neither of which is reliable at volume. Automated tracking through a transaction coordination platform eliminates the possibility that a deposit deadline slips through during a busy week. If you track [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), deposit timing errors rank high, especially during summer volume spikes.

## Dispute Resolution: The Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller both claim the earnest money and neither will sign OREF 038, the broker faces a dilemma. Oregon law prohibits the broker from choosing sides. Under ORS 696.581, the broker must continue to hold the funds in trust until the parties reach written agreement or a court decides.

The broker's escape hatch is interpleader—a legal action where the broker deposits the disputed funds with the court and asks the judge to determine entitlement. Filing an interpleader petition under ORCP 31 releases the broker from liability for the deposit itself, though the broker may still face claims related to the underlying transaction. Many brokerages include an interpleader clause in their listing and buyer agreements, putting both parties on notice that the firm will file if no resolution comes within a specified number of days.

The cost of interpleader—attorney fees, filing fees—can be recovered from the deposit if the agreement allows it. OREF 001 Section 15 addresses dispute resolution costs, and the [OREA statutes page](https://www.oregon.gov/rea/Pages/Statutes-and-Rules.aspx) provides the full text of ORS 696.581 for reference.
