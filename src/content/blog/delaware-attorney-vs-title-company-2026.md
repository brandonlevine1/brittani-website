## Why the Question of Delaware Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026 Matters Now

Delaware remains one of a small number of states where a licensed attorney must be involved in the closing of a residential real estate transaction. If you sell property here, the question of "Delaware attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" is not theoretical — it determines who sits at the settlement table, who reviews documents, and who bears professional liability when something goes wrong. Getting it wrong can kill a deal or expose your license to disciplinary action.

The distinction between attorney-closing states and title-company-closing states confuses agents who work across state lines or who relocated from markets where a title officer handles everything. Delaware's framework is rooted in unauthorized practice of law (UPL) doctrine and reinforced by state statute, Delaware Supreme Court rulings, and Delaware Real Estate Commission regulations. This post breaks down exactly what the law requires, where agents commonly trip up, and what brokers should be auditing right now.

## The Legal Foundation: Why Delaware Requires an Attorney

**Delaware's requirement for attorney involvement at closing is grounded in 24 Del. C. Chapter 56 — the statute governing the practice of law — and longstanding Delaware Supreme Court opinions on UPL.** The court has consistently held that preparing deeds, mortgages, settlement statements, and affidavits for a real estate closing constitutes the practice of law. Only a member of the Delaware Bar in good standing may perform those functions.

The Delaware State Bar Association has issued multiple advisory opinions reinforcing this position. The most cited is the principle that selecting, preparing, or reviewing legal instruments that affect title to real property is a legal act, not a ministerial one. A title company employee who is not a Delaware-licensed attorney cannot conduct the settlement conference, explain documents to buyers and sellers, or resolve legal issues that arise at the table.

This does not mean title companies have no role. Title companies in Delaware routinely perform title searches, issue title insurance commitments and policies, and coordinate logistics. The distinction is functional: the title company handles the insurance product, and the attorney handles the legal act of closing.

## Attorney vs. Title Company: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

The following table clarifies which tasks belong to the attorney and which belong to the title company in a typical Delaware residential transaction.

| Task | Attorney | Title Company |
|---|---|---|
| Conduct title search | Sometimes (may order from title company) | Yes |
| Issue title insurance commitment/policy | No (unless also a title agent) | Yes |
| Prepare the deed | Yes | No |
| Prepare the HUD-1/Closing Disclosure review | Yes (reviews and approves) | May assist with figures |
| Preside at the settlement table | Yes | No |
| Disburse funds from escrow | Yes (from attorney escrow account) | Sometimes (for title insurance premiums) |
| Resolve title defects or legal disputes | Yes | No (flags issues, refers to attorney) |

Many Delaware attorneys also serve as title insurance agents, which consolidates both roles into one office. When that happens, agents sometimes assume the title company is running the show. It is still the attorney, wearing two hats, who bears the legal responsibility for the closing.

## What Delaware Agents Must Know About Their Own Compliance

**The Delaware Real Estate Commission, operating under 24 Del. C. Chapter 29, sets the licensing and conduct rules for agents and brokers.** Agents do not conduct the closing — but they have compliance obligations that interact directly with the attorney requirement.

Delaware agents use standard forms developed by the Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR). The primary purchase contract is the DAR Agreement of Sale, and agents should be familiar with the settlement-related provisions in that form, including the designation of the settlement agent and the buyer's right to select an attorney. The DAR Addendum forms — covering contingencies, inspections, and financing — also contain deadlines that must align with the attorney's settlement schedule.

An agent who steers a client away from attorney representation, or who attempts to conduct settlement functions (such as preparing a deed or explaining legal documents), risks a UPL complaint with the Delaware Supreme Court's Office of Disciplinary Counsel and a separate disciplinary action from the [Delaware Real Estate Commission](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/). Both carry serious consequences, including license suspension or revocation.

If you have worked in states where [agents handle more of the closing process themselves](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026), Delaware's bright line between agent duties and attorney duties can feel restrictive. But the line exists for a reason, and crossing it — even inadvertently — creates real exposure.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make at or Before Closing

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in Delaware transactions, and each one traces back to confusion about the attorney's role.

**First, agents draft or modify legal documents.** Filling in blanks on a DAR-approved form is within an agent's scope. Drafting a custom addendum, modifying deed language, or writing a legal rider is not. If the transaction requires anything beyond the standard DAR forms, the client's attorney must prepare it. Agents who freelance legal language expose themselves to UPL claims and E&O coverage gaps.

Second, agents schedule closings without confirming attorney availability. In Delaware, the closing literally cannot happen without the attorney present. Agents who lock in a closing date based solely on lender timelines and then discover the settlement attorney is unavailable end up scrambling for continuances. Always confirm the attorney's calendar before committing to a settlement date in the Agreement of Sale.

Third, agents fail to disclose that the settlement attorney does not represent both parties. Delaware attorneys at closing typically represent only one side — most often, the buyer or the lender. The other party should be advised to retain independent counsel. Agents who tell their clients "the closing attorney will take care of everything for both of you" are creating a misrepresentation risk. The attorney's engagement letter will specify who the client is, and the agent should make sure their own client understands the distinction.

Fourth, agents provide legal interpretations of title commitments. When a title search reveals an easement, lien, or boundary issue, agents sometimes try to explain the legal significance to their clients. That explanation is legal advice. The correct response is to refer the client to the settlement attorney or their own independent attorney for an explanation of any title exception.

Fifth, agents overlook escrow account requirements. Delaware attorneys hold settlement funds in [Interest on Lawyer Trust Accounts (IOLTA)](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) governed by Delaware Supreme Court Rule 1.15. Agents who direct clients to wire funds to a non-attorney account — or who hold earnest money in their own brokerage escrow past the point where it should transfer to the attorney — risk both regulatory violations and wire fraud exposure.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under 24 Del. C. Section 2912, which means an agent's UPL violation or compliance failure can land on the broker's desk as a commission complaint, a lawsuit, or both. Active auditing is not optional.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement of Sale forms | Confirm only DAR-approved forms and attorney-drafted addenda are used | Every transaction |
| Settlement attorney confirmation | Verify a licensed DE attorney is identified before closing is scheduled | Before closing date is set |
| Earnest money handling | Confirm funds transfer to attorney escrow per contract terms | Within contractual deadline |
| Agent communications | Spot-check emails and texts for legal advice or document drafting | Quarterly random audit |
| Closing file documentation | Confirm attorney settlement statement and deed copies are in file | Post-closing review |

Brokers should also maintain a written policy — distributed to every agent — that explicitly states agents may not prepare deeds, explain title exceptions, or preside over any portion of a settlement conference. A one-page policy memo, signed annually by each agent, creates a paper trail that matters if a complaint is filed.

If your brokerage handles volume across multiple states, the differences in closing requirements between Delaware and neighboring states like Maryland or Pennsylvania can cause process drift. Standardized checklists that flag Delaware-specific attorney requirements help prevent agents from defaulting to habits they picked up in [other markets where title companies run closings](/blog/wisconsin-title-and-closing-procedures-2026).

## How Agents Should Talk to Clients About Attorney Costs

Buyers and sellers in Delaware sometimes push back on attorney fees, especially first-time buyers who are already stretched on closing costs. Agents need to explain the requirement clearly without practicing law about the requirement itself.

The standard framing is straightforward: Delaware law requires a licensed attorney to conduct the closing, and the attorney's fee covers document preparation, settlement oversight, escrow management, and recording. Fees typically range from $500 to $1,500 for a standard residential closing, depending on complexity and location within the state.

**Agents should never recommend a specific attorney as the "cheapest option" without disclosing any financial relationship.** The Delaware Real Estate Commission's regulations on referral fees and kickbacks align with RESPA (12 U.S.C. Section 2607), and undisclosed compensation arrangements between agents and settlement attorneys are a fast path to dual regulatory exposure. If you refer clients to an attorney, disclose the relationship. If you receive anything of value in return, disclose that too — and confirm it complies with both state and federal law.

## The 2026 Landscape: What Has Changed and What Has Not

No pending Delaware legislation as of mid-2026 changes the fundamental attorney-closing requirement. The Delaware General Assembly has not introduced bills to convert Delaware to a title-company-closing state, and the Delaware Supreme Court has not signaled any shift in its UPL doctrine as applied to real estate settlements.

What has changed is enforcement posture. The Delaware Real Estate Commission increased its focus on transaction documentation audits in 2025, and that trend continues into 2026. Agents and brokers should expect that complaint investigations will include a review of whether proper attorney involvement occurred at closing. The commission has also flagged concerns about remote closings and RON (Remote Online Notarization) sessions conducted without adequate attorney supervision — an area where technology has outpaced some agents' understanding of the rules.
