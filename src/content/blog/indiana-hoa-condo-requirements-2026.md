## Understanding Indiana HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Indiana's framework for HOA and condominium disclosures is governed by a patchwork of statutes that many agents only half-understand. The **Indiana hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** requirements sit primarily in Indiana Code Title 32, Article 25.5 (the Indiana Homeowners Association Act) and Article 25 (the Horizontal Property Law, governing condominiums). Getting these wrong does not just irritate buyers—it can unwind a deal or expose your brokerage to statutory liability.

Unlike states that consolidate everything into a single disclosure package, Indiana splits obligations across the seller, the association, and the agent. The seller must obtain and deliver certain documents. The association must produce them within a statutory timeframe. And the agent must ensure delivery occurs before the buyer's rescission clock starts ticking.

This post breaks down what you actually need to deliver, the timelines that matter, the forms you should have in your file, and the consequences if you miss something.

## The Statutory Framework: IC 32-25.5 and IC 32-25

**Indiana Code 32-25.5-3-2 requires a seller of property subject to an HOA to furnish a resale disclosure packet to the buyer.** This packet must include the association's governing documents, current fee schedules, any pending special assessments, the association's current budget, and details about any pending litigation involving the association.

For condominiums specifically, IC 32-25-8-1 through IC 32-25-8-5 impose additional requirements. A condominium resale certificate must include the declaration of covenants, the bylaws, rules and regulations, current financial statements, and any outstanding liens against the unit attributable to common expenses. These are not optional addenda—they are statutory mandates.

The Indiana Real Estate Commission (IREC), which operates under the Professional Licensing Agency, enforces agent compliance through its disciplinary process. Violations of disclosure duties can trigger complaints under [Indiana Administrative Code 876 IAC 8-2-4](https://www.in.gov/pla/professions/real-estate-commission/), which addresses failure to exercise reasonable skill and care in a transaction.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

Indiana does not use a single state-issued "HOA disclosure form" the way some states do. Instead, agents must coordinate multiple documents from different sources. Here is how the primary forms break down:

| Document | Source | Governing Statute | Typical Fee |
|----------|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Resale Disclosure Packet (HOA) | Homeowners Association | IC 32-25.5-3-2 | $150–$400 |
| Condominium Resale Certificate | Condo Association/Manager | IC 32-25-8-1 | $200–$500 |
| Sales Disclosure Form (State Form 46234) | Seller | IC 32-21-5-7 | None |
| IAR Addendum to Purchase Agreement (HOA/Condo) | Agent/Broker | Indiana Association of Realtors | Included in membership |

The Indiana Association of Realtors (IAR) publishes a specific HOA/Condo Addendum that agents attach to purchase agreements. This addendum spells out the buyer's rescission rights, allocates costs for obtaining the resale packet, and establishes the delivery timeline within the contract itself. If you are not attaching this addendum on every HOA or condo deal, you are creating a gap that invites disputes.

**State Form 46234, the Residential Real Estate Sales Disclosure, is a separate obligation.** It addresses physical property conditions—roof age, water damage, environmental hazards—and does not replace the HOA-specific disclosures. Agents who assume one satisfies both are making a dangerous error.

## Buyer Rescission Rights and Delivery Deadlines

Under IC 32-25.5-3-3, once the buyer receives the resale disclosure packet, they have three business days to rescind the purchase agreement without penalty. This is not a negotiable period—it is statutory. If the packet arrives late, the rescission window simply shifts forward, potentially delaying your closing.

Here is where the timing gets tricky. The statute does not impose a hard deadline by which the seller must deliver the packet after contract execution. It only guarantees the buyer's right to rescind once they receive it. Smart agents solve this by building a delivery deadline into the purchase agreement addendum—typically five to ten days after acceptance.

If the seller never delivers the packet, the buyer's rescission right never expires. That means a buyer could theoretically walk away the day before closing with no contractual recourse for the seller. This scenario plays out more often than brokers want to admit, particularly on FSBO conversions where no one ordered the documents early enough.

Agents on the listing side should order the resale packet during the listing period, not after an offer comes in. [Understanding why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with delayed disclosure documents that give buyers cold feet at the wrong moment.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Indiana HOA Disclosures

**The most frequent compliance failures are not dramatic—they are procedural oversights that compound under pressure.** Based on transaction audit patterns across Indiana brokerages, these five errors appear repeatedly.

First, agents confuse the state Sales Disclosure Form with the HOA resale packet. They deliver State Form 46234 and assume the HOA obligation is satisfied. It is not. These are distinct statutory requirements with different content, different sources, and different legal consequences.

Second, agents fail to attach the IAR HOA/Condo Addendum to the purchase agreement. Without this addendum, the contract lacks a specific delivery deadline and cost allocation for the resale packet. The buyer still has statutory rescission rights, but the contract gives neither party a mechanism to enforce timely delivery.

Third, listing agents wait until after contract execution to request the packet from the association. Many Indiana management companies take seven to fourteen business days to produce these documents. If your contract has a ten-day delivery window, you are already behind.

Fourth, agents do not confirm receipt in writing. The three-business-day rescission period begins upon delivery—but if there is no documented proof of when the buyer received the packet, disputes about the rescission window become he-said-she-said arguments. Always obtain a signed acknowledgment of receipt with a date stamp.

Fifth, buyer's agents do not review the packet contents for red flags before their clients sign off. Pending special assessments, underfunded reserves, or active litigation against the association are all deal-relevant facts that buyers miss unless their agent highlights them. [Knowing why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) often comes down to not building review steps into their workflow.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry vicarious liability for their agents' disclosure failures under Indiana law. IREC can discipline both the agent and the supervising broker if a transaction file lacks required documentation. Here is what managing brokers should verify on every HOA or condo transaction.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Where It Should Be |
|------------|---------------|-------------------|
| HOA/Condo Addendum attached | Signed by all parties, dated | Purchase agreement package |
| Resale packet ordered | Order confirmation with date | Transaction file notes |
| Resale packet delivered | Buyer's signed receipt with date | Disclosure section of file |
| Rescission period expired | Three business days confirmed elapsed | Timeline documentation |
| State Form 46234 separate | Both forms present independently | Disclosure section of file |

**Brokerages that rely solely on agents to self-report compliance are asking for trouble.** A quarterly file audit is the minimum standard. Better practice is a transaction management system that flags missing documents in real time, before closing—not during a post-closing complaint investigation.

IREC has authority under IC 25-34.1-8-3 to fine brokers, suspend licenses, or impose probation for supervisory failures. The commission's enforcement actions are public record, and a pattern of disclosure violations can trigger an office-wide audit. No broker wants that letter.

One practical approach: build a standard operating procedure that requires the listing coordinator or [transaction coordinator](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) to order the resale packet within 48 hours of listing activation for any property with an HOA. This eliminates the most common bottleneck entirely.

## Liability Exposure When Disclosure Fails

The consequences of non-compliance extend beyond IREC discipline. Under IC 32-25.5-3-4, a buyer who does not receive the required resale disclosure packet may recover actual damages from the seller. Courts have also found agents liable under negligence theories when they failed to ensure timely delivery despite knowing the property was subject to an association.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. A buyer who receives a late packet and discovers a $15,000 pending special assessment has both a contractual and statutory right to walk away. The seller loses the deal, the agent loses the commission, and the brokerage may face a complaint from either side.

Beyond individual transactions, E&O carriers increasingly scrutinize HOA disclosure compliance during claims evaluation. If your file lacks evidence of proper delivery and a dispute arises, the carrier may deny coverage on the basis that you failed to follow standard procedures. That transforms a commission dispute into a personal liability event.
