## Idaho FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Stakes Are Higher Than You Think

Every year, agents across the Treasure Valley and Sun Valley corridor close transactions involving foreign nationals without fully understanding the withholding obligations those deals carry. The Idaho firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 create a dual-layer compliance burden—federal withholding under IRC Section 1445 and Idaho's own nonresident withholding under Idaho Code Section 63-3026A. Getting either one wrong can shift six-figure tax liability directly onto your buyer client.

**FIRPTA—the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act—requires the buyer to withhold 15% of the gross sale price when acquiring U.S. real property from a foreign person.** That is not 15% of the gain. It is 15% of the entire purchase price, remitted to the IRS within 20 days of closing using Form 8288 and Form 8288-A. Idaho layers an additional 2% state withholding on the estimated gain, collected by the closing company and remitted to the Idaho State Tax Commission.

The consequences of noncompliance do not land on the seller. They land on the buyer—and by extension, on the agents and title officers who failed to flag the issue before closing.

## How Federal FIRPTA Withholding Works in an Idaho Transaction

The federal withholding obligation is triggered whenever a "transferee" (buyer) acquires a U.S. real property interest from a "foreign person." Foreign person includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, and certain foreign trusts or estates. The standard withholding rate is 15% of the amount realized—meaning the full contract price, not just the seller's equity.

Sellers can apply for a reduced withholding by filing IRS Form 8288-B before closing, requesting a withholding certificate. The IRS typically takes 90 days to process these applications, which means agents need to identify foreign seller status at listing, not at the closing table. If the certificate is granted, it may reduce withholding to the seller's actual expected tax liability, often far less than 15%.

There are limited exemptions. If the property will be used by the buyer as a residence and the sale price does not exceed $300,000, no withholding is required. Between $300,001 and $1,000,000, the rate drops to 10% if the buyer intends to use the property as a personal residence. Above $1,000,000, the full 15% applies regardless of buyer intent.

## Idaho's Additional Nonresident Withholding Layer

Idaho Code Section 63-3026A imposes a separate obligation on real property sales by nonresidents—not just foreign nationals, but any seller whose permanent residence is outside Idaho. The closing agent (title company or attorney) must withhold 2% of the net gain or, if gain cannot be determined, 2% of the gross sales price. This amount is remitted to the Idaho State Tax Commission on [Form 51](https://tax.idaho.gov/forms/EFO00088.pdf).

| Obligation | Rate | Base Amount | Form | Remitted To | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal FIRPTA | 15% | Gross sale price | IRS 8288 / 8288-A | IRS | 20 days post-closing |
| Idaho nonresident | 2% | Net gain (or gross if gain unknown) | Idaho Form 51 | Idaho State Tax Commission | 20 days post-closing |
| Reduced FIRPTA (residence, under $1M) | 10% | Gross sale price | IRS 8288 / 8288-A | IRS | 20 days post-closing |

**The Idaho withholding applies to all nonresidents, not just foreign nationals.** An American citizen living in Oregon who sells a vacation home in McCall triggers the 2% state withholding. A Canadian citizen selling a ski condo in Ketchum triggers both the federal 15% and the state 2%. Agents often confuse these two obligations or assume one covers the other.

Sellers can request exemption from Idaho's withholding by filing Form 51 with the closing agent if they can demonstrate the transaction will result in no Idaho tax liability—for example, if the property was sold at a loss. The closing agent is authorized to accept or reject this claim, which creates liability exposure for title companies that accept questionable exemption requests.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The statutory liability chain is unforgiving. Under IRC Section 1445(e), if the buyer fails to withhold the required amount, the buyer is personally liable for the tax plus interest and penalties. The buyer can then seek indemnification from the listing agent, title company, or anyone who represented that withholding was unnecessary.

**Idaho courts have upheld negligence claims against real estate licensees who failed to identify a seller's nonresident status or who affirmatively told a buyer that no withholding was needed.** The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) can also pursue disciplinary action under Idaho Code Section 54-2060 if an agent's failure to disclose material transaction facts—including withholding obligations—amounts to incompetence or dishonesty.

The financial exposure on a $2 million Sun Valley property is staggering: $300,000 in federal withholding plus $40,000 in state withholding. If that money leaves the country with the seller and the IRS comes looking, the buyer writes that check. That buyer's attorney then looks at every email, text, and document from the transaction to determine who knew—or should have known—the seller was foreign.

Deal cancellation is another risk. If FIRPTA withholding is discovered mid-transaction and the seller refuses to accept a reduced net proceed, the deal collapses. Buyers who learn they bear personal liability for a six-figure tax withholding may walk, especially if the [purchase agreement contingency deadlines](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-contingency-deadlines-2026) have already passed and they lack contractual exit rights.

## Five Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make With FIRPTA

The first mistake is assuming the title company handles everything. Title companies facilitate the mechanics—they calculate withholding, prepare Form 8288, and submit payment. But they rely on accurate information from the parties. If an agent never asks the seller about citizenship status, the title company may never learn the seller is foreign until it is too late.

The second error is confusing residency with citizenship. A foreign national who lives in Boise year-round and holds a green card is generally treated as a U.S. person for FIRPTA purposes. Conversely, a U.S. citizen who moved abroad permanently is still a U.S. person. Agents conflate "lives here" with "not foreign" and miss the actual test, which hinges on immigration status, not mailing address.

Third, agents fail to address FIRPTA at listing. The time to obtain a non-foreign affidavit (also called a FIRPTA affidavit or certification of non-foreign status) is during the listing intake process—not three days before closing. Late discovery of foreign status creates chaos around Form 8288-B applications that need 90 days to process.

Fourth, agents miscalculate the buyer's residence exemption. The reduced rate or full exemption applies only when the buyer personally intends to reside in the property for at least 50% of the days it is in use during each of the first two 12-month periods. Investment buyers, LLC purchasers, and second-home buyers do not qualify. Agents sometimes tell buyers they are exempt because the property "could be" a residence.

Fifth, agents ignore the Idaho nonresident withholding for domestic out-of-state sellers. A California investor selling a Meridian rental triggers Idaho Form 51 withholding. This is separate from FIRPTA but frequently overlooked because agents associate "withholding" exclusively with foreign sellers.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Idaho bear supervisory responsibility under Idaho Code Section 54-2038. If an agent under your license closes a transaction with a foreign seller and no withholding is made, IREC can hold the broker accountable for inadequate supervision.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Listing file contains FIRPTA affidavit | Signed non-foreign certification or notation of foreign status | No affidavit and no mention of seller's status |
| Transaction timeline accounts for 8288-B | If seller is foreign, file shows withholding certificate application | Foreign seller identified but no withholding plan documented |
| Idaho Form 51 addressed | Closing disclosure shows 2% withholding or exemption documentation | Out-of-state seller with no withholding noted |
| Buyer informed of liability | Written communication showing buyer understands FIRPTA obligation | No buyer acknowledgment in file |
| Title company coordination documented | Email or notes confirming title company received foreign status info | Agent assumed title company "just knows" |

**Brokers should add a FIRPTA compliance checkpoint to their transaction checklists at two stages: listing intake and contract ratification.** The listing intake checkpoint confirms seller status. The contract ratification checkpoint confirms the buyer's side is aware of withholding obligations and that the title company has received accurate information. Without these checkpoints, [deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at the worst possible moment—during final closing preparations.

Training is non-negotiable. Idaho continuing education does not currently mandate FIRPTA-specific coursework, but brokers can require it internally. A 60-minute training covering Form 8288, Form 8288-A, Form 8288-B, Idaho Form 51, and the FIRPTA affidavit template should be standard onboarding for any agent working in resort markets like Ketchum, Sun Valley, Sandpoint, or Coeur d'Alene where foreign buyers and sellers are common.

## Practical Workflow for Listing Agents

At the listing appointment, ask directly: "Are you a U.S. citizen or permanent resident?" Document the answer. If the seller is a foreign national, explain the withholding obligation immediately and discuss whether to file Form 8288-B for a reduced withholding certificate before going to market. Ninety days of processing time means this application should be submitted before the property hits the MLS.

During escrow, coordinate with the title company in writing. Send an email stating: "Seller has confirmed foreign national status. Please prepare for FIRPTA withholding at 15% of gross sale price unless a withholding certificate is received." This email protects you. It creates a paper trail showing you identified the issue and communicated it to the responsible party.

If the seller disputes withholding, do not provide legal advice. Refer the seller to a tax attorney or CPA who specializes in international tax. The [Idaho State Tax Commission](https://tax.idaho.gov/) provides guidance on the state-level withholding but does not advise on federal FIRPTA obligations. Your role is identification and coordination—not tax counsel.
