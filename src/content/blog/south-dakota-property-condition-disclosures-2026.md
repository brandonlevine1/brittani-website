South Dakota's disclosure rules sit at the intersection of state statute, federal mandate, and common-sense risk management, yet agents still trip over them every transaction cycle. Understanding **South Dakota property condition & environmental disclosures 2026** requirements is not optional if you want to keep your license, your broker's trust, and your deal pipeline intact. The stakes are real: a single missed form can unwind a closing, trigger civil litigation, or land you in front of the South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC).

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, deadlines, and enforcement mechanisms that govern residential property disclosures across the state heading into 2026.

## The Statutory Framework Behind South Dakota Disclosures

South Dakota codified its seller disclosure requirements under [SDCL Chapter 43-4](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-4), specifically sections 43-4-38 through 43-4-44. These statutes mandate that sellers of residential real property provide a written disclosure statement covering the known condition of the property before or at the time a purchase agreement is signed.

**The law applies to transactions involving one-to-four-unit residential dwellings occupied or intended for occupancy.** That scope catches single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes, and four-plexes. Commercial property, agricultural land sold without a residential dwelling, and certain exempt transactions fall outside the requirement.

Exemptions under SDCL 43-4-39 include transfers by foreclosure, transfers by a fiduciary administering an estate, court-ordered transfers, transfers between co-owners, and sales of new construction covered by a builder's warranty. Agents who assume an exemption applies without verifying the statutory basis are courting trouble.

## Forms You Must Know by Number

The South Dakota Association of REALTORS (SDAR) publishes the standardized forms that most brokerages across the state adopt. The primary disclosure document is the **Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement**, which SDAR periodically updates. Agents should confirm they are using the most current version, as outdated forms can omit newly required line items and expose the transaction to challenge.

The disclosure statement covers structural components, roof condition, plumbing, electrical systems, HVAC, water and sewer, environmental hazards, and known neighborhood nuisances. Sellers must mark items as "Yes," "No," or "Unknown," and any "Yes" answer requires a written explanation. The form also includes a section on whether the property has been subject to flooding, drainage problems, or soil instability.

For properties built before 1978, federal law under 42 U.S.C. 4852d and the EPA/HUD lead-based paint disclosure rule kicks in. Agents must provide the buyer with the **EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home"** and ensure the seller completes a separate lead-based paint disclosure form. The buyer then receives a 10-day opportunity to conduct a lead inspection, which can be waived in writing but never eliminated by the agent or seller unilaterally. South Dakota agents working older housing stock — and there is plenty of it in Sioux Falls, Rapid City, and Aberdeen — should treat the lead-paint disclosure as a parallel, non-negotiable requirement. ## South Dakota Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026: What Sellers Must Reveal

Environmental disclosures go beyond lead paint. South Dakota sellers must disclose known contamination from underground storage tanks, hazardous substances, radon, asbestos, and mold if they have actual knowledge. The state does not mandate seller-paid testing for these hazards, but the disclosure obligation attaches to anything the seller knows or reasonably should know.

**Radon is a significant concern across much of South Dakota, with the EPA classifying large portions of the state as Zone 1 (highest potential).** The seller's disclosure form asks about radon testing and results. If a seller has conducted a radon test and the results exceeded the EPA action level of 4.0 pCi/L, that information must appear on the disclosure. An agent who coaches a seller to omit radon test results is inviting both SDREC discipline and civil liability.

Water quality disclosures matter too, particularly for rural properties relying on private wells. Sellers should disclose known issues with nitrate levels, bacteria contamination, or other water quality deficiencies. While South Dakota does not mandate pre-sale well testing at the state level, some municipalities and counties have adopted local ordinances requiring it.

| Disclosure Category | Statutory Basis | Seller Obligation | Agent Duty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Property condition (structural, systems) | SDCL 43-4-38 | Complete written disclosure form | Ensure form is delivered before or at contract signing |
| Lead-based paint (pre-1978 homes) | 42 U.S.C. 4852d | Disclose known lead paint, provide EPA pamphlet | Confirm form completion and buyer's 10-day inspection right |

| Underground storage tanks | SDCL 43-4-38 | Disclose existence and known contamination | Flag for buyer due diligence |

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

SDCL 43-4-44 spells out the consequences for sellers who fail to disclose or who misrepresent property conditions: the buyer may recover actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney fees. But agents are not immune from the fallout.

**If a listing agent has actual knowledge of a material defect and fails to ensure it appears on the disclosure, that agent faces civil liability alongside the seller.** The SDREC can also pursue disciplinary action under SDCL 36-21A, which governs licensee conduct. Sanctions range from reprimand and mandatory continuing education to license suspension or revocation.

Buyer's agents also carry risk. Under South Dakota agency law, a buyer's agent owes a duty of reasonable care, which includes flagging obvious defects observed during showings and advising buyers to conduct independent inspections. An agent who notices water stains, foundation cracks, or unusual odors and says nothing is not protected by the argument that "it's the seller's disclosure, not mine."

Deal cancellation is the most immediate consequence. Under most SDAR purchase agreement forms, the buyer retains the right to rescind if the seller fails to deliver the required disclosure within the contractual deadline. That deadline is typically tied to the inspection contingency period, and missing it can blow up a closing that took weeks to negotiate. ## Five Common Mistakes Agents Make on Disclosures

The first mistake is treating the disclosure as a formality instead of a legal instrument. Agents who hand the form to the seller with a casual "just check the boxes" instruction are setting up everyone for failure. The seller needs to understand that each answer carries legal weight, and the agent should walk through the form section by section without filling it out on the seller's behalf.

The second mistake is delivering the disclosure late. SDCL 43-4-40 requires delivery before or at the time of the purchase agreement. Agents who wait until after mutual acceptance create a window where the buyer may not have received material information before committing. Late delivery also gives the buyer a statutory rescission right under SDCL 43-4-42.

Third, agents confuse "unknown" with "no." A seller who has never tested for radon should mark the radon field as "Unknown," not "No." Coaching a seller to select "No" when the accurate answer is "Unknown" is a misrepresentation, and the SDREC has flagged this pattern in past disciplinary proceedings.

Fourth, agents fail to update the disclosure when new information surfaces. If the seller discovers a roof leak after completing the form but before closing, a supplemental disclosure is required. The obligation is ongoing, not static. Agents who do not prompt sellers to amend the form when circumstances change are exposing the transaction to post-closing claims.

Fifth, agents neglect the lead-paint disclosure on pre-1978 properties entirely. This is a federal violation carrying penalties of up to $19,507 per occurrence under the Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act. The fine is per violation, not per transaction, and HUD enforcement has not slowed down.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-63, which means a sloppy agent's disclosure failure can become the broker's problem fast. Auditing disclosure compliance is not a suggestion; it is a fiduciary and regulatory obligation.

| Audit Checkpoint | What Broker Should Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form version | Current SDAR form is being used, not an outdated version | Every listing intake |
| Timing of delivery | Disclosure delivered before or at purchase agreement execution | Every transaction file |
| Lead-paint compliance | Separate lead-paint form completed for pre-1978 properties; EPA pamphlet in file | Every applicable listing |
| Supplemental disclosures | Updated disclosure filed if material facts changed before closing | File review at closing |
| Agent conduct | Agent did not complete the form on the seller's behalf | Spot audits quarterly |

**Brokers should build a disclosure checklist into their transaction management workflow and review it at three touchpoints: listing intake, mutual acceptance, and pre-closing file review.** Relying on agents to self-police is how violations accumulate undetected. ## Practical Workflow for Disclosure Compliance

A clean disclosure workflow starts at the listing appointment. The agent provides the seller with the disclosure form, explains each section, and sets a deadline for completion — ideally before the property goes active on the MLS. The completed form goes into the transaction file and is made available to prospective buyers or their agents upon request.

At the time of offer, the listing agent confirms that the buyer or buyer's agent has received the disclosure. The purchase agreement should reference the disclosure by date. If the seller amends the disclosure at any point before closing, the amendment gets documented, signed, and delivered to the buyer with a new acknowledgment.

For pre-1978 properties, the lead-paint disclosure and EPA pamphlet delivery happen at the same stage. The buyer's signed acknowledgment, including their election to waive or exercise the 10-day inspection period, goes into the file.
