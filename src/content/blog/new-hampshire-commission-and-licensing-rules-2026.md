# New Hampshire Commission & Licensing Rules 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Know

The New Hampshire commission & licensing rules 2026 cycle brings renewed scrutiny to how agents disclose compensation, maintain active licenses, and document buyer representation. The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), operating under RSA 331-A, has sharpened its enforcement posture following the NAR settlement ripple effects that reached every state. If you hold a New Hampshire license—or supervise agents who do—the margin for error on commission documentation and licensing compliance has never been thinner.

This is not a casual refresher. This is a statute-by-statute breakdown of what the state expects, where agents keep getting burned, and what brokers should be auditing right now.

## The Regulatory Framework Governing New Hampshire Agents

New Hampshire real estate licensing falls under RSA Chapter 331-A, administered by the NHREC within the Office of Professional Licensure and Certification (OPLC). The Commission has rulemaking authority under NH Administrative Rules Rea 100-800, which cover everything from license qualifications to disciplinary proceedings.

**Every active licensee operates under the direct supervision of a principal broker, and RSA 331-A:16 makes clear that no commission or fee can be paid to an agent except through their employing broker.** This is not a suggestion—it is a statutory mandate that carries civil liability and potential license revocation. An agent who accepts compensation directly from a client, another brokerage, or a third party without routing it through their broker violates state law.

The NHREC uses Form NHREC-1 (License Application), Form NHREC-3 (Transfer Request), and Form NHREC-7 (Disclosure of Agency) as core regulatory documents. Familiarity with these form numbers matters because disciplinary complaints often hinge on whether the correct form was executed at the correct time.

## Commission Structures and the Post-Settlement Reality

Since the NAR settlement took effect in August 2024, New Hampshire agents have operated under decoupled commission rules. Offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer appear in MLS listings on the NNEREN (Northern New England Real Estate Network) system. This means buyer-agent compensation must be negotiated, documented, and disclosed outside of the MLS entirely.

RSA 331-A:25-b requires that all compensation arrangements be reduced to writing. The written buyer representation agreement must specify the amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, and it must be signed before the agent provides substantive brokerage services. Verbal agreements do not satisfy the statute.

For listing agents, the seller's obligation to pay a cooperating broker (if any) now exists only if negotiated in the listing agreement or a separate written agreement between the parties. Brokers should update their listing agreement templates to include a specific field addressing whether the seller will offer buyer-agent compensation and, if so, the amount or method of calculation.

| Document | Purpose | When Required |
|----------|---------|---------------|
| Written Buyer Representation Agreement | Establishes compensation terms with buyer | Before substantive services |
| Listing Agreement (Commission Section) | Defines seller's compensation obligations | At listing execution |
| Form NHREC-7 (Agency Disclosure) | Discloses agency relationship type | At first substantive contact |
| Commission Disbursement Authorization | Authorizes broker-to-broker payment | At or before closing |

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Deadlines

New Hampshire real estate licenses renew on a two-year cycle, with the renewal date tied to the licensee's birth month. Active salesperson and broker licenses require 15 hours of continuing education per cycle, including a 3-hour mandatory core course set by NHREC each period. The 2025-2026 core course covers updated commission disclosure rules and fair housing enforcement trends.

**Failure to complete CE before your renewal deadline triggers automatic license inactivation.** NHREC provides a 90-day grace period under Rea 403.01, during which you may renew with a late fee, but you cannot practice during the lapse. Any transaction activity performed on an inactive license is a violation of RSA 331-A:28, which carries fines up to $2,000 per occurrence and potential criminal misdemeanor charges.

Broker licenses carry additional requirements. Principal brokers must complete 24 hours of CE, including a broker-management course, and must maintain errors and omissions insurance with minimum coverage of $100,000 per occurrence. The OPLC audits compliance through random verification requests, and brokers who cannot produce proof of current E&O coverage face immediate license suspension.

For agents considering [how licensing portability works across state lines](/blog/wisconsin-license-reciprocity-portability-2026), New Hampshire has limited reciprocity agreements. Agents licensed in Maine, Vermont, or Massachusetts may apply for a New Hampshire license with a reduced exam requirement, but must still complete the NH-specific state portion of the exam and submit Form NHREC-1 with proof of active out-of-state licensure.

## Common Mistakes That Trigger Disciplinary Action

Disciplinary cases before the NHREC reveal consistent patterns. These are not obscure technicalities—they are errors that experienced agents make under time pressure, often without realizing the exposure until a complaint lands.

**First: Failing to provide Form NHREC-7 (Agency Disclosure) at first substantive contact.** The statute requires disclosure before providing any brokerage services, which includes property tours, pricing opinions, and negotiation advice. Agents who build rapport over several conversations before formalizing the relationship often miss this window. The NHREC interprets "first substantive contact" broadly, and waiting until an offer is written is far too late.

Second: Accepting referral fees or commission splits from out-of-state agents without broker authorization. Under RSA 331-A:16, all compensation flows through the principal broker. An agent who receives a direct Venmo or check from a referring agent in another state—even for a legitimate referral—has violated the statute. The fix is simple: route all payments through your broker's trust or operating account.

Third: Practicing on an expired or inactive license. This happens most often during the grace period when agents assume they can still close pending transactions. They cannot. Even if the transaction was initiated while the license was active, earning commission on a closing that occurs during a lapse period is prohibited.

Fourth: Failing to disclose dual agency in writing using the proper NHREC-approved disclosure language. NH Administrative Rule Rea 701.01 requires written informed consent from both parties before a licensee or brokerage practices disclosed dual agency. Generic brokerage disclosure forms that do not match NHREC-approved language expose the agent and broker to disciplinary complaints and potential transaction rescission.

Fifth: Misrepresenting commission terms to buyers. Post-settlement, some agents have told buyers that "the seller always pays" or that buyer-agent compensation is "free." These statements violate RSA 331-A:26 (misrepresentation) and NHREC's advertising rules under Rea 601. The Commission issued an advisory bulletin in late 2025 specifically flagging this language as misleading.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers bear supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:15 for the acts of their affiliated agents. This is not vicarious liability in the traditional sense—it is direct statutory responsibility. A broker who fails to maintain adequate supervision systems faces personal disciplinary action regardless of whether they knew about the violation.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Statutory Basis |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Agency disclosure forms on file | Every transaction | RSA 331-A:25-a, Rea 701 |
| Written buyer representation agreements | Every buyer transaction | RSA 331-A:25-b |
| CE completion verification | Annually | Rea 403 |
| E&O insurance currency | Annually | Rea 302.05 |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | RSA 331-A:22, Rea 801 |
| Advertising compliance review | Quarterly | Rea 601 |

**Brokers should conduct a quarterly file audit that specifically checks for signed NHREC-7 forms, executed buyer representation agreements with compensation terms, and proper commission disbursement documentation.** Missing any of these in a random NHREC audit can result in a consent order, mandatory additional education, or license suspension.

Trust account management deserves particular attention. RSA 331-A:22 requires that earnest money deposits be placed in the broker's trust account within five business days of contract execution unless the contract specifies otherwise. Monthly reconciliations are required under Rea 801, and discrepancies must be reported to the NHREC within 30 days. For agents who [miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), a lapsed trust deposit is one of the fastest paths to a formal complaint.

## Penalties and Liability for Non-Compliance

The NHREC's disciplinary authority under RSA 331-A:28 includes license revocation, suspension, fines up to $2,000 per violation, mandatory education, and public consent orders that appear on the [OPLC licensee lookup database](https://www.oplc.nh.gov/real-estate-commission). These are not theoretical outcomes—the Commission publishes enforcement actions quarterly.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability exposure. A buyer or seller who discovers they were not properly informed about agency relationships or commission structures can seek rescission of the purchase agreement or damages in superior court. New Hampshire courts have upheld claims under RSA 331-A in cases where agents failed to provide timely written disclosures, with damages including return of commission plus consequential losses.

Criminal exposure exists as well. Practicing without a license under RSA 331-A:2 is a misdemeanor offense. Misappropriation of trust funds can escalate to felony theft charges under RSA 637. These are not risks limited to bad actors—they catch otherwise competent agents who let administrative compliance slide during busy seasons.

The relationship between [proper transaction documentation and deal survival](/blog/where-deals-break-down) cannot be overstated. A single missing form can unwind a closing, trigger a complaint, and generate legal fees that dwarf the commission at stake.
