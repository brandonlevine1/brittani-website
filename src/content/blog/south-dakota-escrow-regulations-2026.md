## Understanding South Dakota Escrow Regulations 2026

South Dakota escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who get sloppy with trust fund handling. The state's regulatory framework, anchored in South Dakota Codified Laws (SDCL) Title 36, Chapter 21A, and the administrative rules under ARSD Title 20, Article 69, governs how every dollar of earnest money moves from a buyer's hands into a trust account and eventually to closing. Getting this wrong does not just kill deals — it puts licenses on the line.

The [South Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/) (SDREC), housed under the Department of Labor and Regulation, enforces these rules with auditing authority, complaint investigation powers, and the ability to impose fines, suspensions, and revocations. If you have been treating escrow handling as a formality rather than a regulatory obligation, 2026 is the year to correct course.

Escrow compliance intersects with nearly every other transaction management function. Agents who struggle with [deadline tracking in general](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) will find that escrow timelines punish that habit faster than almost any other part of the deal.

## The Statutes That Govern Escrow in South Dakota

**SDCL 36-21A-72 is the statutory backbone of escrow handling for South Dakota licensees.** It mandates that all funds received by a licensee on behalf of a principal must be deposited into a trust account maintained by the broker. The statute does not offer wiggle room — funds received means funds deposited, and "reasonable time" is the standard the Commission uses to evaluate compliance.

ARSD 20:69:04:03 adds administrative specificity. This rule requires that trust accounts be maintained in a federally insured financial institution located in South Dakota or a state contiguous to South Dakota. The account must be designated as a "trust" or "escrow" account, and the broker's name must appear on the account.

ARSD 20:69:04:04 addresses record-keeping. Brokers must maintain a ledger or accounting system that tracks each deposit by transaction, showing the date received, the date deposited, the source, the amount, and the disbursement. These records must be retained for a minimum of three years and must be available for Commission inspection upon request.

## Forms and Documents Agents Actually Use

South Dakota residential transactions rely on forms published by the South Dakota Association of REALTORS (SDAR). The primary form governing escrow terms between the parties is the **SDAR Form 101 — Residential Purchase Agreement**. Section 3 of this form addresses earnest money, specifying the amount, the holder (broker or title company), and the deposit timeline.

**SDAR Form 107 — Addendum to Purchase Agreement** is used when parties need to modify escrow terms after execution. This form matters because any change to the escrow holder, the deposit amount, or the disbursement conditions must be documented in writing — verbal agreements carry no weight if a dispute reaches the Commission.

SDAR Form 201, the **Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement**, does not directly control escrow, but it frequently triggers contingency timelines that affect when escrow funds become at risk. A buyer exercising an inspection contingency under the terms of Form 101 can request return of earnest money, and the escrow holder must know the exact contractual basis before disbursing.

| Form | Name | Escrow-Related Function |
|------|------|------------------------|
| SDAR 101 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Sets earnest money amount, holder, and deposit deadline |
| SDAR 107 | Addendum to Purchase Agreement | Modifies escrow terms post-execution |
| SDAR 201 | Seller's Property Condition Disclosure | Triggers contingency timelines affecting fund release |
| SDAR 301 | Listing Agreement | Identifies broker's trust account for commission escrow |
| Broker Internal | Trust Account Ledger | Tracks deposits, disbursements, and balances per ARSD 20:69:04:04 |

Agents who handle transactions without referencing the correct form numbers are already operating in a gray area. Every escrow-related decision should trace back to a signed document.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of escrow violations in South Dakota scale from embarrassing to career-ending. SDCL 36-21A-90 gives the SDREC authority to discipline licensees for trust account violations, and the Commission has consistently shown it will act.

**Fines can reach $10,000 per violation under SDCL 36-21A-91.** That is per violation, not per complaint — meaning a single audit that uncovers multiple mishandled deposits can produce a penalty that dwarfs the commissions earned on those transactions. The Commission can also order restitution to harmed parties.

License suspension or revocation is on the table for serious or repeated offenses. SDCL 36-21A-86 lists "failure to account for or to remit any moneys coming into the licensee's possession which belong to others" as explicit grounds for revocation. Beyond the regulatory penalties, agents who mishandle escrow funds face civil liability from aggrieved buyers or sellers, and in extreme cases — such as commingling or conversion — criminal prosecution under South Dakota's theft statutes.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical consequence. When a buyer or seller loses confidence in fund handling, the transaction collapses. Agents who have experienced a [deal falling apart over process failures](/blog/where-deals-break-down) know that escrow disputes rank among the most common triggers.

## Common Escrow Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make

**Mistake one: depositing earnest money into a personal account.** This sounds too obvious to happen, but the Commission sees it every year, particularly from newer agents who receive a check at a showing and deposit it into the wrong account before notifying their broker. Under SDCL 36-21A-72, all funds must go into the broker's trust account. No exceptions.

Mistake two: failing to deposit within the contract's stated timeframe. SDAR Form 101 specifies when the deposit must occur. Agents who hold checks in desk drawers, car consoles, or briefcases past the deadline are violating the purchase agreement and inviting both regulatory action and a buyer's claim for breach. The Commission interprets "reasonable time" strictly — two banking days from acceptance is the practical ceiling unless the contract explicitly allows more.

Mistake three: disbursing earnest money without written authorization from all parties. When a deal falls apart and one side demands the funds, the escrow holder cannot simply comply with whichever party yells louder. ARSD 20:69:04:05 requires that disbursement follow the terms of the purchase agreement or a mutual written release. Absent mutual agreement, the funds stay in trust until a court orders otherwise or the parties reach a signed resolution.

Mistake four: sloppy record-keeping that cannot survive an audit. Brokers who use spreadsheets with missing dates, unlabeled deposits, or no clear connection between a ledger entry and a specific transaction are setting themselves up for Commission findings. The three-year retention requirement under ARSD 20:69:04:04 means that a 2026 transaction could be audited as late as 2029.

Mistake five: commingling personal or operating funds with trust funds. Brokers sometimes deposit a small amount of personal funds into the trust account to cover bank fees, which South Dakota permits only if the amount does not exceed $500 and is clearly documented. Anything beyond that threshold crosses into commingling territory and triggers potential discipline.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry the ultimate liability for trust account compliance. SDCL 36-21A-72 places the duty on the broker, not the individual agent, meaning that an agent's mistake becomes the broker's problem the moment the Commission opens a file.

**Every brokerage in South Dakota should conduct monthly trust account reconciliations.** This is not a suggestion from best-practices guides — it is the practical minimum to catch discrepancies before they compound. The reconciliation must compare the bank statement balance against the ledger balance and against the sum of all individual transaction balances held in the account.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Regulatory Basis | Risk if Missed |
|------------|-----------|------------------|----------------|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | ARSD 20:69:04:04 | Undetected shortages, Commission findings |
| Individual transaction ledger review | Per transaction, at deposit and disbursement | ARSD 20:69:04:04 | Misdirected funds, civil liability |
| Deposit timing verification | Within two banking days of each deposit | SDCL 36-21A-72 | Disciplinary action, contract breach claims |
| Disbursement authorization check | Before every release of funds | ARSD 20:69:04:05 | Unauthorized disbursement, restitution orders |
| Three-year record retention confirmation | Annually | ARSD 20:69:04:04 | Inability to defend against future complaints |

Brokers should also maintain a written escrow policy manual distributed to every agent in the office. The manual should specify who is authorized to accept earnest money, how quickly it must be delivered to the broker, and the exact procedures for handling disputes. When the Commission investigates, the first question is often whether the brokerage had a documented policy — and whether the agent followed it.

Training matters as much as documentation. An annual compliance review session focused on escrow handling, ideally before license renewal season, gives brokers a defensible record that they took reasonable steps to educate their agents. The SDREC has historically treated brokerages with documented training programs more favorably during disciplinary proceedings than those operating on verbal expectations alone.

## Disputed Escrow Funds and Interpleader Actions

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the earnest money, the escrow holder faces a genuine legal bind. South Dakota does not have a statutory escrow dispute resolution process specific to real estate, which means the holder must either obtain mutual written release or file an interpleader action in circuit court under SDCL Chapter 15-6.

**An interpleader action allows the escrow holder to deposit the disputed funds with the court and step out of the fight.** The court then determines entitlement. Filing an interpleader protects the broker from liability to either party, but it comes with legal costs and delays that neither side wants.

Agents should understand that [using structured checklists to track contingency deadlines and cancellation terms](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is one of the most effective ways to prevent disputes from reaching the interpleader stage. Most escrow fights start because someone missed a contractual deadline and both sides now claim the deposit.
