The 2026 legislative session hit Oregon real estate practitioners harder than most expected. If you hold an active license in this state and have not yet reviewed the Oregon new real estate laws 2026 package, you are running transactions on borrowed time. Three separate bills signed by the governor between March and June reshape disclosure obligations, buyer representation mechanics, and the penalty framework the Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) now wields against non-compliant licensees.

This is not a minor clean-up year. The statutory changes touch every phase of a residential deal, from the initial buyer consultation to the final recording. Below is a detailed breakdown of what changed, what it means operationally, and where agents are most likely to trip.

## Oregon New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Framework

**House Bill 3042, Senate Bill 891, and Senate Bill 1107 collectively form the backbone of this year's regulatory shift.** HB 3042 amends ORS 105.465 through 105.490, expanding the mandatory seller disclosure categories. SB 891 rewrites portions of ORS 696.800 through 696.870, modifying buyer agency requirements. SB 1107 updates the disciplinary authority granted to OREA under ORS 696.990.

All three bills carry a September 1, 2026 effective date. The OREA published its [interpretive guidance and updated administrative rules](https://www.oregon.gov/rea) in July, giving licensees roughly 60 days to adjust their practices. That is not much runway for a state with over 22,000 active licensees.

The Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) committee released updated form versions in tandem. Agents who rely on outdated form packets after September 1 face immediate compliance exposure, a risk that compounds with every transaction closed on stale paperwork.

## Buyer Representation Changes Under SB 891

SB 891 codifies what many brokerages already practiced voluntarily: a written buyer representation agreement must precede the delivery of substantive real estate services. The statute defines "substantive services" broadly, encompassing property tours, offer preparation, negotiation on behalf of a buyer, and market analysis prepared for a specific buyer's purchasing decision.

**The critical operational detail is timing.** Under revised ORS 696.805(3), the agreement must be signed before the first showing, not before the first offer. Agents who historically toured properties with unrepresented prospects and only formalized the relationship at offer stage now face a clear statutory prohibition.

OREF Form 040-2026 (Buyer Representation Agreement) replaces the prior 040-2024 version. The new form adds mandatory fields for compensation disclosure, including the specific dollar amount or percentage the buyer's agent expects, and whether that compensation will be sought from the seller, the buyer, or a combination. This mirrors the broader post-NAR-settlement framework but carries Oregon-specific statutory penalties rather than relying solely on MLS policy enforcement.

| Requirement | Before Sept. 1, 2026 | After Sept. 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Written buyer agreement | Recommended, not mandatory | Mandatory before substantive services |
| Compensation disclosure | Required at offer stage | Required in the buyer agreement itself |
| Form version | OREF 040-2024 | OREF 040-2026 |
| Penalty for non-compliance | OREA discretionary discipline | Mandatory civil penalty up to $5,000 per violation |
| Buyer's right to cancel | Per agreement terms | Statutory 3-day cooling-off period added |

The three-day cooling-off period is new. Under ORS 696.805(5), a buyer may rescind the representation agreement without cause within three business days of signing. Agents should not schedule high-stakes showings or prepare offers during that window unless the buyer provides a written waiver of the cooling-off period on OREF Form 040A-2026.

## Expanded Seller Disclosure Obligations Under HB 3042

Oregon's seller disclosure framework has long been one of the more detailed in the country. HB 3042 pushes it further by adding two new mandatory disclosure categories to the Seller's Property Disclosure Statement: wildfire risk and insurance availability.

Section 14 of OREF Form 001-2026 now requires sellers to disclose whether the property is located within a mapped wildfire-risk zone as designated by the Oregon Department of Forestry under ORS 477.027, whether the seller has received any notice of non-renewal or cancellation of homeowner's insurance due to wildfire risk, and whether the seller is aware of any pending or completed wildfire mitigation assessments on the property. Section 15 addresses insurance availability more broadly, asking whether the seller experienced difficulty obtaining or renewing coverage during ownership and whether coverage requires a surplus-lines carrier.

**Listing agents bear affirmative responsibility under Administrative Rule 863-015-0235 to verify the seller has completed these sections before the property is marketed.** This is not a passive obligation. The rule states that a listing agent "shall review the disclosure form for completeness and accuracy" and "shall not publish a listing where material sections remain blank without documented seller refusal."

If a seller refuses to complete Section 14 or 15, the agent must document that refusal on OREF Form 001A-2026 (Seller Disclosure Refusal Addendum) and deliver the refusal addendum alongside the disclosure to prospective buyers. Omitting this step exposes both the agent and the brokerage to disciplinary action.

For agents managing [deadlines across multiple active files](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), this adds another critical checkpoint that cannot be deferred to closing.

## Penalty Escalation Under SB 1107

The most consequential change for many agents will not be the new forms themselves but the enforcement teeth behind them. SB 1107 restructures OREA's disciplinary authority under ORS 696.990, replacing the prior discretionary fine schedule with a tiered mandatory penalty framework.

| Violation Category | Prior Maximum Penalty | New Mandatory Minimum | New Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Failure to provide required disclosure | $1,000 | $2,500 per transaction | $10,000 |
| Operating without required written agreement | $500 | $2,500 per occurrence | $5,000 |
| Material misrepresentation on forms | $5,000 | $5,000 | $25,000 |
| Pattern of non-compliance (3+ violations in 24 months) | License suspension discretionary | License suspension mandatory 90 days | Revocation |

The shift from discretionary to mandatory minimums means OREA investigators no longer have the latitude to issue warnings for first-time disclosure failures. A single missed wildfire-risk section on one transaction now carries a minimum $2,500 civil penalty with no negotiation room.

License suspension becomes automatic at the third substantiated violation within a rolling 24-month window. That timeline is short enough to catch agents who close a high volume of transactions but have sloppy compliance habits. Three errors across 60 deals in two years triggers mandatory suspension.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make (and How to Avoid Them)

Based on the OREA's July guidance bulletin and conversations with managing brokers across the state, several failure patterns are already emerging in early-adoption transactions.

**First, agents are using the wrong form version.** The September 1 effective date does not mean you can use 2024 forms for transactions that opened before September 1. The OREA's interpretive guidance specifies that transactions where the disclosure has not yet been delivered to the buyer must use the 2026 form, regardless of when the listing agreement was signed. If your disclosure was already delivered and acknowledged before September 1, you are grandfathered. If not, swap the form.

Second, agents are treating the buyer agreement cooling-off period as a formality and scheduling immediate showings. If a buyer later rescinds within the three-day window, any offers submitted during that period lack valid agency authority. The seller could argue the offer was defective, and E&O carriers have already signaled they may deny claims arising from this scenario.

Third, listing agents are leaving Sections 14 and 15 blank when the seller says "I don't know." Under the new rule, "unknown" is a valid answer that the seller must affirmatively mark. A blank field is not the same as an "unknown" response, and OREA treats blank fields as incomplete disclosures triggering the buyer's rescission right. This distinction will catch agents who [rely on memory rather than structured checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to verify form completeness.

Fourth, agents are failing to update their CRM templates and auto-populated form packets. If your transaction management software still pulls OREF 001-2024 from the template library, every disclosure you generate after September 1 is non-compliant by default. Audit your tech stack now.

Fifth, buyer's agents are not retaining proof of when the representation agreement was signed relative to the first showing. Without a timestamp or dated acknowledgment, an OREA investigation defaults to the assumption that services preceded the agreement. Digital signature platforms with audit trails solve this problem entirely.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.810. When an agent in the brokerage violates the new disclosure or buyer agreement statutes, the broker faces separate disciplinary exposure unless they can demonstrate active supervisory compliance measures.

**OREA's updated Administrative Rule 863-015-0250 now requires brokers to maintain a written compliance plan that addresses the 2026 statutory changes.** The plan must be distributed to all affiliated licensees and updated annually. Brokers who cannot produce this document during an audit face a standalone penalty of $1,500 per quarter of non-compliance.

Specifically, brokers should audit four areas before September 1. First, verify that all form templates across every platform used by affiliated agents reflect the 2026 OREF versions. Second, confirm that buyer's agents have a documented process for obtaining signed representation agreements before substantive services begin. Third, establish a file-review protocol that checks Sections 14 and 15 of every seller disclosure for completeness or documented refusal. Fourth, update the brokerage policy manual to reflect the new penalty tiers so agents understand the personal financial risk of non-compliance.

For brokerages managing dozens or hundreds of agents, manual file review becomes unsustainable. The volume of compliance checkpoints per transaction has increased enough that [understanding where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) is no longer optional knowledge — it is a risk-management requirement.

Quarterly transaction audits should sample at minimum 10% of closed files per agent. Brokers should flag any file missing the signed buyer agreement timestamp, incomplete disclosure sections, or outdated form versions. Document the audit findings in writing and retain them for the OREA's standard three-year lookback period.

## How This Intersects with Existing Oregon Requirements

These changes layer on top of Oregon's existing agency disclosure framework under ORS 696.800-696.870 and the lead-based paint disclosure requirements that remain unchanged. The Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet (OREF Form 010) still must be delivered at first contact. The new buyer representation agreement requirement does not replace the agency disclosure — it adds to it.

The sequencing now looks like this for a buyer-side agent: deliver OREF Form 010 at first substantive contact, then execute OREF Form 040-2026 before any showing or service, then honor the three-day cooling-off period unless waived in writing on Form 040A-2026, then proceed with services. Missing any step in this sequence creates independent grounds for discipline.

For listing agents, the stacking is equally rigid. Execute the listing agreement, verify the seller completes OREF Form 001-2026 including Sections 14 and 15, obtain OREF Form 001A-2026 if the seller refuses any section, then market the property. The property cannot appear on the MLS or any public marketing channel until the disclosure is complete or the refusal is documented. This is a hard line under the new administrative rule, not a best practice.
