## Why Oregon Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026 Matter More Than Ever

Oregon reported over $18 million in real estate wire fraud losses in 2024, according to FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center data for the state. The Oregon wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 framework places direct responsibility on licensees to warn clients, verify instructions, and document every step. Agents who treat this as a mere checkbox exercise are gambling with their license and their client's life savings.

The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) has steadily tightened expectations around wire fraud since the passage of ORS 696.301, which codifies the standard of care for licensed activity. While Oregon does not yet mandate a single statutory form for wire fraud advisories the way some states do, the regulatory body has made clear through advisory opinions and disciplinary actions that agents who fail to warn clients face real consequences. The Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) system responded by creating Form 060, the Wire Fraud Advisory, now treated as a de facto requirement by most brokerages operating in the state.

## The Legal Framework: ORS 696 and OREA Administrative Rules

**Oregon's licensing statute, ORS 696.301, establishes that a licensee must exercise reasonable skill and care in all aspects of a transaction, which OREA has interpreted to include cybersecurity warnings.** The statute does not enumerate wire fraud by name, but OREA's administrative rules under OAR 863-015-0125 outline the duty of agents to disclose known risks and material facts that could harm their clients.

OREA's 2025 guidance bulletin explicitly stated that failure to provide a wire fraud warning constitutes a potential violation of the agent's duty of care. This guidance references both the agent's individual obligation and the principal broker's supervisory duty under ORS 696.022. The [Oregon Real Estate Agency](https://www.oregon.gov/rea) has published sample language and pointed licensees toward OREF Form 060 as an acceptable method of compliance.

The distinction here matters. Oregon does not have a standalone wire fraud prevention statute like some states have enacted. Instead, the obligation flows from existing duty-of-care provisions and OREA interpretive guidance, making it no less enforceable but slightly more ambiguous for agents who want a bright-line rule.

## OREF Form 060: What It Covers and When to Use It

OREF Form 060—the Wire Fraud Advisory—is a one-page document that warns all transaction parties about email compromise schemes, spoofed phone numbers, and altered wire instructions. It instructs clients to verbally confirm all wiring details directly with the title or escrow company using a known phone number, not one provided in an email.

**You should present OREF Form 060 at the earliest point where wire instructions might become relevant, typically at mutual acceptance or upon opening escrow.** Waiting until three days before closing is too late—by then, a compromised email thread may have already delivered fraudulent instructions to your client.

Some brokerages layer additional internal forms on top of OREF 060. If your brokerage uses a supplemental acknowledgment, you still need the OREF form in the file. The supplemental does not replace it. Your transaction coordinator or file reviewer should confirm both are signed and dated before the wire is scheduled.

For agents handling the [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process), timing and documentation are everything. A signed form sitting in your CRM with no delivery timestamp is nearly as dangerous as no form at all.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences fall into three categories: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and insurance coverage gaps.

On the regulatory side, OREA can issue license suspensions, civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation under ORS 696.990, and mandatory education requirements. Repeat offenders face revocation. OREA's disciplinary database shows multiple cases since 2023 where agents received formal reprimands for insufficient client advisories around closing procedures.

Civil liability is where the real financial exposure lives. If your client wires $400,000 to a fraudster and you never provided the advisory, a negligence claim becomes straightforward. Oregon courts apply a reasonable-agent standard: what would a competent licensee have done? In 2026, the answer is unambiguous—deliver the wire fraud warning, document it, and follow up verbally.

Your E&O insurance may also become unreliable. Many carriers now include policy language requiring agents to follow "standard industry practices for cybersecurity warnings." If you skipped OREF Form 060, your carrier has a coverage defense.

| Failure Type | Regulatory Risk | Civil Liability | E&O Coverage Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| No advisory delivered | Suspension, fine up to $5,000 | High exposure for negligence | Likely denial of claim |
| Advisory delivered late (after compromise) | Reprimand, mandatory education | Moderate exposure | Partial coverage possible |
| Advisory delivered but not documented | Warning letter | Low-moderate exposure | Coverage likely intact |
| Advisory delivered and documented on time | None | Minimal exposure | Full coverage available |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

The first and most frequent error is treating the advisory as a closing-week task. Agents batch their compliance paperwork and deliver OREF Form 060 alongside the closing disclosure review. By that point, clients have often already received and acted on wire instructions—sometimes fraudulent ones. The advisory must go out at or near mutual acceptance.

The second mistake is verbal-only warnings. Telling a buyer "watch out for wire fraud emails" at your first meeting is good practice, but it is not documentation. Without a signed acknowledgment, you have no evidence the warning was delivered. OREA investigators look at the file, not your memory.

**Third, agents frequently forget to advise the seller's side.** Wire fraud does not only target buyers. Sellers receive fraudulent proceeds disbursement instructions, fake "updated deposit account" emails, and spoofed lender communications. OREF Form 060 should be delivered to every party you represent, regardless of their role in the transaction.

Fourth, some agents rely on the title company to handle all wire fraud warnings and assume their own obligation is satisfied. It is not. The title company's advisory covers their own liability. Your obligation as a licensee under ORS 696.301 is independent. Both advisories should exist in the transaction file.

Fifth, agents working with [teams or assistants who manage paperwork](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) sometimes delegate the advisory without confirming delivery. Delegation is fine—but the licensee remains responsible. If your assistant forgot, OREA holds you accountable, not them.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Oregon carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.022. This is not theoretical. OREA has disciplined brokers specifically for failing to implement wire fraud prevention policies, even when the individual agent was also sanctioned.

**Brokers should audit three elements in every transaction file: presence of OREF Form 060 (signed and dated), timing of delivery relative to escrow opening, and evidence that the advisory was actually transmitted to the client (email delivery receipt, DocuSign timestamp, or similar).** A form sitting in the agent's desk drawer unsigned does nothing.

Quarterly file reviews should include a wire fraud compliance check as a standalone line item. Do not bury it inside a general "disclosures complete" checkbox. Treating it as its own category forces reviewers to confirm the specific form, the specific date, and the specific delivery method.

Brokerages should also maintain a written office policy on wire fraud prevention procedures, updated annually. OREA expects this policy to exist and may request it during an audit or complaint investigation. The policy should specify which forms are required, when they must be delivered, and what agents should tell clients about verbal verification of wire instructions.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency | Documentation Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| OREF Form 060 present | Signed, dated, both parties | Every transaction | PDF in transaction file |
| Delivery timing | Before or at escrow opening | Every transaction | Timestamp or delivery receipt |
| Office policy current | Annual review, dated | Annually | Signed by principal broker |
| Agent training completed | Wire fraud CE or internal | Annually per agent | Certificate or attendance log |
| Incident response plan | Contact list, reporting steps | Annually | Written and accessible |

## Verbal Verification: The Step That Actually Prevents Fraud

Forms protect you legally. Verbal verification protects your client financially. Every wire fraud advisory tells clients to call the title or escrow company directly—using a phone number they obtained independently, not from an email—to confirm wire instructions before sending funds.

Agents should reinforce this verbally at least twice during the transaction: once when the advisory is delivered and once within 48 hours of the scheduled wire date. Document both conversations in your transaction notes. A simple CRM entry with the date, time, and summary is sufficient.

The reason this matters operationally is that [missed deadlines and forgotten steps](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are the root cause of most wire fraud losses. The client received the form, filed it away, then got a convincing email three weeks later and forgot the warning. Your second verbal reminder—close to the wire date—is the intervention that actually stops the loss.
