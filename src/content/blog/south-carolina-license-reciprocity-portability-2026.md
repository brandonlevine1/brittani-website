## South Carolina Real Estate License Reciprocity & Portability 2026: The Rules That Actually Apply

Agents relocating to the Palmetto State or working deals near its borders run into a hard truth almost immediately: South Carolina real estate license reciprocity & portability 2026 rules are far more restrictive than many assume. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC), operating under Title 40, Chapter 57 of the South Carolina Code of Laws, does not grant blanket reciprocity to any other state. That distinction matters because it determines whether you can legally earn a commission on a transaction touching South Carolina soil—or whether you are exposing yourself, your brokerage, and your client to serious liability.

This post breaks down the specific statutory framework, the forms you need, the penalties for non-compliance, and the audit responsibilities brokers carry under current SCREC rules.

## How South Carolina Defines Reciprocity vs. Portability

The terms "reciprocity" and "portability" are often used interchangeably by agents, but they carry different legal weight. Reciprocity means two states have a formal agreement allowing licensees from one to practice in the other with minimal additional requirements. Portability refers to a state's willingness to let out-of-state agents participate in a limited number of transactions without holding a local license.

**South Carolina offers neither in the traditional sense.** There is no interstate compact, no cooperative license, and no temporary practice permit. What South Carolina does offer is a partial education waiver pathway under S.C. Code Section 40-57-340, which allows experienced agents from other states to skip some pre-licensing coursework—provided they meet strict criteria.

This puts South Carolina in a minority of states that still require full licensure before any brokerage activity occurs within its borders. Agents licensed in states with portability agreements elsewhere—like those familiar with [Wisconsin's approach](/blog/wisconsin-license-reciprocity-portability-2026)—often assume they can operate similarly here. They cannot.

## The Statutory Framework: S.C. Code Sections You Need to Know

The relevant statutes cluster around Title 40, Chapter 57 of the South Carolina Code of Laws. Section 40-57-20 defines what constitutes brokerage activity. Section 40-57-150 establishes penalties for unlicensed practice. Section 40-57-340 outlines the pathway for out-of-state licensees seeking a South Carolina credential.

> "No person shall engage in or carry on, directly or indirectly, or advertise or hold himself out as engaging in or carrying on the business, act in the capacity of, or deal in any activity constituting those activities performed by a real estate broker or salesperson within this state without first obtaining a license." — S.C. Code Section 40-57-150(A)

That language is deliberately broad. It covers advertising, negotiating, advising on value, and showing property—not just writing offers. An out-of-state agent who hosts an open house for a listing near the state line without a South Carolina license has already violated the statute.

SCREC enforces these provisions through its Investigative Division. Complaints can originate from consumers, other licensees, or the Commission's own audit processes.

## The Application Process for Out-of-State Agents

Out-of-state agents who want to practice in South Carolina must submit a Non-Resident License Application to SCREC. This application requires a Certification of Licensure (sometimes called a license history certification) issued by the applicant's home state commission. The certification must confirm active licensure status, disciplinary history, and years of experience.

| Requirement | Resident Applicant | Out-of-State Applicant |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-licensing education (90 hours) | Required in full | May be partially waived under 40-57-340 |
| South Carolina state exam | Required | Required |
| National exam portion | Required | Waived if passed previously |
| Certification of Licensure | N/A | Required from home state |
| Broker affiliation in SC | Required before activation | Required before activation |
| Background check (SLED/FBI) | Required | Required |

The partial waiver under Section 40-57-340 does not eliminate the South Carolina state exam. Every applicant, regardless of experience, must pass the state-specific portion administered through PSI Testing. The exam covers South Carolina contract law, the Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act, agency relationships under the Brokerage Relationships Act (S.C. Code Section 40-57-350), and SCREC regulations.

Once licensed, the out-of-state agent must affiliate with a South Carolina broker-in-charge. SCREC will not activate a license without an affiliated broker on file.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance: What Is Actually at Stake

The consequences for practicing without proper licensure extend well beyond a fine. Under S.C. Code Section 40-57-150, unlicensed practice constitutes a misdemeanor. Each transaction or act can be charged as a separate violation, with fines up to $5,000 per occurrence.

**Beyond criminal exposure, the contractual fallout can be devastating.** A purchase agreement facilitated by an unlicensed person may be declared voidable by either party. That means a buyer who discovers their agent lacked South Carolina credentials can walk away from the deal—and potentially pursue damages for any earnest money lost or due diligence expenses incurred.

Brokers face their own jeopardy. Under S.C. Code Section 40-57-135, a broker-in-charge who allows an unlicensed individual to perform brokerage activities can face license suspension, fines, and civil liability to affected consumers. The Commission has shown willingness to hold brokers accountable even when the unlicensed activity occurred without the broker's explicit knowledge, reasoning that supervision duties require proactive verification.

Commission disputes arising from these situations often [result in deals breaking down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in ways that generate lawsuits against multiple parties simultaneously.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With South Carolina Portability

Five errors appear repeatedly in SCREC disciplinary actions and advisory opinions related to out-of-state agents.

**First, assuming a neighboring state license provides automatic authority.** Agents licensed in North Carolina and Georgia make this mistake most frequently. The geographic proximity and volume of cross-border referrals create a false sense of permission. No neighboring state has a reciprocity agreement with South Carolina.

Second, confusing referral fees with brokerage activity. An out-of-state agent may legally receive a referral fee for connecting a client with a South Carolina licensee—but only if the out-of-state agent performs zero brokerage activities within South Carolina. Showing a property, attending an inspection, or negotiating repair requests crosses the line, even if another agent writes the offer.

Third, letting a license lapse during the transition period. Agents who move to South Carolina sometimes allow their home state license to expire before obtaining their South Carolina credential. This creates a gap during which they hold no valid license anywhere—and any activity during that gap constitutes unlicensed practice in both states.

Fourth, failing to disclose licensure status on South Carolina contract forms. The [SC Real Estate Commission](https://llr.sc.gov/res/) requires agents to identify their license status on all transaction documents. Using a home state license number on a South Carolina form does not satisfy this requirement.

Fifth, overlooking the continuing education mismatch. South Carolina requires 10 hours of CE biennially, including mandatory topics set by SCREC. Agents who maintain dual licenses sometimes complete CE in their home state and assume it satisfies South Carolina requirements. Unless the course is specifically approved by SCREC, it does not count.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge bear direct regulatory responsibility for every agent operating under their license. SCREC Regulation 105-10 requires brokers to maintain complete records of each affiliated licensee's credentials, including confirmation that the license is active and in good standing.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Source Document |
|---|---|---|
| License status verification | At affiliation and each renewal | SCREC online verification portal |
| CE completion confirmation | Every 2-year renewal cycle | SCREC transcript or approved provider certificate |
| Out-of-state agent exam passage | Before affiliation | PSI score report |
| Background check clearance | Before affiliation | SLED/FBI results on file with SCREC |
| Supervision documentation | Ongoing | Transaction files, broker review logs |

**Brokers who manage agents relocating from other states should implement a pre-affiliation checklist that goes beyond simply confirming SCREC has issued a number.** The checklist should verify the agent has completed the South Carolina state exam, that their Certification of Licensure is on file, and that any conditions imposed by SCREC (such as additional coursework) have been satisfied before the agent touches a transaction.

Failing to maintain these records does not just risk a fine. It can result in the broker's license being suspended or revoked under SCREC's authority in Section 40-57-110. The Commission treats supervision failures as a pattern issue—one gap in documentation often triggers a broader file audit.

Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on their licensure paperwork put their brokers in an impossible position. The broker cannot legally allow an expired or inactive licensee to continue working, even if a transaction is mid-stream.
