## Understanding Utah Teams & Supervision Rules 2026

The Utah Division of Real Estate has tightened its stance on team operations over the past two licensing cycles, and the current regulatory framework under **Utah teams & supervision rules 2026** leaves little room for ambiguity. Whether you run a two-person duo or a 15-agent mega team, the rules apply uniformly—and the Division enforces them with increasing frequency. The governing statutes sit primarily in Utah Code Title 61, Chapter 2f, supplemented by Utah Administrative Code R162-2f.

**Utah's team regulations differ from neighboring states in one critical respect: the principal broker retains direct, non-delegable responsibility for every transaction a team closes.** This means a team leader cannot serve as a supervisory shield between agents and the broker. The broker must maintain independent oversight regardless of internal team structures.

The Division defines a "team" as two or more licensees who work together under a shared identity within a single brokerage, market themselves collectively, and share compensation from transactions. Solo agents with unlicensed assistants do not constitute a team under this definition, but they face a separate set of assistant-supervision rules under R162-2f-401a(7).

## Team Registration and Structural Requirements

Every team operating in Utah must be registered through the principal broker with the Division of Real Estate before conducting any business under a team name. The broker submits this registration through the Division's online licensing portal, and the team name must meet specific advertising standards outlined in R162-2f-401a(4).

A team name cannot imply that it is a separate brokerage. It cannot include terms like "realty," "real estate company," or "brokerage" unless those words also appear in the licensed brokerage name. The Division rejected 47 team-name applications in 2025 alone for violating this single provision.

Each team member must hold an active Utah real estate license in good standing, affiliated with the same brokerage under which the team is registered. You cannot have a team member licensed under Brokerage A operating as part of a team registered under Brokerage B—even if both brokerages consent.

## Broker Supervision Duties Under Utah Code 61-2f-401

Utah Code 61-2f-401 places supervisory responsibility squarely on the principal broker's shoulders. The statute requires that brokers establish, maintain, and enforce written policies governing agent conduct, transaction documentation, trust account handling, and advertising compliance. For teams, this obligation intensifies because the broker must supervise both individual agents and the team's collective activities.

| Supervision Element | Broker Duty | Team Leader Role |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction review | Must personally review or establish systematic review protocol | May assist but cannot substitute for broker oversight |
| Advertising approval | Must pre-approve all team advertising | Can draft materials but broker has final authority |
| Trust fund oversight | Direct responsibility, non-delegable | No authority over trust accounts |
| Complaint response | Must respond to Division inquiries within 10 business days | Must cooperate but broker is primary respondent |
| Training and policy updates | Must provide annual policy updates to all affiliates | May facilitate team-specific training under broker guidance |

The broker cannot contract away this responsibility. Even if a team leader agreement assigns certain "supervisory" tasks to the team lead, the Division will hold the broker accountable for failures in oversight. This was affirmed in a 2024 Division disciplinary action where a Salt Lake County broker argued that the team leader was responsible for reviewing contracts—the Division disagreed and suspended the broker's license for 90 days.

**Brokers who rely on team leaders as de facto supervisors without maintaining independent verification systems are operating on borrowed time.** The Division's enforcement unit has made team oversight a stated priority for 2026 audits.

## Advertising Rules That Trip Up Teams

Advertising generates more Division complaints against teams than any other category. Under [R162-2f-401a(4)](https://rules.utah.gov/publicat/code/r162/r162-02f.htm), every team advertisement—digital, print, signage, or social media—must include the brokerage name in a clear and conspicuous manner. The brokerage name must appear in type no smaller than the team name.

This means your yard signs, Facebook ads, Instagram posts, and email signatures all need the brokerage name displayed prominently. A team name in 48-point font with the brokerage in 8-point font at the bottom violates the rule. The Division considers the "clear and conspicuous" standard from the consumer's perspective, not the agent's.

Teams also cannot use the word "associates" or "group" in a way that implies independent brokerage status. The Division's 2025 newsletter specifically warned that phrases like "The Smith Group — Your Full-Service Real Estate Firm" violate R162-2f-401a(4) because "firm" implies a standalone entity.

## Common Mistakes Utah Teams Make

**First, failing to update team rosters when members join or leave.** The Division requires that the broker notify the Division within 10 days of any change in team composition. Agents who leave a team but remain on the team's marketing materials create a dual violation: outdated registration and misleading advertising. Both carry separate penalties.

Second, team leaders directing unlicensed assistants to perform licensed activities. Utah law is explicit—unlicensed assistants cannot negotiate terms, advise clients on pricing, show properties independently, or hold themselves out as licensed agents. When a team leader asks an assistant to "handle the showing because I'm double-booked," that single act can trigger a Division investigation against both the team leader and the broker.

Third, commingling team operational funds with client trust funds. Some teams maintain internal "team accounts" for marketing and operational expenses. When these accounts inadvertently receive earnest money deposits or other client funds—even temporarily—the broker faces trust account violations under Utah Code 61-2f-401(4). The penalty for trust account violations starts at $5,000 and escalates to license revocation for repeat offenses.

Fourth, using the Real Estate Purchase Contract (Form 100 series through the Utah Association of Realtors) without proper identification of the team relationship. Every contract must identify the licensee, their brokerage, and their role. Listing only the team name without the individual agent's license number violates Division requirements and can create confusion about who holds fiduciary duties to the client.

Fifth, team leaders conducting "team training" that substitutes for required continuing education. The Division does not recognize internal team training as CE credit unless the course is pre-approved through the Division's CE approval process. Agents who rely on team meetings for their education hours find themselves in license-renewal trouble every two years. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers who oversee teams should run structured audits at least quarterly. The Division's enforcement staff has testified in administrative hearings that "reasonable supervision" requires documented, systematic review—not sporadic check-ins. Here is a framework for what to audit.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Red Flags |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | All active digital and print ads for brokerage name, size, and placement | Team name dominates; brokerage name absent or illegible |
| Roster accuracy | Current team members vs. Division registration records | Departed agents still listed; new agents not registered |
| Contract documentation | Randomly selected closed and pending files for proper agent identification | Missing license numbers, team name used instead of individual name |
| Unlicensed activity | Assistant task logs, showing records, communication records | Assistants scheduling showings solo, sending pricing opinions |
| Trust account segregation | Bank statements for team operational accounts vs. trust accounts | Any client funds touching a non-trust account |

**A broker who cannot produce audit records when the Division requests them faces a presumption of inadequate supervision.** The burden shifts to the broker to prove oversight existed even without documentation—a nearly impossible standard to meet.

Beyond audits, brokers need written team agreements that spell out the team leader's delegated responsibilities versus the broker's retained authority. These agreements should be updated annually and signed by every team member. The Division has signaled that it will request these documents during routine license renewals beginning in 2026. ## Penalties for Non-Compliance

The Division of Real Estate operates under a tiered enforcement structure. Minor advertising violations typically result in a Notice of Non-Compliance with a 30-day cure period. If uncured, the Division escalates to a formal Citation carrying fines between $500 and $5,000 per violation.

Supervision failures by brokers trigger more serious consequences. Under Utah Code 61-2f-404, the Division can suspend or revoke a broker's license, impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, order restitution to harmed consumers, and require additional education. In cases involving trust account mishandling, the Division refers matters to the Utah Attorney General for potential criminal prosecution.

Individual team members face personal liability as well. If an agent's conduct violates the statute, the agent cannot hide behind the team structure. The Division can discipline the individual agent, the team leader (if they had delegated supervisory duties and failed), and the broker—all from the same incident. This layered liability structure means a single compliance failure can generate three separate disciplinary actions from one transaction.
