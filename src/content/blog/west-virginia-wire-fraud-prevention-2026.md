## Understanding West Virginia Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

Wire fraud losses in real estate transactions exceeded $446 million nationally in 2024, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. West Virginia wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 now place explicit compliance obligations on licensees, meaning agents who treat these as optional paperwork risk both their license and personal liability. The West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC) updated its advisory requirements effective January 1, 2026, tightening expectations around when and how agents warn clients about fraudulent wire schemes.

The state's approach differs from neighboring Virginia and Ohio in one critical respect: West Virginia assigns affirmative disclosure duties to the licensee, not just the title company. This means you cannot delegate the warning to closing counsel and assume your obligation is met. The WVREC's position, codified through its administrative rulemaking under W. Va. Code §30-40-19, treats failure to deliver the mandated advisory as a violation of the licensee's duty to protect the public interest.

## The Specific Forms West Virginia Agents Must Use

**Form WFA-1 (Wire Fraud Advisory) is the primary document the WVREC requires agents to deliver to every buyer and seller in a transaction involving electronic fund transfers.** This one-page advisory warns parties that criminals impersonate real estate professionals via email, instructs them never to trust wiring instructions received electronically without phone verification, and provides a protocol for confirming legitimate instructions directly with the closing attorney or title company.

Form WFA-2 (Wire Instruction Verification Acknowledgment) serves as a secondary confirmation at or near closing. While the WVREC classifies WFA-2 as "recommended" rather than mandatory, brokerages operating under best practices treat it as required. The form documents that the client independently verified wire routing numbers by calling a confirmed phone number before initiating the transfer.

Both forms are available through the WVREC's [official forms portal](https://wvrec.wv.gov/). Agents should verify they are using the 2026 revision, as the Commission updated language in both forms to address emerging AI-generated phishing schemes that mimic voice calls.

| Form | Purpose | When Delivered | Mandatory? |
|------|---------|---------------|------------|
| WFA-1 | Wire Fraud Advisory | At or before contract execution | Yes |
| WFA-2 | Wire Instruction Verification Acknowledgment | Before wire transfer at closing | Recommended (treated as mandatory by most brokerages) |
| Transaction file notation | Agent's record of delivery | Within 24 hours of delivery | Yes, per WVREC record-keeping rules |

## Liability Consequences for Non-Compliance

Failing to deliver Form WFA-1 exposes you to three distinct categories of risk. First, the WVREC can initiate disciplinary proceedings under W. Va. Code §30-40-23, which authorizes fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension, or revocation. Second, if a client wires funds to a fraudulent account and can demonstrate that the agent never warned them, civil liability attaches under negligence theories. Third, most E&O insurance policies contain exclusions for losses arising from the agent's failure to follow mandated compliance procedures.

**The practical consequence is that a $400,000 transaction can generate personal liability far exceeding the commission earned if the buyer loses their funds to a spoofed email.** West Virginia courts have shown willingness to pierce the brokerage relationship in fraud cases where individual licensee negligence is demonstrated. The 2024 decision in Marshall County involving a licensee who verbally warned but failed to document delivery of written materials resulted in a $187,000 judgment against the agent personally.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. When fraud is discovered mid-transaction, closing attorneys in West Virginia typically halt proceedings, and the contract timeline resets or terminates. Agents who failed to provide the advisory face claims from both sides of the transaction for resulting delays or losses.

## Common Mistakes West Virginia Agents Make

The first and most frequent error is delivering Form WFA-1 too late. Agents often include it in the closing package rather than at contract execution. The WVREC's position is clear: the advisory must reach the client before any wiring instructions could plausibly be exchanged. By the time closing documents are assembled, the client may have already received and acted on fraudulent instructions.

The second mistake involves relying on email delivery without confirmation. Sending WFA-1 as a PDF attachment to an email is permitted, but the agent must obtain signed or electronic acknowledgment of receipt. Simply attaching it and moving on does not satisfy the documentation requirement under the WVREC's record-keeping standards in W. Va. CSR §174-1-3.

**Third, agents frequently confuse their obligation with the title company's obligation.** The closing attorney verifies wire routing details. The agent's job is to warn the client and document that warning. These are separate duties, and performing one does not excuse the other.

Fourth, some agents use outdated form versions. The 2026 revision of WFA-1 includes updated language addressing AI voice-cloning scams, which were not referenced in the 2024 version. Using the old form technically satisfies the delivery requirement but creates liability gaps if a client falls victim to a scheme the outdated form failed to address.

Fifth, agents neglect to warn both parties. The wire fraud advisory applies to sellers receiving proceeds just as much as buyers wiring purchase funds. Seller-side agents who skip the form because "sellers receive money, they don't send it" ignore the reality that fraudulent actors redirect incoming wires by impersonating the closing attorney to the title company.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under W. Va. Code §30-40-20 for the compliance failures of their affiliated licensees. This means a broker who does not have a system for tracking WFA-1 delivery faces the same disciplinary exposure as the agent who forgot to deliver it. Quarterly audits of transaction files should confirm that every closed deal contains a signed WFA-1 dated at or before contract execution.

| Audit Item | What Brokers Should Verify | Frequency |
|------------|---------------------------|-----------|
| WFA-1 in file | Signed copy with date at or before contract execution | Every transaction |
| WFA-2 in file | Signed copy dated before wire transfer | Every transaction involving wire |
| Form version | 2026 revision used after January 1, 2026 | Quarterly spot-check |
| Delivery method | Email acknowledgment or wet signature on file | Every transaction |
| Both parties covered | WFA-1 delivered to buyer AND seller | Every transaction |

Brokers should also implement written office policies specifying when and how agents deliver wire fraud advisories. The WVREC's examiner guidelines reference "written brokerage procedures" as a mitigating factor during disciplinary hearings. Having a policy that agents violate is better positioned than having no policy at all, but active enforcement through transaction coordinator review or automated compliance tracking closes the gap entirely.

Training documentation matters. Brokers who can demonstrate annual wire fraud training for their agents receive favorable consideration in disciplinary proceedings. The WVREC accepts continuing education courses that cover wire fraud prevention, but also recognizes in-house training sessions documented with sign-in sheets and curriculum outlines.

## How Wire Fraud Prevention Intersects With Other Disclosures

Wire fraud advisories do not exist in isolation. They sit alongside the [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) and [lead paint disclosures](/blog/west-virginia-lead-paint-disclosure-2026) that agents must track in every transaction. The challenge is timing: each form has a different delivery window, and mixing them up creates compliance gaps that compound liability exposure.

The WFA-1 delivery window is particularly unforgiving because it must precede any possible wire instruction exchange. In practice, this means handing it to clients at the same meeting where you present or execute the purchase agreement. Treating all disclosure forms as a single delivery event at contract execution ensures nothing slips through, though agents must document each form's acknowledgment separately.
