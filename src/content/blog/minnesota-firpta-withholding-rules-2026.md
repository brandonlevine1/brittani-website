## Minnesota FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Federal-State Double Layer

Every Minnesota agent who lists property for a non-U.S. person—or represents a buyer purchasing from one—faces a compliance obligation that can torpedo a deal or expose the buyer to five-figure IRS liability. The Minnesota firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 combine a 15% federal withholding requirement under Internal Revenue Code Section 1445 with a separate Minnesota state withholding obligation under Minnesota Statutes Section 290.923. Misunderstanding either layer, or assuming the title company handles everything, is how agents land in regulatory trouble.

FIRPTA (the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act) requires that when a foreign person disposes of a U.S. real property interest, the buyer must withhold and remit tax to the IRS. The federal rate stands at 15% of the gross sales price for transactions above $1 million, with reduced rates for certain residential-use transactions below that mark. Minnesota stacks its own withholding on top—generally 2% of the net gain—payable to the Minnesota Department of Revenue.

## Who Qualifies as a "Foreign Person" and How Agents Verify Status

**A foreign person under FIRPTA includes any nonresident alien individual, foreign corporation, foreign partnership, foreign trust, or foreign estate.** Residency for tax purposes is not the same as immigration status. A seller may hold a green card, live in Edina year-round, and still be classified as a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes if they fail the substantial presence test or claimed treaty benefits.

The primary verification mechanism is [IRS Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9), which a U.S. person provides to certify domestic status. If the seller cannot or will not furnish a valid W-9 with a U.S. taxpayer identification number, the buyer must presume foreign status and withhold accordingly. Agents should never accept verbal assurances—demand the W-9 or flag the transaction for FIRPTA compliance immediately.

Minnesota agents should also know that the Minnesota Department of Revenue may request a completed Form M1NR (Minnesota Individual Income Tax Return for Nonresidents) or evidence of a withholding exemption when a seller is both a foreign person and a Minnesota nonresident. The dual reporting creates two separate filing obligations that run on different timelines.

## Federal Withholding: Forms, Rates, and Deadlines That Bind Buyers

| Element | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| Standard withholding rate | 15% of gross sales price |
| Reduced rate (residential use, $300,001–$1,000,000) | 10% of gross sales price |
| Full exemption threshold | Sale price $300,000 or less, buyer will use as residence |
| Forms required | IRS Form 8288 + Form 8288-A |
| Filing deadline | Within 20 days of closing |
| Responsible party | Buyer (or buyer's agent acting as withholding agent) |

The buyer—not the seller, not the listing agent—is the withholding agent under Section 1445. If a closing agent (title company or attorney) agrees to act as a "qualified substitute," they assume the obligation. But if no one withholds and remits, the IRS comes after the buyer personally for the full 15% plus penalties and interest under IRC Section 1461.

A seller who believes their actual tax liability is less than the withholding amount can apply for a withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B before closing. Processing takes 90 days or more, which means the listing agent needs to identify foreign seller status early—ideally at the listing agreement stage, not the week before closing. This timing issue catches agents off guard more than any other single factor, as covered in [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Minnesota's Additional Layer: State Withholding Under Section 290.923

Minnesota Statutes Section 290.923 imposes a separate withholding obligation on transferees (buyers) when the seller is a nonresident of Minnesota—regardless of U.S. citizenship status. For foreign sellers, both FIRPTA and the Minnesota requirement apply simultaneously.

**The state withholding is calculated differently from the federal amount.** Minnesota typically requires withholding of 2% of the net gain, though alternative calculations exist when the gain cannot be readily determined at closing. The closing agent or buyer remits this amount to the Minnesota Department of Revenue using state withholding forms, with the remittance due by the 15th day of the month following the month of closing.

Sellers can apply for a reduced withholding or full exemption by filing a request with the Department of Revenue before closing. The state's processing timeline is generally faster than the IRS's 90-day window, but agents still need to plan for a potential 30-to-45 day delay.

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences split across three categories: IRS enforcement against the buyer, Minnesota Department of Revenue enforcement, and professional licensing risk for the agent.

If the buyer fails to withhold the federal 15%, the IRS assesses the full withholding amount against the buyer, plus interest from the date of closing and potential penalties under IRC Section 6651. The buyer can then attempt to recover from the seller—who may be overseas and unreachable. Agents who knew or should have known about the foreign seller's status and failed to advise their client face potential malpractice claims and E&O exposure.

At the state level, Minnesota can assess the unpaid withholding against the buyer, impose a 5% penalty per month (up to 25%) for late filing, and charge interest at the statutory rate. The Minnesota Department of Commerce, which oversees real estate licensing, considers failure to disclose known material facts about transaction requirements a violation of Minnesota Statutes Section 82.81. Repeated or egregious failures can trigger license suspension proceedings.

Deals can also collapse entirely. Title companies in the Twin Cities increasingly refuse to close transactions where FIRPTA compliance is unclear, and lenders may halt funding if withholding documentation is incomplete. These issues compound when agents discover foreign seller status late in the process, [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) most frequently.

## Common Mistakes Minnesota Agents Make with FIRPTA Transactions

**The first and most damaging error is assuming the title company will handle everything without agent involvement.** Title companies facilitate the mechanical process of withholding and remittance, but they rely on the parties to identify foreign status. If the listing agent never raises the question, title may never ask.

The second mistake is confusing immigration status with tax residency. A seller with a valid work visa, a Minnesota driver's license, and a Minnetonka mailing address can still be a nonresident alien for tax purposes. Only the W-9 or a tax attorney's determination resolves this question—not the seller's word.

Third, agents routinely fail to account for the dual federal-state withholding when negotiating net proceeds. A foreign seller expecting to walk away with a certain number may be shocked to learn that 15% federal plus 2% state withholding decimates their proceeds. This surprise kills deals at the closing table when it should have been addressed in the listing presentation.

Fourth, listing agents forget to begin the IRS Form 8288-B withholding certificate application early enough. The 90-day processing window means that if the seller wants reduced withholding, the application must be filed before the property even goes under contract in many cases. Waiting until after acceptance guarantees a delay or forced full withholding.

Fifth, agents conflate FIRPTA obligations with Minnesota's general nonresident withholding. They are separate requirements with separate forms, separate filing deadlines, and separate enforcement agencies. Satisfying one does not satisfy the other.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents who handle foreign seller transactions should implement three specific controls within their transaction management workflow.

First, require a foreign seller identification checkbox at the listing intake stage. The Minnesota Association of Realtors does not mandate a specific form for this purpose, but brokers can create an internal compliance checklist that triggers when the seller cannot produce a W-9. This single step prevents the downstream chaos of discovering foreign status at closing.

Second, brokers should audit that IRS Form 8288-B applications—when applicable—are filed within 10 days of listing agreement execution. The 90-day processing reality means no other timeline works. Brokers who track this in a centralized system, rather than relying on individual agent memory, dramatically reduce compliance failures. Third, brokers should verify that both federal and state withholding amounts appear on the closing disclosure as separate line items. If only one appears—or neither—the transaction is non-compliant and the broker's supervisory liability under Minnesota Statutes Section 82.81 is triggered. A [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that includes FIRPTA status as a checklist item provides the oversight layer most brokerages lack.

## Building FIRPTA Compliance Into Your 2026 Practice

The Minnesota firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 are not new law—they are existing obligations that agents routinely mishandle because the issue surfaces rarely enough to feel unfamiliar each time it appears. The fix is structural: build foreign seller identification into your listing intake process, understand the dual federal-state withholding math, and give your sellers accurate net proceed estimates from day one. Agents who treat FIRPTA compliance as a closing-week problem will continue losing deals, facing liability, and damaging client relationships that took years to build.
