## Understanding Delaware Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Delaware earnest money & escrow laws 2026 impose specific obligations on licensees that go far beyond simply collecting a check at contract signing. The Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC), operating under Title 24, Chapter 29 of the Delaware Code, governs how agents and brokers handle trust funds from the moment a buyer commits money to a deal. Mishandling these funds is one of the fastest ways to lose a license in this state.

The regulatory framework here differs from neighboring states in several meaningful ways. Delaware does not mandate a statutory minimum earnest money amount, leaving that figure to market norms and negotiation. What it does mandate—strictly—is the timing of deposit, the type of account used, and the fiduciary standard applied to every dollar held in trust.

## Statutory Foundation: Title 24, Chapter 29 and Commission Regulations

**The primary statute governing escrow in Delaware is 24 Del. C. § 2912, which outlines prohibited conduct including commingling, conversion, and failure to account for trust funds.** Violations under this section can result in license suspension, revocation, or civil penalties up to $5,000 per offense. The Delaware Real Estate Commission's administrative regulations—specifically Regulation 8.0 (Escrow and Trust Accounts)—fill in the procedural details that the statute leaves to rulemaking.

Regulation 8.0 requires that all earnest money deposits be placed in a federally insured account at a financial institution located in Delaware or within a reasonable proximity. The account must be clearly labeled as an escrow or trust account, and the broker's name and license number must appear on the account. Interest-bearing escrow accounts are permissible only when all parties to the transaction consent in writing, with the interest disposition spelled out in the Agreement of Sale.

The [Delaware Real Estate Commission's website](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/) publishes the full text of these regulations and provides guidance bulletins that update annually.

## Forms Agents Must Know

Delaware residential transactions rely on the standard forms published by the Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR). The primary contract form is the **DAR Standard Form Agreement of Sale for Residential Resale (Form 35)**. Within this form, Paragraph 3 addresses the earnest money deposit—specifying the amount, the form of payment, and the escrow agent's identity.

Additional forms relevant to escrow handling include:

| Form | Purpose | Key Escrow Provision |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| DAR Form 35 | Residential Agreement of Sale | Paragraph 3: deposit amount, timing, escrow holder |
| DAR Form 35A | Addendum to Agreement of Sale | Modifies deposit terms or escrow instructions |
| DAR Form 40 | Release of Earnest Money Deposit | Requires mutual signatures for disbursement |
| DAR Form 41 | Dispute of Earnest Money | Used when parties disagree on disposition |

**When a deal falls apart and both sides cannot agree on who receives the deposit, Form 41 becomes the broker's best friend.** It formally documents the dispute and protects the broker from claims of unauthorized disbursement while the matter is resolved—either through mediation, the courts, or a Commission-sanctioned interpleader action.

Agents who skip Form 40 and disburse funds based on verbal agreements expose themselves and their brokers to disciplinary action. The Commission has consistently held that verbal authorization is insufficient for releasing escrowed funds.

## Deposit Timing and Account Requirements

Delaware does not impose a blanket statutory timeline for depositing earnest money. Instead, Regulation 8.0 defers to the contract terms. Most DAR Form 35 contracts specify that the deposit must be delivered within three business days of mutual execution. If the contract is silent on timing, the Commission interprets "prompt deposit" as no more than five business days—a standard that has been reinforced in multiple disciplinary opinions.

The escrow account itself must meet several criteria. It must be a demand deposit account (checking), unless an interest-bearing account is specifically authorized. The broker of record—not the individual agent—must be the signatory on the account. Agents who open their own escrow accounts without broker authorization are in direct violation of Regulation 8.0 and 24 Del. C. § 2912(a)(4).

| Requirement | Delaware Standard |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Deposit deadline | Per contract terms; default is 3 business days |
| Account type | Demand deposit (checking) unless parties agree to interest-bearing |
| Account location | Federally insured institution in or near Delaware |
| Account label | Must include "escrow" or "trust" and broker's name |
| Signatory | Broker of record (or designated broker) |
| Commingling | Prohibited; broker may maintain up to $500 of personal funds to cover bank fees |

That $500 allowance for personal funds is not a grace amount for sloppy bookkeeping. It exists solely to prevent account closures due to minimum balance requirements or service charges. Any amount beyond $500 that is not attributable to a specific transaction triggers a commingling violation.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make

Five recurring errors show up repeatedly in DREC disciplinary files, and every one of them is avoidable with basic diligence.

**First, agents accept earnest money checks made payable to themselves rather than to the brokerage or escrow agent named in the contract.** This creates a chain-of-custody problem and raises commingling concerns the moment the check touches the agent's personal account—even temporarily. The fix is simple: instruct buyers to make checks payable to the entity listed in Paragraph 3 of Form 35.

Second, agents fail to document extensions or modifications to deposit deadlines. When a buyer requests extra time to deliver the earnest money, agents sometimes grant informal extensions via text message without executing a written addendum (Form 35A). If the deal later collapses, the buyer may argue the contract was never properly formed, and the seller may claim breach—leaving the agent exposed to claims from both sides.

Third, agents release earnest money to one party without obtaining the written consent of the other party. Delaware requires mutual authorization for disbursement, period. Even when a buyer clearly defaults and the seller appears entitled to the deposit, the agent cannot unilaterally release funds. The proper path is Form 40 signed by both parties, or an interpleader action if agreement cannot be reached.

Fourth, agents fail to notify their broker that a deposit has not been received by the contract deadline. Under [Regulation 8.0](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/), the broker is ultimately responsible for escrow compliance. When agents conceal missed deposits—hoping the buyer will eventually deliver—they compound the violation and expose the broker to liability for funds that were never actually held in trust. If you find that [missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are a pattern in your practice, the root problem is usually a systems failure, not mere forgetfulness.

Fifth, agents use post-dated checks as earnest money without disclosing this to the seller. A post-dated check is not a current deposit; it is a promise to pay. If the seller's decision to accept the offer was influenced by the belief that funds were already secured, the agent may have committed a material misrepresentation.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Delaware bear personal liability for escrow violations committed under their supervision. DREC Regulation 8.0 places the escrow account squarely in the broker's domain, and the Commission has disciplined brokers who claimed ignorance of agent misconduct. Ignorance is not a defense when the regulation assigns you the duty to supervise.

**Monthly reconciliation of escrow accounts is not optional—it is the minimum standard the Commission expects.** Each reconciliation should compare the bank statement balance against the ledger of individual transaction deposits. Any discrepancy must be investigated and resolved within 48 hours. Brokers who rely on quarterly reconciliations or annual CPA reviews are operating below the Commission's expectations and exposing themselves to disciplinary risk.

Brokers should audit for these specific items: deposit timing compliance (was the check deposited within the contract-specified window?), proper payee designation on all checks received, executed Form 40 or Form 41 documentation for every disbursement or dispute, and confirmation that no agent has opened a separate trust account without authorization. A [structured checklist approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to these audits ensures nothing slips through during high-volume months.

Additionally, brokers must maintain escrow records for at least five years after the conclusion of a transaction, per DREC record-retention requirements. These records include bank statements, deposit receipts, ledger cards, and all correspondence related to the escrow. During a Commission audit—which can occur without advance notice—failure to produce these records carries the same weight as a substantive violation.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for escrow violations in Delaware operate on three levels: regulatory, civil, and criminal. At the regulatory level, 24 Del. C. § 2917 grants the Commission authority to suspend or revoke licenses, impose monetary penalties, require additional education, and issue public reprimands. Fines can reach $5,000 per violation, and each day of noncompliance may constitute a separate offense.

Civil liability exposes agents and brokers to lawsuits from buyers, sellers, or both. A buyer whose earnest money was wrongfully disbursed can sue for conversion. A seller who lost a transaction because the agent failed to secure the deposit on time can pursue damages for breach of fiduciary duty. Delaware courts have consistently held that real estate agents owe fiduciary duties to their clients regarding trust funds, and the standard of care is measured against the Commission's published regulations.

Criminal exposure is rare but real. Conversion of escrow funds—spending trust money on personal expenses—constitutes theft under Delaware criminal law. Even temporary "borrowing" from the escrow account has resulted in criminal charges in reported cases. The line between a civil commingling violation and criminal conversion is often just a matter of intent, and prosecutors have shown willingness to pursue agents whose conduct suggests deliberate misappropriation.

## Dispute Resolution and Interpleader Actions

When buyer and seller cannot agree on the disposition of earnest money after a failed transaction, the broker faces a genuine dilemma. Holding the funds indefinitely creates administrative burden and potential liability. Releasing them to either party without mutual consent violates Regulation 8.0.

Delaware law permits the broker to file an interpleader action in the Court of Chancery or Superior Court, depositing the disputed funds with the court and requesting that a judge determine the rightful recipient. The broker may deduct reasonable costs associated with the interpleader from the deposited funds, provided the Agreement of Sale or a subsequent written agreement authorizes such deduction. Filing an interpleader insulates the broker from claims by either party and should be considered whenever Form 41 discussions stall beyond 30 days.

Brokers who are unfamiliar with interpleader mechanics should consult with their brokerage counsel early in any dispute. Delays in filing can result in one party claiming the broker's inaction caused damages—an argument that gains traction the longer funds sit in limbo without resolution.
