The Nebraska legislature did not ease up this session. Between LB 847, amendments to the Nebraska Real Estate License Act, and updated Nebraska Real Estate Commission (NREC) rules taking effect January 1, agents across the state face a set of obligations that did not exist twelve months ago. Understanding Nebraska new real estate laws 2026 is not optional — it is the difference between closing deals cleanly and facing license actions that end careers.

This article breaks down the specific statutory changes, the forms affected, the penalties for non-compliance, and the operational shifts brokers must enforce across their offices.

## Nebraska New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Major Statutory Changes

**LB 847, signed by the Governor in April 2026, rewrites buyer representation requirements and expands seller disclosure obligations.** The bill amends Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2401 through 76-2430 and introduces new sections governing written agreements, compensation transparency, and consumer protection disclosures.

The three pillars of LB 847 are: mandatory written buyer representation agreements before touring, expanded material fact disclosure by sellers, and a new tiered penalty structure for violations. Each pillar touches different NREC forms and changes the timeline agents must follow during a transaction.

Separately, LB 912 addresses wire fraud prevention by requiring closing agents and licensees to follow specific verification protocols before transmitting wiring instructions to any party. This statute creates civil liability for agents who forward unverified wire details.

## Buyer Representation Agreements: What Changed and What Form to Use

Before 2026, Nebraska agents could show property to unrepresented buyers without a signed agreement in hand. That era is over. LB 847 requires a written buyer representation agreement executed prior to any in-person or virtual property tour.

The NREC has updated Form 20-Buyer Agency Agreement to reflect the new statutory language. Agents must use the revised version dated January 2026 — older versions will not satisfy the statutory requirement, even if the substantive terms are similar.

The agreement must specify three elements the old form left optional: the duration of representation (capped at 6 months unless renewed in writing), the exact compensation structure including source of payment, and a plain-language explanation of the buyer's right to terminate. Omitting any of these elements renders the agreement non-compliant under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2412(3).

| Requirement | Pre-2026 Rule | 2026 Rule (LB 847) |
|---|---|---|
| Written buyer agreement | Recommended, not required | Required before touring |
| Maximum duration | No statutory cap | 6-month cap without renewal |
| Compensation disclosure | General disclosure | Specific dollar or percentage amount |
| Termination clause | Optional | Mandatory plain-language provision |
| Form version | Form 20 (2019 revision) | Form 20 (January 2026 revision) |

## Seller Disclosure Expansions Under the Amended Act

**Nebraska's Seller Property Condition Disclosure Statement — Form 57.601 — now includes five new categories of mandatory disclosure.** LB 847 amended Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2,120 to require sellers to disclose known information about flooding history (even outside FEMA zones), past insurance claims, radon test results, HOA litigation, and broadband infrastructure limitations.

The previous version of Form 57.601 did not address these categories. Agents must confirm their sellers are using the 2026 revision and that every section is completed — not left blank. A blank field is no longer treated as "unknown" under the new statute; it is treated as a refusal to disclose, which triggers the buyer's right to extend the inspection period by 10 business days.

Listing agents have an affirmative duty to inform sellers of the expanded disclosure obligations. Simply handing over the form is insufficient. Under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2,120(4)(b), the listing agent must document that they explained each new category to the seller, and the NREC recommends using a separate acknowledgment addendum.

## Penalties and Liability: What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The previous penalty structure gave the NREC limited enforcement teeth. LB 847 changes that significantly. Civil penalties now reach $10,000 per violation, up from the prior $2,500 cap. License suspension is mandatory for three or more violations within a 24-month period.

Beyond regulatory penalties, the private right of action expanded. Buyers who do not receive a compliant disclosure form can rescind the purchase agreement up to 30 days after closing — not just during the inspection period. This is a dramatic shift that exposes sellers and their agents to post-closing litigation that previously had no statutory basis.

**Wire fraud failures carry separate liability.** Under LB 912, an agent who forwards wiring instructions without completing the required telephone verification with the closing agent faces personal liability for losses, regardless of whether the agent acted in good faith. The statute removes the "innocent intermediary" defense that agents previously relied upon.

| Violation Type | Penalty Range | License Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Missing buyer agreement | Up to $5,000 per instance | Written warning (first); suspension (repeat) |
| Incomplete seller disclosure | Up to $10,000 per instance | Mandatory education + probation |
| Unauthorized form version | Up to $2,500 per instance | Written warning |
| Wire instruction forwarding without verification | Personal liability for losses | Suspension or revocation |
| Three violations in 24 months | Cumulative | Mandatory suspension |

Agents should also recognize that E&O insurance policies may not cover penalties arising from willful non-compliance. Several Nebraska insurers have already issued bulletins clarifying that regulatory fines fall outside standard coverage.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

Even though these laws do not take full effect until January, the NREC opened its compliance hotline in June and has tracked recurring errors in early-adoption transactions. Here are the specific mistakes surfacing most frequently.

**First, agents are using the old Form 20 with handwritten addenda instead of the 2026 revision.** The NREC has stated that handwritten modifications to the 2019 form do not satisfy LB 847. The statutory language references "Commission-approved forms," and only the January 2026 version carries that approval.

Second, listing agents are treating the new seller disclosure categories as optional. Some agents have told sellers to write "N/A" in the flooding and radon sections without verifying whether the seller actually has knowledge. Under the amended statute, "N/A" is acceptable only when the seller genuinely lacks information — not as a shortcut. An agent who coaches a seller to mark N/A without inquiry risks aiding a material misrepresentation.

Third, buyer agents are obtaining signed agreements after the first showing and backdating them. The NREC addressed this directly in its July 2026 guidance bulletin: backdated agreements are void and constitute a violation independent of the underlying representation. The Commission tracks showing confirmations through MLS access logs, making this an easily detectable infraction.

Fourth, agents are forwarding title company wire instructions via email without completing the phone verification step required by LB 912. The statute requires a live telephone call — not a text message, not a voicemail confirmation. The call must be logged with the date, time, and name of the person contacted at the title company. Many agents are learning about this requirement the hard way, especially those managing [transactions where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) due to last-minute funding failures.

Fifth, some agents are failing to provide a copy of the signed buyer agreement to the buyer within 24 hours of execution. The prior rule allowed "reasonable time." The 2026 revision of Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2412(5) imposes a hard 24-hour deadline, and the NREC counts this from the moment of signature, not the next business day.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory liability for every transaction their agents handle. Under the amended Nebraska Real Estate License Act, a broker who fails to implement systems for verifying form compliance faces the same penalties as the offending agent — plus an additional $5,000 supervisory failure fine.

**Brokers should immediately audit three things: form version control, agreement timing, and wire verification logs.** If your office still has a stack of 2019 Form 20s in the printer tray, that is an audit failure waiting to become a Commission complaint. Every office should have a documented process for ensuring only current NREC-approved forms are accessible to agents.

Transaction files must include proof that buyer agreements were signed before the first showing. The simplest method is requiring agents to upload the signed agreement before the MLS showing confirmation can be scheduled. Brokers who rely on agents self-reporting compliance are accepting unnecessary risk, which is [why checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in every compliance study conducted on real estate operations.

Wire verification logs should be standardized across the brokerage. The NREC's model log — published in its July 2026 bulletin — includes fields for the transaction address, title company name, verifying agent, phone number called, person spoken with, date, and time. Brokers should require this log to be completed and filed before any wire instructions reach a client's inbox.

The NREC also recommends quarterly internal audits. Brokers who can demonstrate a documented audit trail receive favorable treatment during Commission investigations — the "good faith compliance" defense under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2428(2) applies only when the broker can show systematic oversight, not ad hoc corrections after a complaint surfaces.

## Deadlines and Timelines That Shifted

Several transaction timelines changed under LB 847, and agents accustomed to Nebraska's traditionally relaxed deadlines need to recalibrate. The buyer's rescission window for non-receipt of seller disclosures now extends to 30 days post-closing, as noted above. But the inspection contingency clock also changed.

When a seller delivers an incomplete Form 57.601 (any blank field in the new categories), the buyer's inspection period automatically extends by 10 business days from the date of discovery. This extension is not optional and does not require written notice from the buyer — it triggers by operation of law. Agents who schedule closings tightly around the original inspection deadline without confirming disclosure completeness risk blown timelines and [missed deadlines that kill transactions](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

The buyer agreement termination notice period is now 72 hours written notice, delivered by any method specified in the agreement. Previously, termination was governed solely by contract terms. The statutory floor of 72 hours means no agreement can require less, though parties may agree to longer notice periods.

## How the NREC Plans to Enforce These Changes

The [Nebraska Real Estate Commission](https://nrec.nebraska.gov/) has hired four additional compliance investigators specifically to handle LB 847 enforcement. The Commission announced in its June 2026 meeting minutes that it will prioritize complaints involving consumer harm — particularly wire fraud losses and undisclosed property conditions — but will not ignore procedural violations.

Random file audits will increase from 2% of licensees annually to 5%. The Commission stated that it will pull transaction files from the prior 12 months during any audit, meaning transactions closed in 2025 under old rules will not be reviewed under 2026 standards, but anything closing on or after January 1, 2026 must be fully compliant.

Agents should also know that the NREC shares complaint data with E&O insurers. A formal complaint — even one that results in no finding of violation — may affect future premium calculations.
