## Understanding Maine FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every transaction involving a non-U.S. person selling real property in Maine triggers overlapping federal and state withholding obligations. The Maine firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 require agents and closing attorneys to coordinate IRS requirements under Internal Revenue Code Section 1445 with Maine's own income tax withholding statute at 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A. Getting either layer wrong exposes the buyer—and potentially the agents—to direct financial liability.

**Federal FIRPTA mandates that the buyer withhold 15% of the gross sale price when the transferor is a "foreign person" as defined under IRC § 7701(b).** This is not discretionary. The funds must be remitted to the IRS within 20 days of closing using Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons).

Maine layers its own requirement on top. Under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A, the buyer or the buyer's agent must withhold 2.5% of the consideration paid to any nonresident seller—whether foreign or simply out-of-state—unless an exemption applies. The Maine withholding is reported on Maine Revenue Services Form REW-1 (Real Estate Withholding Return for Transfer of Real Property) and the corresponding REW-1-1096 transmittal form.

## Federal Withholding Rates and Exemptions That Apply in Maine

The 15% FIRPTA rate applies to most sales where the amount realized exceeds $300,000. A reduced rate of 10% applies when the amount realized is between $300,001 and $1,000,000 and the buyer intends to use the property as a principal residence. Sales at or below $300,000 where the buyer will use the home as a residence carry a 0% withholding obligation.

| Sale Price | Buyer Use | Federal Withholding Rate | Maine State Withholding |
|---|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Buyer's residence | 0% | 2.5% still applies |
| $300,001–$1,000,000 | Buyer's residence | 10% | 2.5% still applies |
| Over $1,000,000 | Any use | 15% | 2.5% still applies |
| Any amount | Investment/rental | 15% | 2.5% still applies |

Notice the critical asymmetry: even when the federal withholding drops to zero, the Maine 2.5% withholding under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A does not disappear. Agents who assume a low sale price eliminates all withholding obligations are setting up their transaction for a compliance failure.

Sellers seeking relief can file IRS Form 8288-B before or at closing to request a withholding certificate for a reduced amount. This is common when the seller's actual gain is far less than 15% of the gross price. However, the IRS typically takes 90 days to process these applications, which means the withheld funds usually sit in escrow until the certificate is issued.

## Maine-Specific Filing Obligations Under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A

Maine Revenue Services (MRS) requires that Form REW-1 be filed and the 2.5% payment remitted within 30 days of closing. The form must include the seller's identifying information, the property address, the sale date, and the amount withheld. A copy of Form REW-1-1096 accompanies the payment as a transmittal document.

**There are limited exemptions from Maine withholding, and agents must verify them rather than assume they apply.** The seller can avoid the 2.5% withholding by providing a Maine residency affidavit (sometimes called a "Seller's Residency Certification") or by demonstrating that the transfer qualifies under one of the statutory exceptions—such as a like-kind exchange under IRC § 1031 where the replacement property is also in Maine, or a transfer where the consideration is $100,000 or less.

The closing attorney in Maine typically handles the mechanical filing, but the legal liability rests with the transferee (buyer). If no withholding occurs and no valid exemption certificate is on file, MRS can assess the 2.5% against the buyer plus penalties of 25% of the unpaid amount and interest accruing from the date of transfer. ## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

Agent liability under FIRPTA is indirect but real. The IRS imposes the withholding obligation on the transferee (buyer), not the listing agent. However, under Maine Real Estate Commission Rule Chapter 10, agents have an affirmative duty to disclose material facts that affect the transaction. If a listing agent knows the seller is a foreign person and fails to communicate that to the closing team, the Maine Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action including license suspension.

On the buyer's side, the financial exposure is severe. If the 15% federal withholding is not remitted, the IRS can assess the full amount against the buyer personally—plus penalties and interest under IRC § 6651. That means on a $500,000 sale, a buyer could owe $75,000 to the IRS because the closing team failed to withhold.

Maine's penalties compound the problem. Under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A(5), the transferee who fails to withhold the 2.5% is personally liable for the tax, plus a penalty equal to 25% of the amount that should have been withheld. Deals rarely get cancelled solely over FIRPTA issues, but they absolutely stall—sometimes fatally—when a foreign seller's withholding certificate application is pending and the buyer refuses to close with $75,000 or more sitting in escrow for months.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Foreign Seller Transactions

**First, agents confuse "nonresident" with "foreign person."** Under Maine law, any seller who is not a Maine resident triggers the 2.5% state withholding—this includes a U.S. citizen living in New Hampshire. FIRPTA's 15% only applies to foreign persons (non-U.S. citizens and non-resident aliens). Conflating the two leads to either over-withholding or missing the state obligation entirely.

Second, listing agents fail to ask the citizenship question early enough. By the time a closing attorney discovers the seller is a foreign national, the timeline for obtaining a withholding certificate under Form 8288-B has already collapsed. Best practice is to determine foreign person status during the listing agreement phase—not at the title commitment stage.

Third, agents assume that because an entity (LLC or trust) holds title, FIRPTA does not apply. In reality, if any foreign person owns a controlling interest in the entity, the entity itself may be treated as a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. The IRS looks through domestic entities to their beneficial owners in certain circumstances under Treasury Regulation § 1.1445-2.

Fourth, buyer's agents neglect to inform their clients about the withholding obligation. The buyer is the one liable, and yet many buyer's agents treat FIRPTA as "the listing agent's problem." A buyer who first learns about a $75,000 withholding at the closing table is a buyer who may walk away.

Fifth, agents rely on verbal assurances rather than documented certifications. The Non-Foreign Affidavit (sometimes called a FIRPTA Affidavit or Transferor's Certification of Non-Foreign Status) under IRC § 1445(b)(2) must be in writing, signed under penalties of perjury, and include the seller's taxpayer identification number. A phone call from the seller saying "I'm a U.S. citizen" protects no one.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents in Maine should build FIRPTA compliance into their transaction review process at three checkpoints: listing intake, contract execution, and pre-closing file review.

| Checkpoint | Broker Audit Action | Document Required |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake | Confirm seller citizenship/residency status | Signed questionnaire or residency affidavit |
| Contract execution | Verify FIRPTA language in purchase agreement | Contract addendum referencing withholding |
| Pre-closing review | Confirm withholding amount calculated; Form 8288-B filed if applicable | Copy of 8288-B receipt or closing attorney confirmation |
| Post-closing | Verify REW-1 filed with MRS within 30 days | Copy of REW-1 and payment confirmation |

**Brokers should not rely solely on the closing attorney to catch foreign seller issues.** The [Maine Real Estate Commission](https://www.maine.gov/pfr/professionallicensing/professions/real-estate) expects supervising brokers to maintain policies that ensure compliance with disclosure obligations. A pattern of missed FIRPTA situations across a brokerage's transactions could trigger a commission audit and potential sanctions against the broker's license under 32 M.R.S. § 13187.

Transaction files should contain either a signed Non-Foreign Affidavit or documentation that the appropriate withholding was applied. ## Coordination Between Federal and State Withholding at Closing

The closing attorney in Maine bears the mechanical responsibility for calculating, collecting, and remitting both the federal and state withholding amounts. But agents must understand the math to advise their clients accurately on net proceeds.

Consider a foreign seller disposing of a waterfront property in Scarborough for $800,000. The federal FIRPTA withholding at 15% equals $120,000. The Maine withholding at 2.5% equals $20,000. Combined, $140,000 is withheld from the seller's proceeds at closing—before any mortgage payoff, commission, or other closing costs. If the seller's actual capital gain is only $50,000, they will eventually receive a refund after filing IRS Form 1040-NR and Maine Form 1040ME-NR, but that refund could take six to twelve months.

This timeline matters for listing agents because foreign sellers often balk at the withholding once they understand the cash flow impact. The solution is early disclosure and, where appropriate, filing Form 8288-B well before closing to obtain a reduced withholding certificate.
