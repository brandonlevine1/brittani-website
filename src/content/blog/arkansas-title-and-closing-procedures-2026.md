## Understanding Arkansas Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Arkansas handles real estate closings differently than many neighboring states, and agents who assume uniformity across state lines put their transactions at risk. The rules governing **Arkansas title & closing procedures 2026** flow primarily from the Arkansas Insurance Code (Title 23), the Real Estate License Law (Arkansas Code 17-42), and regulations set by the Arkansas Insurance Department and the Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC). Failing to understand where your responsibilities end and where the title company's begin is one of the fastest ways to tank a deal or invite a commission complaint.

The state does not mandate attorney-present closings, but it does impose strict requirements on who can issue title insurance, who can certify a title opinion, and what disclosures must accompany the settlement statement. Agents who treat closing day as someone else's problem often discover too late that their contractual obligations extend well beyond writing offers.

## The Role of Title Companies Under Arkansas Law

**Arkansas Code 23-103-405 requires that all title insurance commitments be based on a title opinion prepared by or under the direction of a licensed Arkansas attorney.** This means the title company you recommend to clients is not simply running a database search. The attorney's title opinion examines the abstract of title, identifies defects, and determines insurability.

Title companies in Arkansas operate under the regulatory authority of the [Arkansas Insurance Department](https://insurance.arkansas.gov/), not AREC. They must hold a certificate of authority under Arkansas Code 23-103-103 and comply with rate filings approved by the commissioner. Agents sometimes blur the lines between their advisory role and the title company's fiduciary obligations, which creates liability exposure on both sides.

One practical implication: agents cannot direct title companies to ignore or minimize title defects to keep a deal alive. If you pressure a title examiner to clear an exception they've flagged, you may be creating tortious interference claims against yourself and your brokerage.

## Key Forms and Contract Provisions Agents Must Know

Arkansas real estate transactions rely on AREC-approved forms. The primary purchase contract is **AREC Form 2 (Residential Real Estate Contract)**, which contains specific provisions governing title evidence, closing timelines, and the allocation of closing costs. Section 7 of Form 2 addresses title insurance requirements and gives buyers a defined period to review the title commitment.

| Form/Document | Purpose | Who Prepares It |
|---|---|---|
| AREC Form 2 | Residential purchase contract | Listing or buyer's agent |
| AREC Form 4 | Property disclosure | Seller |
| Title Commitment | Identifies title exceptions | Title company/attorney |
| Settlement Statement (CD) | Final cost accounting | Title company/lender |
| Warranty Deed | Transfers title | Title company/attorney |

**Agents must ensure that the title commitment review period specified in Section 7 of Form 2 aligns with realistic search timelines.** Writing a five-day title review period in a rural county where the courthouse still uses paper indices is a recipe for blown deadlines. The standard practice is 15 to 30 days from contract execution, depending on the county.

AREC Form 2 also addresses who pays the owner's title policy versus the lender's policy. While custom in most Arkansas counties places the owner's policy cost on the seller, this is not statutory—it is contractual. Agents who fail to specify this clearly in the contract invite last-minute disputes at the closing table.

## The Closing Process Step by Step

Arkansas closings follow a predictable sequence, though the timeline varies based on whether the transaction involves conventional financing, FHA/VA, or cash. Here is the typical flow once a contract is executed:

The title company opens an order, engages an attorney to prepare the title opinion, and issues a commitment within 10 to 14 business days. The commitment lists Schedule B exceptions—items the policy will not cover unless resolved. The buyer's agent must review these exceptions with the client and, if necessary, demand curative action from the seller within the contractual timeframe.

Once title objections are resolved or accepted, the file moves to pre-closing. The title company prepares the deed, coordinates with the lender on the Closing Disclosure (which must be delivered three business days before closing under TRID rules), and schedules the settlement appointment. On closing day, all parties sign documents, funds are disbursed, and the deed is recorded with the county circuit clerk under [Arkansas Code 14-15-404](https://insurance.arkansas.gov/).

> "No instrument affecting title to real property shall be valid against a subsequent purchaser for a valuable consideration without notice thereof, unless filed for record." — Arkansas Code 18-40-102

This statute underscores why same-day recording is not optional—it is essential to protecting your client's interest against intervening liens or claims.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Arkansas Closings

**The gap between what agents assume and what the law requires produces recurring errors that delay or destroy transactions.** Here are the most frequent offenders seen by title companies and the commission alike.

First, agents routinely fail to verify that the title commitment has actually been delivered to the buyer within the contract timeline. AREC Form 2 creates a contractual obligation, and if the buyer never receives the commitment, the review period may never start running—or the buyer gains an argument to terminate. Tracking this deadline is your responsibility, not the title company's.

Second, agents neglect to confirm the legal description matches across all documents. A mismatch between the MLS listing, the contract, and the deed can cloud title or cause the lender to refuse funding. This happens most often with rural properties described by metes and bounds rather than lot-and-block.

Third, agents fail to communicate [wire instructions verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) procedures to clients, leaving them vulnerable to wire fraud. Arkansas has seen a surge in business email compromise schemes targeting closings, and agents who forward wire instructions via unencrypted email may face negligence claims.

Fourth, agents agree to side deals or undisclosed credits outside the settlement statement. Arkansas Code 23-103-408 prohibits rebates or inducements not reflected on the closing documents. Violations can result in the title company losing its license and the agent facing AREC discipline.

Fifth, agents misunderstand the role of earnest money at closing. Under AREC regulations, earnest money held by a brokerage must be disbursed to the title company prior to closing per the contract terms. Delays in transferring these funds—often caused by agents who [miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines)—can push closing past rate lock expirations and cost buyers thousands.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Arkansas Code 17-42-311 for the acts of their affiliated agents. This is not a theoretical risk. AREC routinely disciplines brokers who fail to implement systems ensuring compliance with title and closing procedures.

| Broker Responsibility | Regulatory Basis | Risk of Non-Compliance |
|---|---|---|
| Verify contract forms are current AREC versions | AREC Rule 10.7 | Fine up to $2,500 per violation |
| Confirm earnest money is deposited within 3 business days | AREC Rule 10.14 | License suspension, trust account violation |
| Maintain closing file documentation for 5 years | Arkansas Code 17-42-313 | Commission audit failure, fine |
| Ensure agents do not practice law (drafting deeds, title opinions) | Arkansas Code 16-22-211 | Unauthorized practice of law referral to AG |
| Audit that wire instructions are verified before forwarding | AREC advisory guidance | Negligence liability, E&O claims |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits specifically targeting the gap between contract execution and title commitment delivery.** This is where most compliance failures hide. If an agent is consistently writing unrealistic title review periods or failing to document that commitments were delivered, the brokerage is exposed.

Training programs should explicitly address the boundaries between real estate practice and the unauthorized practice of law. Arkansas Code 16-22-211 defines the practice of law broadly, and agents who draft legal instruments, provide title opinions, or advise on the legal effect of title exceptions are crossing a statutory line that can result in criminal referral—not just a commission complaint.

## Liability When Things Go Wrong

AREC has the authority under Arkansas Code 17-42-311 to impose fines up to $2,500 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional education. But commission discipline is often the least of an agent's concerns when title and closing procedures fail.

If a deal collapses because an agent failed to communicate a title defect within the contractual period, the buyer may sue for out-of-pocket damages including inspection costs, appraisal fees, and rate lock losses. Sellers may counterclaim for market damages if the property must be relisted at a lower price. Both sides will name the agent's brokerage and errors-and-omissions carrier.

Beyond civil liability, agents who knowingly omit title defects from their communications may face fraud claims under Arkansas Code 4-88-107, the state's deceptive trade practices act. This opens the door to treble damages and attorney fees, which no E&O policy fully covers.
