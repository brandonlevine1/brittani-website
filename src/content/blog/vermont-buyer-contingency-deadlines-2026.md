## Why Vermont Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026 Demand Your Full Attention

Missing a single contingency deadline can collapse a deal, expose your client to forfeiture of earnest money, or land your license in front of the Vermont Real Estate Commission. Vermont buyer contingency deadlines 2026 carry heightened importance because the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) updated several standard form provisions in its most recent revision cycle, and the Commission continues to enforce supervisory expectations outlined in 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41. Every agent and broker in the state needs to understand not just the dates on a calendar but the specific contractual mechanics that govern what happens when those dates pass.

The stakes are not abstract. The Vermont Real Estate Commission has disciplinary authority under [26 V.S.A. Section 2296](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/26/041/02296) to suspend or revoke licenses for failure to act competently, which includes negligent handling of contract deadlines. A missed financing contingency deadline that costs a buyer their $15,000 deposit is exactly the kind of fact pattern that triggers complaints.

## The VAR Forms That Control Every Deadline

Vermont residential transactions rely almost entirely on forms published by the Vermont Association of Realtors. The core documents agents must know inside and out are the VR-402 Purchase and Sale Contract, the VR-405 Addendum for Inspection Contingency, the VR-406 Financing Contingency Addendum, and the VR-410 Lead Paint Contingency Addendum. Each form contains its own deadline provisions, and they do not always mirror each other's structure.

**The VR-402 serves as the master contract, and its general provisions section dictates how all deadlines are calculated.** Calendar days are the default unit of measurement. If a deadline expires on a Saturday, Sunday, or Vermont state holiday, performance is pushed to the next business day.

The VR-405 Inspection Contingency Addendum is where most deadline disputes originate. It requires the buyer to complete inspections and deliver a written response — either acceptance, request for repairs, or cancellation — within the negotiated inspection period. Agents who treat this form casually are the ones who end up in mediation.

## How Each Major Contingency Deadline Works

Understanding the contractual trigger for each deadline is more important than memorizing a number of days. The table below breaks down the primary contingencies, their default or typical timeframes, and the consequence of missing them.

| Contingency | Typical Deadline | Trigger Event | Consequence of Missing Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inspection (VR-405) | 10-14 calendar days | Binding agreement date | Buyer deemed to accept property condition |
| Financing (VR-406) | 21-30 calendar days | Binding agreement date | Buyer waives right to cancel for financing failure |
| Appraisal (VR-406, Section 3) | Tied to financing deadline | Binding agreement date | Buyer waives appraisal objection right |
| Lead Paint (VR-410) | 10 calendar days (federal minimum) | Binding agreement date | Buyer waives lead paint cancellation right |
| Title Review | 10-15 calendar days after title delivery | Seller's delivery of title evidence | Buyer deemed to accept title as marketable |
| Sale of Buyer's Property | Negotiated | Binding agreement date | Seller may invoke kick-out clause |

Notice that the appraisal contingency under the VR-406 is not a standalone deadline in most Vermont transactions — it is embedded within the financing contingency. This catches agents off guard, especially those relocating from states where appraisal and financing contingencies operate independently. If your buyer's appraisal comes in low on day 25 of a 21-day financing contingency window, the objection right is already gone.

**The financing contingency deadline is the single most consequential date in a Vermont buyer's contract.** Once it passes, the buyer is contractually bound to close regardless of loan approval status, and the earnest money deposit is at risk if they cannot perform.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make With Contingency Deadlines

Five errors show up repeatedly in Commission complaints and brokerage E&O claims across the state. Recognizing them is the first step toward eliminating them from your practice.

The first mistake is backdating the deadline start. Some agents count the inspection period from the date the buyer signs the offer rather than the date of the binding agreement. Under the VR-402, the binding agreement date is when the last party signs or delivers acceptance, not when the offer was drafted. This miscount can shave two or three days off a buyer's inspection window without anyone realizing it until it is too late.

The second error is relying on verbal extensions. Vermont contract law requires modifications to be in writing. An agent who calls the listing agent and agrees to extend the inspection deadline by five days — without executing a written amendment using the VR-420 Amendment form — has created a modification that may not be enforceable. When the deal sours, the seller's attorney will point to the original deadline and argue the buyer's inspection response was untimely.

**The third mistake is confusing delivery methods with delivery dates.** The VR-402 addresses how notices and responses are delivered (email, hand delivery, or other agreed methods), and the delivery date matters for deadline compliance. An inspection response emailed at 11:58 PM on the last day of the contingency period may or may not be deemed delivered that day depending on how the parties defined delivery in the contract. Agents who assume "sent equals delivered" are playing a dangerous game.

The fourth error involves lead paint deadlines on pre-1978 homes. Federal law under 42 U.S.C. Section 4852d requires a minimum 10-day inspection opportunity, but agents sometimes negotiate shorter inspection periods in the VR-405 without realizing that the lead paint contingency under the VR-410 cannot be shortened below the federal floor. This creates a conflict between the two addenda that can void the buyer's waiver entirely.

The fifth and perhaps most preventable mistake is simply forgetting to calendar a deadline at all. Agents managing multiple transactions in a busy Vermont market — particularly during the compressed spring and summer seasons around Chittenden County and the Upper Valley — lose track of overlapping dates. There is no safety net built into the VAR forms; the deadlines expire whether or not anyone is watching. This is exactly the kind of operational failure that [costs agents deals and commissions](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Vermont law places supervisory responsibility squarely on the broker. Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299, brokers are required to exercise reasonable supervision over the activities of their licensees. The Vermont Real Estate Commission has interpreted this to include transaction file review and deadline monitoring, particularly in disciplinary actions involving missed contingencies.

Brokers should audit three areas of every active transaction file. First, verify that the binding agreement date is clearly documented and that all subsequent deadlines are correctly calculated from that date. Second, confirm that every contingency response, extension, or waiver is memorialized in writing using the appropriate VAR form — VR-420 for amendments, VR-415 for contingency removal. Third, check that the delivery method and timestamp for each deadline-sensitive communication match the requirements specified in the contract.

**A broker who discovers a deadline error mid-transaction has a narrow window to correct it before the consequences become irreversible.** If the inspection period has lapsed and no response was delivered, the broker needs to immediately assess whether a written extension can be secured from the seller or whether the buyer's position has been compromised. Documenting the remedial steps taken is equally important for defending against a future Commission complaint.

Many Vermont brokerages still rely on spreadsheets or memory to track deadlines across their roster. That approach fails at scale, and it fails at exactly the worst moment — when an agent is on vacation, sick, or simply overwhelmed. Automated [checklist and deadline systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) are not optional for brokerages that want to avoid E&O exposure; they are a baseline operational requirement.

## Liability Exposure When Deadlines Are Blown

The consequences of a missed contingency deadline in Vermont operate on three levels: contractual, regulatory, and civil.

At the contractual level, the buyer loses the protection of the contingency. A missed financing deadline under the VR-406 means the buyer cannot cancel the contract if their loan falls through. A missed inspection deadline under the VR-405 means the buyer is deemed to have accepted the property's condition, including defects that a timely inspection would have uncovered.

At the regulatory level, the Vermont Real Estate Commission can open an investigation based on a consumer complaint or a referral from another licensee. Violations of the competency standard under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296(a)(1) can result in license suspension, mandatory continuing education, fines, or a consent order that becomes part of the agent's public disciplinary record. The Commission's investigative process is detailed in its Rules of Practice, and agents should understand that the burden of proof in administrative proceedings is lower than in civil court.

At the civil level, the buyer or seller may sue the agent for negligence. Vermont follows a standard negligence framework: duty, breach, causation, and damages. An agent who failed to calendar a financing deadline and whose buyer lost a $20,000 deposit as a result has a textbook breach-of-duty problem. E&O insurance will likely respond, but the deductible, premium increase, and reputational damage are real costs that no policy fully covers.

The intersection of these three liability layers is what makes [understanding where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) so critical for practicing agents. A single missed deadline can trigger all three simultaneously.

## How to Build a Deadline Tracking System That Actually Works

A reliable system has three components: a centralized calendar, automated reminders, and a secondary verification step.

The centralized calendar must be shared between the agent and their transaction coordinator or broker. Include a buffer reminder — typically 48 to 72 hours before each deadline — so the agent has time to obtain the buyer's written response or request an extension.
