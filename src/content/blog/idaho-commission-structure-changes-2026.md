## Understanding Idaho Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026

The practice changes stemming from the National Association of Realtors settlement have now been in effect for nearly two years, and Idaho agents who treated 2024's rollout as a soft launch are running out of grace period. Idaho commission structure rules after the nar settlement 2026 demand that every agent and broker operating under the Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) follow specific documentation, disclosure, and negotiation protocols—or face real consequences. The days of assumed co-op commission splits communicated through the Intermountain MLS are over.

Idaho's regulatory framework sits at the intersection of federal settlement terms and state-specific statutes. IREC enforces Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20 (the Idaho Real Estate Brokerage Representation Act), which already required written agency disclosures and fiduciary accountability. The NAR settlement layered additional requirements on top of that foundation, and IREC has signaled through its 2025-2026 enforcement guidance that it will hold licensees to both sets of rules simultaneously.

## What the NAR Settlement Changed for Idaho Agents

**The single biggest operational shift is that buyer agent compensation can no longer appear in MLS listing data.** The Intermountain MLS removed the co-op compensation field in August 2024, and Idaho agents must now negotiate buyer-side compensation directly with buyers, sellers, or both—outside of the MLS system.

This means three things in practice. First, buyers must sign a written representation agreement before any property touring begins. Second, that agreement must state a specific compensation amount or percentage—not a vague "whatever the seller offers." Third, if the seller is not offering compensation or is offering less than what the buyer agreed to pay, the buyer agent must have a plan for addressing that gap before writing an offer.

Idaho REALTORS updated its standard forms to reflect these requirements. The RE-14 Buyer Representation Agreement is the primary document, and it now includes fields for specifying exact compensation terms, duration of the agreement, and the scope of services provided.

## Key Idaho Forms and Statutory Requirements

Idaho agents must be familiar with the specific forms and statutes governing commission disclosure and buyer representation. Here is how the documents map to the compliance requirements:

| Form / Statute | Purpose | When Required |
|---|---|---|
| RE-14 (Buyer Representation Agreement) | Establishes compensation terms and agency relationship with buyer | Before first property showing |
| RE-36 (Seller Representation Agreement) | Documents listing terms including any offered buyer agent compensation | At listing appointment |
| RE-44 (Disclosure of Agency) | Discloses agency relationships to all parties | First substantial contact |
| Idaho Code 54-2050 | Requires written brokerage representation agreements | Before performing licensed activities |
| Idaho Code 54-2060 | Defines grounds for disciplinary action including misrepresentation | Ongoing compliance |

The RE-14 form requires agents to fill in a dollar amount or percentage—not "TBD" or "per MLS." IREC has clarified that leaving compensation blank or referencing external sources violates the settlement's requirement for specific, disclosed terms. The form also requires a definitive expiration date, which cannot exceed a "reasonable" duration per [IREC's guidance](https://irec.idaho.gov/).

Under Idaho Code 54-2050, performing brokerage services without a signed representation agreement already constituted a violation before the settlement. The NAR terms simply added specificity to what "written agreement" must contain regarding compensation.

## How Compensation Gets Negotiated Now

The mechanics of commission negotiation in Idaho have shifted from a one-step process (seller sets co-op in MLS) to a multi-step negotiation that agents must document at each stage.

**Seller-side compensation offers still exist, but they travel through different channels.** A listing agent can communicate that a seller is willing to contribute to buyer agent compensation through direct agent-to-agent conversation, marketing materials outside the MLS, or as a seller concession written into the RE-25 Purchase and Sale Agreement. What cannot happen is that offer appearing as a field in the Intermountain MLS.

On the buyer side, the agent must first establish their fee in the RE-14. If the buyer's agent fee is 2.5% and the seller only offers 2%, the buyer agent must either absorb the 0.5% difference, ask the buyer to cover it, or renegotiate with the seller. This gap management is where many [deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in post-settlement transactions.

The RE-25 now includes language allowing parties to specify how buyer agent compensation is handled within the purchase agreement itself. Agents should treat this section with the same care as the purchase price—because functionally, it is part of the total transaction cost.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Are Making

After monitoring enforcement actions and industry feedback through mid-2026, several patterns of non-compliance have emerged among Idaho licensees.

**First, agents are still showing property without a signed RE-14 in hand.** Some treat an initial consultation or open house attendance as exempt from the written agreement requirement. It is not. IREC's position is that any activity constituting "brokerage services" under Idaho Code 54-2038 triggers the agreement requirement, and property tours clearly qualify.

Second, agents are using vague compensation language. Writing "up to 3%" or "negotiable" in the RE-14 does not satisfy the settlement requirement for a specific amount. The form must state what the buyer will owe—even if that amount may later be offset by seller concessions. Ambiguity exposes the agent to claims of misrepresentation under Idaho Code 54-2060(1)(a).

Third, some listing agents are still inputting compensation signals into MLS remarks fields. Phrases like "generous seller concessions available" or "full cooperation" in agent-only remarks have drawn scrutiny from Intermountain MLS compliance staff. The prohibition covers any communication of buyer agent compensation through MLS fields, including remarks.

Fourth, agents are failing to update the RE-14 when circumstances change. If a buyer initially agrees to pay 2.5% but the seller later offers 2%, the buyer's obligation changes—and the RE-14 should be amended to reflect the actual amount the buyer will pay out of pocket versus what the seller covers. Stale agreements create disputes at closing.

Fifth, dual agency situations are creating compounded disclosure failures. When an Idaho agent represents both sides under a disclosed dual agency arrangement (permitted under Idaho Code 54-2085), they must still maintain separate, specific compensation disclosures for each role. Agents are collapsing both sides into a single commission figure without proper documentation of how it was allocated.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear direct supervisory responsibility under Idaho Code 54-2044, which makes the designated broker accountable for the acts of their associated licensees. Post-settlement, this means brokers must implement systematic compliance checks rather than relying on agent self-reporting.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| RE-14 completion | Signed before first showing, specific compensation, valid expiration | Missing date, blank compensation field, no expiration |
| MLS compliance | No compensation language in any MLS field or remarks | "Bonus," "co-op," or "concession" language in remarks |
| RE-25 compensation terms | Buyer agent fee clearly addressed in purchase agreement | No reference to how buyer agent gets paid |
| File documentation | Commission source and amount documented before closing | Commission appearing at closing without paper trail |
| Dual agency disclosures | Separate compensation disclosure for each side | Single lump-sum commission without allocation |

**Brokers should conduct weekly file reviews during the first 30 days of any new listing or buyer engagement.** Waiting until closing to audit creates liability that compounds—if a defective RE-14 is discovered at closing, the deal may need to be delayed or restructured, and the brokerage faces exposure for the entire period of non-compliance.

IREC's disciplinary process under Idaho Code 54-2060 allows fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension, and mandatory education requirements. Brokers who demonstrate systemic failure to supervise face enhanced penalties, including potential revocation of their designated broker license. The Commission has publicly stated that "I didn't know my agents were doing it that way" is not a defense.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance in Idaho

The penalty structure for violating commission disclosure rules operates on multiple levels simultaneously. IREC administrative action is the most visible risk, but it is not the only one.

Civil liability presents a more immediate financial threat. A buyer who discovers their agent was earning undisclosed compensation—or compensation different from what the RE-14 specified—has grounds for a breach of fiduciary duty claim under Idaho common law and potentially a Consumer Protection Act violation under Idaho Code 48-603. Damages in such cases can include disgorgement of the entire commission plus attorney fees.

Deal cancellation is the third risk. Under the NAR settlement terms, a transaction where the buyer representation agreement does not meet the specificity requirements could be challenged by either party. While Idaho courts have not yet issued post-settlement case law on this point, the contractual framework supports rescission arguments if material terms (like who pays the buyer's agent) were never properly documented.

Transaction coordinators managing Idaho files should flag any closing where the commission disbursement does not match the RE-14 terms. This kind of [deadline and document tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) catches errors before they become enforcement actions.
