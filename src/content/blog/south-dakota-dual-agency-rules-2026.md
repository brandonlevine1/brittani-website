## Understanding South Dakota Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The rules governing **South Dakota dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** are codified primarily in SDCL Chapter 36-21A, the state's Real Estate Brokerage Disclosure Act. Every licensee working under the jurisdiction of the South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) needs to know exactly where the statutory lines fall, because the consequences of getting agency wrong are not theoretical. They range from license suspension to deal rescission to personal civil liability.

South Dakota permits both dual agency and designated agency, but the two arrangements carry different obligations, different disclosures, and different risk profiles. This post breaks down what the statutes actually require, where agents routinely stumble, and what brokers should be auditing in every transaction file.

## What the Statutes Actually Say About Dual Agency

South Dakota's dual agency framework lives in SDCL 36-21A-109. The statute permits a single licensee to represent both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction, but only with the informed written consent of both parties. "Informed" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence: the agent must explain, before consent is given, that a dual agent cannot advocate for one party's interests over the other's.

**A dual agent in South Dakota is prohibited from disclosing confidential information about either party's negotiating position, motivation, or financial condition without that party's written permission.** This restriction survives even after the transaction closes. The practical effect is that a dual agent functions more like a transaction facilitator than an advocate, and both clients must understand that shift in writing.

The [SDREC](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/) publishes guidance documents reinforcing that dual agency consent must be obtained before any confidential information is exchanged. Agents who begin working with both sides and then circle back for signatures after the fact are already in violation.

## Designated Agency: The Brokerage-Level Alternative

SDCL 36-21A-112 gives brokerages a second option. Instead of one agent serving both sides, the responsible broker can designate one licensee to represent the buyer and a different licensee within the same brokerage to represent the seller. This is designated agency, and it preserves each client's right to full advocacy from their individual agent.

The responsible broker retains supervisory duties over both designated agents but must not share confidential information received from one side with the agent on the other side. That firewall responsibility falls squarely on the broker, not on the individual agents. Brokers who run open-floor-plan offices with no information-barrier protocols should take special note.

Designated agency requires written consent from both parties, just like dual agency. The key difference is that each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties to their respective client, while a dual agent owes limited duties to both. The table below puts the distinctions side by side.

| Feature | Dual Agency (SDCL 36-21A-109) | Designated Agency (SDCL 36-21A-112) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of agents involved | One agent, both sides | Two agents, same brokerage |
| Advocacy level | Limited; neutral facilitation | Full fiduciary to each client |
| Confidentiality firewall | Agent self-manages | Broker must enforce |
| Written consent required | Yes, both parties | Yes, both parties |
| Broker supervision role | Standard | Elevated; firewall obligations |

Understanding these differences matters when you are deciding which path to recommend, and it matters even more when you are filling out the correct forms.

## Required Forms and Disclosure Timing

South Dakota mandates the use of the Brokerage Disclosure Form, which must be presented at the first substantive contact with a prospective buyer or seller. The SDREC prescribes this form, and it covers seller agency, buyer agency, dual agency, and designated agency. Agents should use the current version of the SDREC-approved disclosure form available through their brokerage or directly from the Commission.

**The timing requirement is not a suggestion.** SDCL 36-21A-104 specifies that agency disclosure must occur before any confidential information is disclosed and before any brokerage services are provided. If you show a listing to a buyer before handing over the disclosure form, you have already failed the timing test.

When dual agency arises mid-transaction (for example, an existing buyer client wants to write an offer on your own listing), a separate written dual agency consent must be executed. This consent supplements the original disclosure and must clearly state the limitations on the agent's duties. ## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The SDREC has authority under SDCL 36-21A-119 and SDCL 36-21A-120 to impose sanctions when agents violate the disclosure or consent requirements. Sanctions include formal reprimand, license suspension, license revocation, and civil penalties. The Commission can also require agents to complete additional continuing education as a condition of license reinstatement.

Beyond regulatory action, an undisclosed dual agency relationship creates civil liability exposure. Either party to the transaction can seek rescission of the purchase agreement, arguing they did not give informed consent. Courts in South Dakota have recognized that an agent's failure to disclose a material conflict of interest, such as representing both sides, can constitute a breach of fiduciary duty giving rise to damages.

There is also a practical deal-level risk. Title companies and lenders conducting file reviews may flag missing agency disclosures. A missing consent form discovered during underwriting can delay closing or, in worst-case scenarios, kill the deal entirely. The cost of one skipped form can cascade into lost commissions, damaged client relationships, and formal complaints to the SDREC.

## Five Common Mistakes Agents Make With Dual and Designated Agency

**First, agents frequently obtain dual agency consent too late.** The scenario is predictable: an agent lists a property, an unrepresented buyer walks in at an open house, and the agent begins discussing price and terms before presenting any disclosure. By the time paperwork catches up, confidential information has already crossed both directions. The violation is already complete.

Second, agents confuse dual agency with designated agency on the forms themselves. Checking the wrong box on the Brokerage Disclosure Form is not a harmless clerical error. It misrepresents the agency relationship and can void the consent entirely. If two different agents in the same brokerage are handling opposite sides, the correct designation is designated agency, not dual agency.

Third, agents assume verbal disclosure satisfies the statutory requirement. SDCL 36-21A-109 explicitly requires written consent. A phone conversation where the buyer says "sure, that's fine" is not compliant. Without a signed consent document, the agent has no defense against a later complaint.

Fourth, agents in designated agency arrangements share information across the brokerage firewall. This often happens innocently during Monday morning office meetings or in shared CRM notes. The responsible broker must have protocols preventing an agent representing the seller from accessing the buyer agent's transaction notes, and vice versa. [Understanding where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often points back to exactly these types of internal communication failures.

Fifth, agents fail to update the agency relationship when circumstances change. A buyer who was working with Agent A under a buyer agency agreement may decide to purchase a property listed by Agent B in the same brokerage. The agency relationship has now shifted, and new consent documents must be executed. Agents who treat the original disclosure as a blanket authorization for any scenario are wrong.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Responsible brokers in South Dakota carry an affirmative obligation under SDCL 36-21A-112 and SDCL 36-21A-113 to supervise agency disclosures across every transaction their agents handle. This is not a passive duty. Brokers cannot claim ignorance when an agent under their supervision fails to obtain proper consent.

**Every transaction file should be reviewed for three things: the signed Brokerage Disclosure Form, the correct agency type checked on that form, and the signed dual or designated agency consent if applicable.** Missing any one of these documents exposes the brokerage to regulatory complaints and civil liability. A quarterly audit of closed and pending files is the minimum standard brokers should follow.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage Disclosure Form | Present and signed before first substantive contact | Date on form is after first showing or offer |
| Agency type selection | Correct box checked matching actual arrangement | Dual agency checked when two brokerage agents are involved |
| Dual agency consent | Separate written consent signed by both parties | Missing entirely or signed after offer acceptance |
| Designated agency consent | Both parties consented; broker designated agents in writing | No written broker designation on file |
| Confidentiality firewall | No cross-party notes in shared CRM or files | Buyer and seller agent sharing a single transaction folder |

Brokers should also conduct training at least annually on the differences between dual and designated agency. The SDREC's continuing education requirements do not specifically mandate dual agency training every renewal cycle, so it falls on the brokerage to fill the gap. Documenting that training occurred protects the broker if an agent later claims they were not aware of the rules.
