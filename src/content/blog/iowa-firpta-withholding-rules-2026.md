## Understanding Iowa FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every agent working with international clients in Iowa needs to understand the Iowa firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 before listing a single property. The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act requires buyers to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when purchasing real estate from a foreign person or entity, and the consequences of noncompliance fall on multiple parties at the closing table. Iowa's real estate market—particularly in Des Moines, Iowa City, and the Quad Cities—continues to attract foreign investment in both agricultural land and residential properties, making this an increasingly common scenario for agents statewide.

FIRPTA is a federal statute codified at 26 U.S.C. Sections 1445 and 897. It applies regardless of whether the transaction closes in Polk County or rural Winneshiek County. The Iowa Real Estate Commission, while not directly enforcing federal tax law, expects licensees to understand their disclosure and procedural obligations when foreign sellers are involved.

## Who Qualifies as a "Foreign Person" Under FIRPTA

**A foreign person includes any nonresident alien individual, foreign corporation, foreign partnership, foreign trust, or foreign estate as defined by the Internal Revenue Code.** Green card holders are generally treated as U.S. residents for tax purposes and are typically exempt from FIRPTA withholding. Dual citizens with U.S. citizenship are also exempt.

The determination matters more than agents realize. A seller who lived in Iowa for years on a work visa but never obtained permanent resident status still qualifies as a foreign person under FIRPTA. Agents cannot rely on how long someone has lived in the state or whether they have an Iowa driver's license.

The IRS uses the "substantial presence test" and immigration status—not state residency—to make this determination. When in doubt, the seller should provide a [Certification of Non-Foreign Status](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026) (often an affidavit attached to the closing documents) or agents should direct the seller to a tax attorney before closing.

## The Withholding Rates and Key Exceptions

The default FIRPTA withholding rate is 15% of the gross amount realized on the sale. However, several exceptions reduce or eliminate the requirement. The most commonly triggered exception in Iowa residential transactions is the personal-use residence exemption.

| Scenario | Withholding Rate | Key Condition |
|----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Sale price over $1,000,000 | 15% | No exceptions apply |
| Sale price $300,001–$1,000,000, buyer will use as residence | 10% | Buyer must sign affidavit of intended use |
| Sale price $300,000 or less, buyer will use as residence | 0% | Buyer must sign affidavit of intended use |
| Seller obtains IRS Withholding Certificate | Reduced or 0% | IRS approval via Form 8288-B before closing |
| Seller provides Non-Foreign Affidavit | 0% | Seller certifies under penalty of perjury they are not a foreign person |

Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) must be filed with the IRS within 20 days of closing. Form 8288-A accompanies it as a statement showing the tax withheld. If the seller applies for a reduced withholding rate, Form 8288-B must be submitted before closing, and the IRS typically takes 90 days to process the application.

## Iowa-Specific Considerations: State Tax and Nonresident Withholding

Iowa does not impose a separate FIRPTA-style withholding tax at the state level. However, Iowa Code Chapter 422 governs state income tax obligations for nonresidents who realize gain on Iowa real property. The Iowa Department of Revenue may expect nonresident sellers—including foreign persons—to file Iowa Form IA 1040 or IA 1120 and pay tax on Iowa-source income.

**Closing agents and title companies in Iowa often build in a state tax escrow or require estimated payment alongside the federal FIRPTA withholding to protect the buyer from successor liability.** This is not legally mandated the same way FIRPTA is, but it has become standard practice at major Iowa title companies including Iowa Title Guaranty (the state-chartered title guaranty program unique to Iowa).

Iowa Title Guaranty, governed by Iowa Code Chapter 16.91, operates differently from private title insurers in other states. Agents should confirm with their closing team whether ITG or a private underwriter is handling the file, as procedures for holding and remitting withholding funds may differ.

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

The liability chain under FIRPTA is aggressive. The buyer (transferee) is primarily liable for the 15% withholding. If the buyer fails to withhold, the IRS can pursue the full amount plus interest and penalties directly from the buyer. But secondary liability extends further.

Under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(e), any agent of the transferee or transferor who has actual knowledge that a certification of non-foreign status is false may be held liable for the tax. This means listing agents who know their seller is a foreign person and do nothing—or buyer's agents who facilitate a closing without proper withholding—can face personal liability.

The penalties are not theoretical. The IRS can assess a penalty equal to the amount that should have been withheld. On a $500,000 sale, that is $75,000 in potential personal exposure. Beyond IRS penalties, the Iowa Real Estate Commission (operating under Iowa Code Chapter 543B) can take disciplinary action against a licensee whose negligence in a transaction harms a client or third party, including license suspension or revocation.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make with FIRPTA

**The first and most frequent error is assuming FIRPTA does not apply because the seller "lives here."** Physical presence in Iowa does not equal U.S. tax residency. Agents regularly conflate state domicile with federal immigration and tax status, and this single misunderstanding causes the majority of FIRPTA compliance failures in the state.

The second mistake is relying on the seller's verbal representation without obtaining written certification. The Non-Foreign Affidavit (sometimes called a FIRPTA Affidavit or Transferor's Certification) must be in writing, include the seller's taxpayer identification number, and be signed under penalty of perjury. A handshake or email confirmation is legally worthless.

Third, agents confuse the buyer's intended-use exception with an automatic exemption. The reduced rate for properties under $1,000,000 only applies when the buyer signs an affidavit confirming they will use the property as a personal residence for at least 50% of the days it is in use during each of the first two 12-month periods after closing. If the buyer is an investor, the full 15% applies regardless of sale price.

Fourth, agents fail to [track the 20-day filing deadline](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) for Form 8288 after closing. This is not optional. Missing this window triggers automatic penalties, and the closing agent or attorney typically handles it—but the agent who promised their client "everything is taken care of" may find themselves in a malpractice claim if it was not.

Fifth, agents in agricultural transactions forget that FIRPTA applies to farmland. Iowa's 30 million acres of cropland attract significant foreign investment, and the Agricultural Foreign Investment Disclosure Act (AFIDA) reporting requirements under 7 U.S.C. 3501 run parallel to FIRPTA obligations. Failing on both fronts compounds the compliance exposure.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers operating under Iowa Code 543B.34 have supervisory duties over their licensees' transactions. A broker who allows repeated FIRPTA noncompliance in their office faces the same disciplinary risk as the individual agent—plus potential liability as a "transferee's agent" under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|-------------------|-----------|----------------------|
| FIRPTA affidavit present in file | Every transaction with nonresident seller | Signed Non-Foreign Affidavit or Form 8288 confirmation |
| Seller citizenship/residency verified | At listing agreement execution | Written certification or attorney referral note |
| Form 8288 filing confirmation | Within 25 days of closing | IRS filing receipt or title company confirmation |
| Buyer intended-use affidavit (if reduced rate) | At closing | Signed buyer affidavit in closing file |
| Agent training documentation | Annually | CE records or internal training log |

Brokers should build FIRPTA compliance into their [transaction checklist systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rather than relying on individual agent memory. The Iowa Real Estate Commission's 2025 guidance bulletin emphasized that brokers bear responsibility for ensuring their agents understand federal withholding obligations, even though FIRPTA is not state law.

Every listing intake form should include a question about the seller's citizenship and tax residency status. If the answer triggers FIRPTA, the file should be flagged immediately, the closing agent notified, and a tax attorney referral made to the seller. This is not tax advice—it is procedural compliance that protects the brokerage.

## Practical Workflow for Iowa Agents Handling Foreign Sellers

At listing appointment, ask directly: "Are you a U.S. citizen or permanent resident for tax purposes?" Document the answer in writing. If the seller is a foreign person, notify your broker and the anticipated closing agent within 48 hours.

Before going under contract, ensure the purchase agreement addresses FIRPTA withholding. The Iowa Association of Realtors purchase agreement forms include provisions related to tax withholding obligations, but agents should confirm these clauses are not struck or modified without legal counsel. The buyer's agent should be informed immediately so their client understands cash-at-closing will be reduced by the withholding amount.

If the seller wants to reduce withholding below 15%, they must file Form 8288-B with the IRS well before closing—ideally 90 to 120 days in advance. This requires coordination with a CPA or tax attorney, and agents should never attempt to prepare this form themselves. The transaction timeline must account for IRS processing delays, which have averaged 90 days in 2025 and show no signs of improving.
