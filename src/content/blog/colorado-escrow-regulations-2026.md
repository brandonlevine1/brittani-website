## Understanding Colorado Escrow Regulations 2026 and Why They Matter Now

Colorado escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who fail to handle trust funds correctly. The Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC), operating under the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA), updated its position statements and rule interpretations in late 2025, tightening expectations around deposit timelines, record-keeping granularity, and broker supervision duties. If you hold a Colorado license and touch earnest money at any point in a transaction, the rules below apply directly to you.

The governing authority is Colorado Revised Statutes Title 12, Article 10, Part 2—specifically C.R.S. 12-10-217, which addresses trust account requirements. CREC Rule E-1 through E-6 further define how brokers must manage, account for, and disburse funds. Violations don't just risk a fine; they can unravel a transaction, trigger civil liability, and end a career.

This post breaks down the statutes, the forms you must use correctly, the mistakes that keep showing up in DORA audits, and what employing brokers need to check quarterly to avoid regulatory action.

## The Statutory Framework: C.R.S. 12-10-217 and CREC Rules E-1 Through E-6

**Colorado law requires that all money belonging to others and received by a broker in connection with a real estate transaction be deposited into a trust account at a recognized Colorado financial institution.** The statute names the employing broker—not the associate agent—as the party responsible for trust account management. That said, an associate who mishandles funds can still face personal disciplinary action under C.R.S. 12-10-201(6)(a).

CREC Rule E-1 sets the deposit timeline: funds must be deposited according to the contract terms, or if the contract is silent, by the next business day after the broker receives them. Rule E-4 addresses record-keeping, requiring both a journal (chronological record of all deposits and disbursements) and a subsidiary ledger (per-transaction breakdown). Rule E-5 mandates monthly reconciliations between the bank statement, the journal, and the ledgers.

The distinction between Rule E-4 and E-5 matters during an audit. DORA auditors check both independently, and a broker who reconciles the bank statement but fails to maintain transaction-level ledgers will still receive a violation notice.

## Forms That Govern Escrow in Colorado Transactions

Colorado residential transactions rely on CREC-approved forms published by the Colorado Real Estate Commission in conjunction with the Colorado Association of Realtors (CAR). The primary contract forms—CBS1-6-24 (Contract to Buy and Sell, Residential) and CBS2-6-24 (Contract to Buy and Sell, Commercial)—contain sections specifying earnest money amount, form of payment, holder identity, and deposit deadline.

| Form Number | Purpose | Escrow-Related Section |
|---|---|---|
| CBS1-6-24 | Residential purchase contract | Section 4 (Earnest Money) |
| CBS2-6-24 | Commercial purchase contract | Section 4 (Earnest Money) |
| CP40-6-24 | Earnest Money Receipt | Confirms deposit and holder |
| CP44-6-24 | Earnest Money Dispute | Triggers interpleader or mediation |
| TD64-6-24 | Trust Account Disbursement Authorization | Authorizes release of funds |

**Agents must ensure Section 4 of the CBS forms is filled out completely—leaving the "Holder" line blank is a compliance failure, not an oversight.** The CP40 form serves as proof of deposit and must be retained for four years per CREC Rule E-6. When a deal collapses and parties disagree about who gets the earnest money, CP44-6-24 initiates the formal dispute process.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of escrow violations in Colorado fall into three categories: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and transactional fallout.

DORA can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under C.R.S. 12-10-201. For repeat offenders or intentional mishandling, license suspension or revocation is on the table. The Commission publishes disciplinary actions on its [official website](https://dora.colorado.gov/real-estate), and those records follow you permanently.

Civil liability often exceeds the regulatory fine. If a buyer loses earnest money because a broker failed to deposit it on time and the funds were lost or misapplied, the buyer can sue for damages. Colorado courts have awarded actual damages plus attorney fees in cases where brokers violated statutory duties. The broker's E&O insurance may not cover trust account violations, since many policies exclude "dishonest or fraudulent acts"—and a regulator's finding of mishandling can be characterized that way in litigation.

On the transactional side, escrow violations can give the non-breaching party grounds to terminate under Section 25 of the CBS contract. A seller who discovers that earnest money was never deposited may invoke default provisions and walk away, keeping whatever remedies the contract provides.

## Common Mistakes Colorado Agents Make With Escrow

Five errors appear repeatedly in DORA disciplinary reports and broker audit findings. Each one is preventable.

**First: depositing earnest money into the wrong account.** New agents sometimes deposit checks into their personal account or into a brokerage operating account rather than the designated trust account. Under Rule E-1, this constitutes conversion regardless of intent. Even a brief hold in the wrong account triggers a violation.

Second: failing to note the deposit deadline in the contract. When Section 4 of the CBS form doesn't specify a deposit timeline, the "next business day" default under Rule E-1 kicks in. Many agents assume they have three to five days. They don't. If a check arrives on Tuesday afternoon and sits in a desk drawer until Thursday, the broker is already non-compliant.

Third: releasing funds without written authorization from both parties. After a termination, some agents instruct title companies or their brokers to release earnest money based on a phone call or verbal agreement. Colorado requires signed authorization—either a mutual release or a completed TD64-6-24 form. Verbal instructions are insufficient and expose the broker to liability from either side.

Fourth: failing to disclose interest-bearing trust account terms. When earnest money is placed in an interest-bearing account, CREC Rule E-3 requires that the parties be informed in writing of who earns the interest. Many agents skip this disclosure. The CBS contract addresses it in Section 4.6, but agents who don't review that section with clients create a compliance gap.

Fifth: not retaining records for the mandatory four-year period. Rule E-6 mandates that all trust account records—journals, ledgers, bank statements, deposit receipts, and reconciliations—be kept for four years from the date of the last activity on the account. Agents who switch brokerages and don't ensure their transaction files transfer properly risk leaving their former broker with incomplete records, which can result in violations for both parties.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers carry the primary statutory burden for trust account compliance. CREC Rule E-5 requires monthly reconciliations, but best practice—and what DORA auditors expect to see in 2026—goes further.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Bank reconciliation | Monthly | Bank balance matches journal total |
| Subsidiary ledger review | Monthly | Each transaction ledger sums to overall balance |
| Deposit timing | Per transaction | CP40 date vs. contract deadline or next-business-day rule |
| Stale funds identification | Quarterly | Funds held more than 120 days without activity |
| Agent file completeness | Quarterly | CP40, TD64, and contract Section 4 all present and signed |

**Brokers who delegate reconciliation to an office manager or bookkeeper remain personally liable for errors.** DORA does not accept "I didn't know" as a defense. The 2025 rule interpretation update clarified that broker responsibility is non-delegable in the regulatory sense—meaning even if someone else performs the reconciliation, the broker must review and sign off.

Stale funds deserve particular attention. When earnest money sits in trust for more than 120 days without a closing or a signed release, the broker must either obtain written direction from both parties or initiate interpleader proceedings under C.R.S. 38-13-117. Letting disputed funds languish indefinitely is itself a violation.

Brokers should also audit their agents' use of CP44-6-24 in terminated transactions. If a deal dies and no earnest money dispute form or mutual release appears in the file within ten business days, that's a red flag worth investigating immediately.
