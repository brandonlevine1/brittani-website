# Oregon Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of "Oregon attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" trips up agents who relocate from attorney-mandatory states or who handle their first transaction involving out-of-state counsel. Oregon falls firmly in the title-company camp: no attorney is required at the settlement table, and the vast majority of residential closings are conducted by licensed escrow officers without any lawyer in the room. Understanding exactly where the statutory lines fall protects your license, your client, and your commission check.

Oregon is one of roughly 30 states where an escrow or title company can perform all settlement functions without attorney supervision. That does not mean attorneys are irrelevant — it means the regulatory framework assigns specific duties to specific parties, and agents who blur those lines face real consequences.

## Oregon's Statutory Framework for Closing

The governing authority is the [Oregon Division of Financial Regulation](https://dfr.oregon.gov/) (DFR), which licenses and oversees escrow agents under ORS Chapter 696, specifically ORS 696.505 through 696.590. Title insurance companies operating in Oregon must also comply with ORS Chapter 731 and ORS 735.100 et seq. The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA), operating under the Oregon Real Estate Commissioner, regulates licensee conduct through ORS Chapter 696 and OAR Chapter 863.

**Escrow agents in Oregon must hold an individual license issued by the DFR or be an employee of a title insurance company authorized to do business in the state.** They prepare settlement statements, hold earnest money, collect documents, record deeds, and disburse funds. None of these functions requires attorney involvement under Oregon law.

The Oregon State Bar has weighed in repeatedly: drafting standard-form real estate contracts does not constitute the practice of law when agents use forms approved by the Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) committee. However, modifying those forms with custom legal language or advising clients on legal rights absolutely does cross the line.

## What the Title Company Actually Does at Closing

Oregon closings are typically "escrow closings" rather than "round-table closings." Parties rarely sit in the same room. The escrow officer coordinates signing appointments — sometimes days apart — and records documents once all conditions are satisfied.

Here is what happens in sequence: the escrow officer receives the fully executed sale agreement (OREF Form 001), opens an escrow file, orders a preliminary title report, collects lender documents, prepares the closing disclosure in coordination with the lender, schedules signings, collects funds, records the deed, and disburses proceeds. The title company simultaneously issues a title insurance policy under its underwriter's authority.

Agents should understand that an escrow officer is not an advocate for either party. They are a neutral third party bound by escrow instructions. If a dispute arises between buyer and seller over closing terms, the escrow officer cannot mediate or advise — that is where attorneys become relevant, even in a non-attorney state.

## When Attorneys Do Show Up in Oregon Transactions

**Attorneys appear in Oregon closings more often than agents expect, particularly in commercial deals, probate sales, short sales, and transactions involving trusts or conservatorships.** Their presence is optional but common when legal complexity exceeds what standard forms can address.

A buyer or seller can retain personal counsel at any point. OREF Form 001 (Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement) includes advisory language encouraging parties to consult an attorney. If one party has an attorney and the other does not, the escrow officer still runs the closing — the attorney simply reviews documents on behalf of their client.

Oregon courts have clarified that an attorney representing a party at closing cannot also serve as the escrow agent for that transaction due to conflict-of-interest rules under Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 1.7. This distinction matters when a seller's attorney offers to "handle the closing" — they can represent the seller but cannot act as the neutral escrow holder.

## Common Mistakes Oregon Agents Make at Closing

**The first common mistake is drafting custom addenda instead of directing clients to an attorney.** OREF provides standard addendum forms (Form 024 for general addenda, Form 033 for inspection contingency response), but agents who write bespoke legal language risk an unauthorized-practice-of-law complaint filed with the Oregon State Bar or discipline from OREA under ORS 696.290.

The second mistake is directing the escrow officer on how to disburse funds or resolve disputes between parties. The escrow officer follows escrow instructions signed by both parties. When an agent calls the title company and says "release the earnest money to my buyer," they are overstepping their role. Disbursement authority rests with the principals through written instructions or, if disputed, a court order or mutual release (OREF Form 038).

A third error is failing to disclose dual agency status to the escrow officer. Under OAR 863-015-0215, disclosed limited agency must be documented and provided to all parties, including the closing agent. If the escrow officer discovers undisclosed dual agency after closing, the transaction may face rescission claims and the agent faces disciplinary action from OREA.

The fourth mistake involves [missing contractual deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) embedded in OREF forms. Oregon's standard agreements include specific timeframes — five business days for the buyer's investigation period under Form 001, for example — and agents who let these lapse without written extension create default situations that complicate closing.

The fifth mistake is assuming the title company will catch every compliance issue. Title companies verify title status and lien positions. They do not verify that the agent obtained proper agency disclosures, confirmed the buyer's financing contingency was properly waived, or ensured the seller's disclosure (Oregon Property Condition Disclosure form per ORS 105.465) was delivered within the required timeframe.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Oregon's Principal Broker is personally responsible under ORS 696.022 for supervising all licensees operating under their license. This is not a suggestion — it is a statutory obligation that creates direct liability when transactions go sideways.

Brokers should audit every closed file for the following: properly executed Initial Agency Disclosure (OREF Form 060) signed before substantive discussions, timely delivery of the Seller's Property Disclosure Statement, confirmation that earnest money was deposited within the timeframe specified in the sale agreement (typically two business days), and evidence that the buyer received and acknowledged the lead-based paint disclosure for pre-1978 properties.

**A quarterly audit system — rather than a post-complaint scramble — catches problems before they metastasize into OREA investigations or E&O claims.** Brokers who rely on memory instead of documented [checklist-based reviews](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) are gambling with their own license. The Oregon Real Estate Commissioner has the authority under ORS 696.396 to fine a principal broker up to $5,000 per violation and can suspend or revoke the broker's license for failure to supervise.

The [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) model works particularly well for Oregon brokerages managing high transaction volume. By reviewing each file's status against required milestones — disclosures delivered, contingencies tracked, escrow instructions confirmed — brokers catch compliance gaps before closing day rather than after.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

OREA maintains an active enforcement division. Violations of ORS Chapter 696 can result in civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension or revocation, and required completion of additional education. For unauthorized practice of law, the Oregon State Bar can pursue injunctive relief, and the harmed party can file a civil lawsuit for damages.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical risk. If an agent provides legal advice that turns out to be wrong — for example, assuring a seller that a particular easement "won't matter" — and the buyer discovers the issue post-closing, the agent's E&O policy faces a claim, the brokerage faces vicarious liability, and the transaction may unwind. Oregon courts have upheld rescission in cases where licensee misrepresentation materially affected a party's decision to close.

| Violation | Enforcing Body | Maximum Penalty |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Unauthorized practice of law | Oregon State Bar | Injunction + civil damages |
| Failure to disclose agency | OREA | $5,000 fine + license suspension |
| Improper earnest money handling | OREA / DFR | $5,000 fine + license revocation |
| Failure to supervise (broker) | OREA | $5,000 per violation + license action |
| Missed disclosure delivery deadline | Civil court (party lawsuit) | Rescission + actual damages |
