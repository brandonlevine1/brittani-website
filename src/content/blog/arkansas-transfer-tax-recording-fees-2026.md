## Understanding the Arkansas Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting a deed recorded in Arkansas requires more than a signed document and a trip to the circuit clerk. Agents who misquote fees or forget a required form risk delaying closings—or worse, leaving their client's ownership unprotected. The Arkansas transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by the same statutory framework that has been in place for years, but small fee adjustments and automation surcharges catch agents off guard every single cycle.

Arkansas imposes a real estate transfer tax under Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-101 et seq., administered at the county level by circuit clerks acting as collection agents for the state. The tax rate is $3.30 per $1,000 of actual consideration, with no scheduled increase for fiscal year 2026. Recording fees are separately authorized under Ark. Code Ann. 21-6-306, and counties have limited discretion to tack on automation fees per Ark. Code Ann. 21-6-307.

This post breaks down the exact dollar amounts you should be quoting on closing worksheets, the forms you must include, and the consequences of getting any of it wrong.

## Statutory Framework: Where the Numbers Come From

**The Arkansas real estate transfer tax is not a local option—it is a state-mandated levy collected at the county level.** Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-102 requires that the tax be paid before the circuit clerk will accept any instrument conveying title for recording. The current rate of $3.30 per $1,000 applies to the total consideration, not just the equity above any existing mortgage.

The revenue split is defined in Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-112: one-half goes to the State General Revenue Fund, and one-half stays in the county general fund. Because the county keeps a portion, circuit clerks have a direct financial incentive to reject improperly documented deeds rather than accept them and sort out discrepancies later.

Exemptions exist but are narrow. Transfers between spouses, transfers by operation of law (such as court-ordered sales), and transfers where no consideration changes hands are excluded under Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-103. Agents regularly miscategorize estate sales as exempt—they are not, unless no money or other value is exchanged.

## Fee Schedule Breakdown for 2026

Below is the combined fee schedule agents should reference when preparing net sheets or estimated closing costs. These amounts reflect the statutory rates plus the commonly assessed automation surcharge.

| Fee Type | First Page | Each Additional Page | Authority |
|----------|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Base Recording Fee | $15.00 | $5.00 | Ark. Code Ann. 21-6-306 |
| Courthouse Automation Fee | $2.00/page | $2.00/page | Ark. Code Ann. 21-6-307 |
| Real Estate Transfer Tax | $3.30 per $1,000 of consideration | — | Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-102 |
| Non-Standard Document Surcharge | $25.00 (flat) | — | County clerk discretion |

For a $250,000 sale with a standard two-page warranty deed, the combined recording and transfer tax obligation looks like this: $15 (first page) + $5 (second page) + $4 (automation, two pages) + $825 (transfer tax: $250,000 / $1,000 x $3.30) = $849 total. Agents who round to "$800-ish" on net sheets are shortchanging their clients' expectations by nearly $50.

Some counties—Pulaski and Benton among them—have adopted the full automation fee. Others have not. Always verify with the specific circuit clerk's office before quoting fees on a property outside your usual market area.

## Required Forms and the Recording Process

**Every deed submitted for recording in Arkansas must be accompanied by a completed Real Property Transfer Tax Declaration, commonly known as Form RPA.** This form is mandated by the Arkansas Department of Finance and Administration (DFA) and requires disclosure of the full purchase price, the legal description, and the names of both grantor and grantee. Without it, the clerk will reject the deed on sight.

Form RPA is not optional even when an exemption applies. If the transfer is exempt, the declarant marks the applicable exemption category on the form and still submits it. The DFA uses these declarations for revenue tracking and property valuation assessments across all 75 counties.

The deed itself must meet formatting requirements under Ark. Code Ann. 14-15-404: a minimum three-inch top margin on the first page for the clerk's recording stamp, the preparer's name and address, and a return address for the recorded instrument. Failing to meet margin requirements triggers the non-standard document surcharge of $25—a fee that has surprised more than one transaction coordinator mid-closing.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Arkansas Transfer Tax and Recording

Errors at this stage of a transaction tend to cascade. A rejected recording means the title company cannot issue a final policy, which means the lender cannot fund, which means the closing unwinds. Here are the five mistakes Arkansas agents make most often.

**First, agents estimate the transfer tax on net proceeds rather than gross consideration.** The statute is explicit: the tax applies to the total consideration paid, including any assumption of debt. A buyer assuming a $100,000 mortgage on a $300,000 purchase owes transfer tax on $300,000, not $200,000.

Second, agents forget to account for the automation fee when quoting recording costs on listing presentations or buyer consultations. The $2 per page adds up across multiple instruments—deed, deed of trust, assignment of rents—and the discrepancy erodes trust with clients who already feel nickel-and-dimed at closing.

Third, agents mark a transfer as exempt when consideration exists but is non-monetary. A property exchanged for services rendered, or as part of a 1031 exchange, still requires payment of the transfer tax on the fair market value of the consideration received. The DFA audits Form RPA declarations and can assess back taxes plus penalties under Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-108.

Fourth, agents submit deeds with legal descriptions that do not match the county assessor's records. The circuit clerk is not required to verify legal descriptions, but discrepancies between the deed and the Form RPA will trigger a rejection. Always pull the legal from the most recent recorded instrument in the chain of title.

Fifth, agents assume the title company handles everything and never verify that recording actually occurred. Arkansas has no statutory deadline by which a circuit clerk must return a recorded instrument. Deals have gone weeks without recording because a form was missing and nobody followed up. ## What Happens When Compliance Fails

The immediate consequence of an unpaid transfer tax is straightforward: the deed does not get recorded. But the downstream liability extends further than a delayed closing.

Under Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-108, the DFA can assess a penalty of 10% of the unpaid tax plus interest at 10% per annum if it determines that the tax was intentionally avoided. While this penalty targets the taxpayer (typically the seller), agents and brokers can face disciplinary action from the [Arkansas Real Estate Commission](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/) (AREC) if their conduct contributed to the violation—particularly if they advised a client that a transfer was exempt when it was not.

AREC Rule 10.14 requires licensees to exercise reasonable skill and care in all aspects of a transaction. Misadvising on transfer tax obligations or failing to ensure proper recording falls squarely within that duty. Sanctions range from a letter of reprimand to license suspension, depending on whether the error reflects negligence or a pattern of incompetence.

> "No instrument shall be accepted for recording unless accompanied by the prescribed tax declaration and the payment of all taxes due thereon." — Ark. Code Ann. 26-60-102(a)

Beyond regulatory exposure, an unrecorded deed creates a title gap. If the seller subsequently conveys the same property to a bona fide purchaser for value who records first, the original buyer loses under Arkansas's race-notice recording statute (Ark. Code Ann. 18-40-102). Agents who let recording slip through the cracks are handing their clients a potential ownership dispute.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under AREC Rule 10.9, which requires them to maintain adequate oversight of all transactions conducted by their agents. When it comes to transfer tax and recording, that obligation translates into specific audit points.

| Audit Item | What to Verify | Frequency |
|------------|---------------|-----------|
| Form RPA completion | All fields filled, exemption box matches facts | Every transaction |
| Transfer tax calculation | Tax matches gross consideration, not net | Every transaction |
| Recording confirmation | Circuit clerk's file stamp received | Within 10 business days post-closing |
| Fee accuracy on net sheets | Recording + automation fees match county schedule | Listing and buyer presentations |
| Non-standard surcharge disclosure | Client informed if deed exceeds formatting specs | As needed |

Brokers should not rely on title companies to catch agent errors upstream. The Form RPA requires the declarant's signature—often the seller or their authorized representative. If an agent prepares the form incorrectly and the seller signs it based on the agent's guidance, the broker's office is implicated in any subsequent DFA audit.

A quarterly file review that specifically checks transfer tax math against closing statements will catch most discrepancies before they become enforcement issues. ## Practical Tips for Accurate Fee Quoting

Start every listing presentation and buyer consultation with the county-specific fee schedule pulled directly from the circuit clerk's website or a recent closing in that jurisdiction. Do not rely on MLS tax records for the consideration amount—use the contract price.

When preparing estimated net sheets, break out each component separately: base recording fee, automation fee (if applicable), transfer tax, and any non-standard surcharge. Clients appreciate transparency, and itemization protects you if a dispute arises later about what was disclosed.

Keep a running spreadsheet of each county's automation fee status. Arkansas has 75 counties, and not all of them have adopted the surcharge.
