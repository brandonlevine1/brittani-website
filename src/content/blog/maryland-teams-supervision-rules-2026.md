## Why Maryland Teams & Supervision Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) has sharpened its enforcement posture on team structures heading into 2026, and agents who treat team formation as a casual branding exercise are exposed to real liability. Understanding Maryland teams & supervision rules 2026 is no longer optional reading—it is the difference between a smoothly operating group and a disciplinary hearing. The regulatory framework sits across the Business Occupations & Professions Article Title 17, the Code of Maryland Regulations (COMAR) Title 09, Subtitle 11, and a handful of mandatory disclosure forms that teams routinely mishandle.

Maryland's approach to team regulation differs meaningfully from neighboring states like Virginia and the District of Columbia. The Commission does not issue separate team licenses. Instead, every team operates under the umbrella of a licensed brokerage, and the supervising broker assumes personal accountability for every transaction the team touches.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Teams

Maryland's real estate licensing authority derives from the Business Occupations & Professions Article, Title 17 of the Annotated Code of Maryland. Sections 17-322 through 17-325 address advertising standards, which directly control how teams present themselves to the public. COMAR 09.11.02 fills in the operational details—setting out supervision requirements, recordkeeping mandates, and the broker's duty of reasonable oversight.

**There is no standalone "team license" in Maryland.** Every salesperson and associate broker must affiliate with a licensed real estate broker, and any team they form operates as a subset of that brokerage. The Commission's [guidance documents](https://www.dllr.state.maryland.gov/license/mrec/) reinforce that team names are treated as advertising and must comply with all rules applicable to brokerage advertising under §17-322.

The practical implication: if your team name appears on a yard sign, a website, or a social media ad without the brokerage name displayed in a reasonably prominent manner, you are already in violation. MREC auditors have been citing this with increasing frequency since late 2024.

## Advertising Rules for Maryland Teams

COMAR 09.11.02.06 requires that all advertising by a licensee include the name of the broker with whom the licensee is affiliated. For teams, this means every piece of marketing—digital or print—must display the brokerage name in a manner that is not misleading to consumers. The team name cannot be presented in a way that implies it is a separate brokerage entity.

| Requirement | Compliant Example | Non-Compliant Example |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name displayed | "The Smith Group at ABC Realty" | "The Smith Group" (no brokerage) |
| Font size/prominence | Team name and brokerage in comparable font | Brokerage in tiny footer text only |
| Use of "Realty" or "Properties" in team name | Permitted if brokerage name also shown | Creates confusion if brokerage absent |
| Social media profiles | Bio includes full brokerage name | Handle is @SmithRealty with no brokerage mention |
| Email signatures | Brokerage name clearly listed | Only team name and personal phone |

The $5,000 maximum fine per violation under §17-322 may seem manageable in isolation, but MREC treats each advertisement as a separate occurrence. A single Facebook campaign running noncompliant creative across dozens of ad sets can generate exposure well into five figures before anyone flags it.

## Broker Supervision Duties Under Title 17

Section 17-532 of the Business Occupations & Professions Article establishes the supervising broker's duty to "exercise reasonable and adequate supervision" over every licensee affiliated with the brokerage. This duty does not diminish because a team leader handles day-to-day management. The broker cannot delegate statutory supervisory responsibility to a team leader who holds only a salesperson license.

**Brokers who rely on team leaders to self-police compliance are building liability on sand.** The Commission has repeatedly held that a broker's failure to review team advertising, audit transaction files, and confirm proper use of disclosure forms constitutes a breach of the supervision duty—even when the broker had no actual knowledge of the violation.

COMAR 09.11.02.01B states that brokers must maintain policies, procedures, and systems adequate to supervise all affiliated licensees. For teams, this means written protocols covering transaction file review timelines, advertising approval workflows, and [trust account procedures](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) that prevent commingling when team members handle earnest money.

## Required Forms and Disclosure Obligations

Maryland teams must ensure proper use of several state-mandated forms during every transaction. The most consequential for team operations include:

The Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form must be presented at first substantive contact with a consumer. When a team member—not the team leader—makes that first contact, the disclosing agent's name and license number must appear on the form. Errors here create agency confusion that can unwind deals.

The Residential Property Condition Disclosure Statement (form available through MREC's standard form library) must be delivered by the listing agent. If a team's listing coordinator or showing agent handles delivery, the supervising broker must confirm the form was properly completed by the seller and timely provided to the buyer's side.

| Form / Document | Who Delivers | Team-Specific Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding Whom RE Agents Represent | First agent with substantive contact | Wrong team member's info listed |
| Residential Property Condition Disclosure | Listing agent | Delegated to unlicensed team assistant |
| Contract of Sale (MAR/GCAAR forms) | Agent preparing offer | Team leader signs but wasn't involved |
| Dual Agency Consent | Both agents if in-house | Intra-team dual agency undisclosed |
| Lead Paint Disclosure (pre-1978) | Listing agent | Omitted by team showing specialist |

Dual agency deserves special attention for teams. When a buyer contacts one team member about a listing held by another member of the same team, dual agency or intra-company agency issues arise under §17-530. The team must obtain written informed consent before proceeding, and the [disclosure timing matters more than agents realize](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Common Mistakes Maryland Teams Make

**First, teams routinely fail to update advertising when a member leaves.** When a salesperson departs, every sign, website page, Zillow profile, and printed brochure featuring that person must be corrected. MREC considers continued display of a departed licensee's name as misleading advertising.

Second, unlicensed team assistants perform licensed activities. Maryland law is explicit: showing property, negotiating terms, or providing opinions on value requires a license. Teams that allow administrative assistants to host open houses solo, answer pricing questions, or schedule inspections without a licensee present are exposing the broker to disciplinary action and potentially voiding contractual protections.

Third, teams treat intra-team transactions as single-agent representation when dual agency rules apply. If your team lists a property and another team member brings the buyer, you have a dual agency situation under Maryland law. Failing to disclose this properly is a violation of §17-530 and gives either party grounds to rescind the contract.

Fourth, trust account handling gets sloppy when team members collect earnest money checks. All funds must be deposited into the broker's trust account within the timelines prescribed by COMAR 09.11.02.13. Team members who hold checks "until the team leader gets back" are creating escrow violations that the Commission takes seriously.

Fifth, teams fail to maintain transaction records in the format and duration required. COMAR 09.11.02.01 mandates that brokers retain all transaction documents for a minimum of five years. Teams that maintain their own filing systems separate from the brokerage create gaps that surface during audits.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers with affiliated teams should conduct quarterly audits covering at minimum five areas: advertising compliance, form completion accuracy, trust account deposits, team roster accuracy with MREC, and dual agency disclosure logs.

An advertising audit means reviewing every active platform where the team name appears—not just the team's main website but also individual agent profiles, third-party portals, social accounts, and print materials. The broker should maintain a dated log of each review.

Trust account audits for teams require verifying that every earnest money deposit traces back to a specific transaction file and was deposited within the regulatory window. Brokers should also confirm that team members are not maintaining separate or shadow accounts for holding client funds, which is an automatic license revocation scenario.

**Roster accuracy is a sleeper issue that catches brokers off guard during MREC audits.** When team members transfer brokerages or let licenses lapse, the broker must confirm termination with the Commission within the required timeframe. A team operating with an expired or improperly affiliated licensee creates liability for every transaction that agent touched during the lapse period.

For [transaction coordination workflows](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator), brokers should verify that the team's internal processes produce a complete file for every deal—including evidence of timely disclosures, signed agency forms, and compliance with all contractual deadlines.

## Penalties and Enforcement Actions

MREC's disciplinary authority extends to fines, license suspension, license revocation, and required continuing education. For team-related violations, the most common enforcement actions fall into three categories:

Advertising violations under §17-322 carry fines up to $5,000 per occurrence. Repeated violations can trigger suspension proceedings. Supervision failures under §17-532 expose the broker to personal disciplinary action, including potential revocation if the Commission finds a pattern of neglect.

Consumer harm resulting from undisclosed dual agency or form errors can lead to contract rescission, which means the deal dies and both parties may pursue civil damages against the agents and broker. The Commission's ability to refer matters to the Attorney General for prosecution adds a criminal dimension in cases involving trust fund misappropriation.

| Violation Type | Potential Penalty | Who Faces Action |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising without brokerage name | Up to $5,000/occurrence | Team leader + broker |
| Unlicensed activity by assistant | License suspension or revocation | Broker primarily |
| Undisclosed dual agency | Contract rescission + fine | Both agents + broker |
| Trust account violation | Revocation + AG referral | Broker + depositing agent |
| Failure to maintain records | Fine + mandatory CE | Broker |
