## The Mandate Behind Missouri Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

Missouri buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 are not optional guidance—they are enforceable mandates backed by state statute, MLS policy, and the ripple effects of the National Association of REALTORS settlement that reshaped buyer representation nationwide. Every agent operating in the state must secure a written, signed buyer representation agreement before providing substantive real estate services, including property tours. The Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC) has made clear through its published guidance that compliance is not aspirational; it is a condition of continued licensure.

The statutory backbone sits in Chapter 339 of the Missouri Revised Statutes, specifically RSMo 339.100, which grants MREC broad authority to discipline licensees who fail to establish proper agency relationships. Coupled with the [NAR settlement practice changes](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) effective August 17, 2024, Missouri agents face a dual-enforcement reality: state regulators and their local MLS each carry independent penalty authority. Understanding both layers—and the specific forms involved—is the difference between a compliant practice and a suspended license.

## Which Forms Missouri Agents Must Use

**Missouri REALTORS (the state association) publishes the standard forms that most brokerages adopt, and Form 135—Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement—is the primary document agents need to master.** Form 135 establishes the scope of representation, the duration of the relationship, the compensation structure, and the duties owed by the agent to the buyer. It also includes required disclosures about how compensation may be sourced, whether from the seller, the listing broker, or the buyer directly.

For agents who prefer a non-exclusive arrangement, Form 136—Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement—exists as an alternative. The critical distinction: Form 136 allows the buyer to work with multiple agents simultaneously, which introduces compensation disputes if the buyer purchases a property introduced by another licensee. Both forms require a definite termination date, a clear compensation clause, and signatures from all parties before services begin.

| Form Number | Form Name | Relationship Type | Key Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form 135 | Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Exclusive | Standard buyer-agent engagement |
| Form 136 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Non-exclusive | Buyers wanting multiple agent relationships |
| Form 131 | Agency Disclosure | Disclosure only | Initial agency relationship explanation |
| Form 137 | Buyer Representation Amendment | Amendment | Modifying terms mid-relationship |

Form 131—the Broker Disclosure to Seller and Buyer—remains a separate but related obligation. Missouri law under RSMo 339.750 requires that agents disclose their agency relationship at the earliest meaningful contact. Form 131 is not a substitute for Form 135 or 136; it is the disclosure layer, while the buyer agreement is the contractual layer. Agents who confuse these two documents create liability gaps that surface at closing.

## Timing Requirements: When the Agreement Must Be Signed

The most consequential timing rule is straightforward: the buyer representation agreement must be executed before the agent provides any touring, negotiating, or advisory services. This aligns with MLS policies adopted by MARIS (the Mid-America Regional Information Systems), which serves the majority of Missouri's licensed agents. MARIS policy explicitly prohibits agents from showing MLS-listed properties to a buyer who has not signed a written representation agreement.

**Failing to secure the agreement before a showing is not a paperwork technicality—it is a compliance violation with real consequences.** MARIS can impose fines starting at $500 for a first offense and escalating to suspension of MLS access for repeat violations. Simultaneously, MREC retains authority under RSMo 339.100(7) to pursue disciplinary action for "failing to account for or to remit any moneys coming into the licensee's possession which belong to others" when compensation disputes arise from unsigned agreements.

The practical implication: open houses present a gray area. If a buyer walks into your open house and asks you to write an offer on the spot, you must have a signed buyer representation agreement before drafting that offer. Some brokerages now keep blank Form 135 copies at every open house for exactly this reason.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance triggers a cascade of consequences that extend beyond a slap on the wrist. MREC's disciplinary process, governed by RSMo 339.100, allows the commission to suspend or revoke a license, impose civil penalties, or require additional education. The commission publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, meaning a violation becomes part of your public record.

At the MLS level, MARIS treats buyer agreement violations as a conduct infraction under its Rules and Regulations. Fines, mandatory education, and temporary suspension of showing privileges are all within MARIS's enforcement toolkit. Beyond institutional penalties, agents face civil liability from buyers who claim they were not properly represented or from competing agents who argue they are owed the buyer-side compensation.

Deal cancellation is also on the table. If a seller's attorney discovers that the buyer's agent operated without a signed agreement, they may challenge the agent's standing to receive compensation—potentially unwinding the commission split at the closing table. Brokers who have paid out commission from their own accounts based on an unsigned agreement have limited recourse to recover those funds.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make

**The first and most frequent error is backdating the agreement.** Agents who show a property and then ask the buyer to sign Form 135 after the fact—sometimes dating it to before the showing—are committing a documentation fraud that MREC investigators specifically look for during audits. Timestamps on MLS showing confirmations do not lie, and a backdated form creates a paper trail that proves the violation rather than concealing it.

The second mistake is using verbal agreements as a placeholder. Missouri law does not recognize oral buyer representation agreements as sufficient under the post-settlement framework. An agent who says "we'll get the paperwork done later" has no enforceable claim to compensation and no documented agency relationship protecting the buyer.

Third, agents frequently fail to specify compensation with adequate clarity. Form 135 requires that the buyer-side compensation be stated as either a percentage, a flat fee, or a formula. Writing "TBD" or "per MLS" is insufficient under current MARIS rules because MLS cooperative compensation fields no longer guarantee an offer of buyer-agent payment. The buyer must understand—before signing—what they may owe if the seller offers nothing.

Fourth, some agents neglect to provide Form 131 (the agency disclosure) alongside the buyer agreement. These are two separate obligations under two separate statutes. Delivering Form 135 without Form 131 satisfies the contractual requirement but violates the disclosure requirement, leaving the agent exposed to a RSMo 339.750 violation.

Fifth, agents operating across state lines—particularly in the Kansas City metro—sometimes use Kansas forms for Missouri transactions or vice versa. Missouri requires Missouri-specific forms that comply with Missouri statute. A Kansas buyer representation agreement does not satisfy MREC or MARIS requirements, regardless of where the agent is physically located when the buyer signs. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under RSMo 339.160, which establishes broker responsibility for supervising affiliated licensees. A broker who discovers that an agent has been showing properties without signed agreements—and does nothing—becomes a co-respondent in any disciplinary proceeding.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signed Form 135/136 on file | Every transaction | Signature date vs. first showing date | MLS fine, MREC discipline |
| Form 131 delivered | Every new client contact | Signed copy in file before substantive services | RSMo 339.750 violation |
| Compensation clarity | Every agreement | Specific dollar or percentage stated | Compensation dispute at closing |
| Expiration date present | Every agreement | Definite date, not open-ended | Unenforceability of contract |
| Amendment documentation | As needed | Form 137 when terms change | Unauthorized practice claims |

Brokers should implement a file-review checkpoint that prevents any showing request from being processed through the brokerage's systems until a signed buyer agreement has been uploaded. Many transaction management platforms now support this gating function. The brokerage's policy manual should also specify the maximum agreement duration the firm permits—[avoiding the deadline-related pitfalls](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that plague agents who let agreements auto-renew without client re-engagement.

Quarterly audits of a random sample of transaction files—checking that Form 135 dates precede MLS showing confirmations—provide a defensible record that the broker exercised supervisory responsibility. Document these audits in writing and retain them for at least five years, consistent with MREC's record-retention expectations.

## Compensation Disclosure and the Post-Settlement Reality

The NAR settlement fundamentally changed how buyer-side compensation flows in Missouri. Sellers are no longer required to offer cooperative compensation through the MLS, which means the buyer agreement's compensation clause has become the single most important financial term in the document. Agents must disclose that the buyer may be personally responsible for their agent's fee if no seller-side offer materializes.

Form 135's compensation section now carries language permitting the agent to seek payment from the seller, the listing broker, or the buyer—in a specified priority order. Agents who fail to discuss this hierarchy with their clients before signing invite post-closing disputes and potential MREC complaints alleging inadequate disclosure. The conversation is uncomfortable but mandatory.

Missouri brokerages that [track where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) report that compensation misunderstandings are now among the top three reasons buyers terminate their agent relationship mid-transaction. Clear, upfront communication about money—documented in the signed agreement—eliminates most of these conflicts before they escalate.
