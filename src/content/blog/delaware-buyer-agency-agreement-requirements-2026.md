## Understanding Delaware Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The rules around buyer representation in Delaware have tightened significantly since the NAR settlement reshaped how agents and brokers handle compensation and agency relationships. **Delaware buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 now demand a higher level of documentation, transparency, and procedural discipline than most agents practiced even two years ago.** The Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC), operating under the authority of 24 Del. C. Chapter 29, has made clear that written buyer agency agreements are not optional courtesies — they are regulatory expectations.

Delaware is a small state, but its real estate market spans everything from Wilmington urban condos to Sussex County beach properties, and the regulatory framework applies uniformly. Agents who treat the buyer agency agreement as a formality rather than a binding legal instrument are exposing themselves and their brokerages to real liability. This post breaks down exactly what the law requires, which forms to use, where agents are getting tripped up, and what brokers need to do to keep their houses in order.

## The Legal Foundation: 24 Del. C. Chapter 29 and DREC Rules

Delaware's real estate licensing statute, [24 Del. C. Chapter 29](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title24/c029/index.html), establishes the Delaware Real Estate Commission as the regulatory body overseeing all licensee conduct. Section 2912 outlines the grounds for disciplinary action, which include failure to properly disclose agency relationships and failure to act in accordance with a written agreement. DREC's administrative rules further specify that agents must clarify their agency role — buyer's agent, seller's agent, or transaction licensee — before providing services.

The NAR settlement, finalized in 2024, added a federal overlay to these state requirements. Under the settlement terms, MLS participants can no longer make blanket offers of buyer-agent compensation through the MLS, and buyer agents must have a written agreement with their client before touring a property listed on an MLS. Delaware agents who are Realtor members are bound by both DREC rules and NAR's updated policies, which means the written buyer agency agreement is now a dual mandate.

**DREC has not adopted a specific regulatory form, but it does require that any buyer agency agreement contain certain elements.** Those elements include the scope of services, the duration of the agreement, the compensation structure, and a clear explanation of the agent's fiduciary duties. Omitting any of these creates a compliance gap that DREC can cite in a disciplinary proceeding.

## Which Forms Delaware Agents Actually Use

The Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR) publishes the forms that most brokerages in the state adopt as standard practice. For buyer agency, the primary document is DAR Form 601, the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement. This form covers the essential elements DREC expects: identification of the parties, the geographic or property-type scope of the agreement, the term with a specific expiration date, and the compensation terms.

DAR also offers Form 602, the Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement, which allows the buyer to work with multiple agents simultaneously. In practice, most brokerages push for Form 601 because it provides clearer protection for the agent's compensation claim. Form 602 is more common in commercial-adjacent residential deals or situations where a buyer is relocating and working with agents in multiple areas.

A third document agents should know is DAR Form 301, the Agency Disclosure form, which is separate from but closely related to the buyer agency agreement. Form 301 must be presented at first substantive contact to disclose the agent's role. Agents sometimes confuse signing Form 301 with having a buyer agency agreement in place — they are not interchangeable.

| Form | Name | Purpose | When Required |
|------|------|---------|---------------|
| DAR Form 601 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Establishes exclusive representation and compensation terms | Before substantive services, including showings |
| DAR Form 602 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Allows buyer to work with multiple agents | Before substantive services if non-exclusive arrangement is chosen |
| DAR Form 301 | Agency Disclosure | Discloses agent's role in the transaction | At first substantive contact with any party |

Getting the sequencing right matters. Form 301 comes first — it tells the buyer who you represent. Then Form 601 or 602 formalizes the relationship. Skipping or reversing this order is one of the most common audit findings brokers report.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**The consequences of operating without a proper buyer agency agreement in Delaware range from uncomfortable to career-ending.** DREC has authority under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 to impose fines, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely. Disciplinary actions are public record and appear on an agent's license history, which means even a reprimand can affect future brokerage affiliations and client trust.

Beyond DREC sanctions, an agent without a signed buyer agency agreement has a much weaker position in any compensation dispute. If a buyer walks away or claims they never agreed to pay the agent, the absence of a written agreement means the agent has limited legal recourse. Delaware courts apply standard contract principles, and without a signed document specifying the terms, the agent is left arguing implied agreements — a difficult position in litigation.

There is also the MLS enforcement layer. Delaware MLSs, including the Bright MLS system that covers the state, have adopted compliance policies aligned with the NAR settlement. Agents who schedule showings or submit offers through Bright MLS without a buyer agency agreement on file risk MLS-level penalties, including fines and suspension of MLS access. Losing MLS access effectively shuts down an agent's ability to practice.

| Violation | Potential Consequence | Enforcing Body |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------|
| No signed buyer agency agreement before showing | Disciplinary action, fine up to $5,000 per violation | DREC |
| Missing agency disclosure (Form 301) | License suspension or revocation | DREC |
| Scheduling MLS showings without written agreement | MLS fine, suspension of MLS privileges | Bright MLS |
| Misrepresenting compensation terms in agreement | Civil liability, DREC discipline | DREC / Delaware courts |
| Failure to provide copy of signed agreement to buyer | DREC fine, audit citation | DREC |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Buyer Agency Agreements

Even experienced agents stumble on buyer agency compliance, and the post-settlement enforcement environment has made the margin for error razor-thin. Here are the specific mistakes that keep showing up in DREC complaints and brokerage audits.

**First, agents present the agreement at the wrong time.** The rule is clear: the buyer agency agreement must be signed before the agent provides substantive real estate services. That includes touring properties, providing CMAs tailored to a buyer's search, or negotiating on a buyer's behalf. Agents who wait until an offer is being written are already out of compliance. The agreement must precede the showing, not the contract.

Second, agents leave the compensation section vague or blank. Post-settlement, the buyer agency agreement must specify exactly how the agent will be compensated — whether by the buyer directly, through a seller concession, or a combination. Writing "to be determined" or leaving the field open violates both the spirit and the letter of the NAR settlement requirements and gives DREC grounds for a disciplinary finding. The number must be specific: a flat fee, a percentage, or a clearly defined range with a cap.

Third, agents fail to set a reasonable and specific expiration date. DREC expects every buyer agency agreement to include a defined term with an end date. Agreements that run indefinitely or for unreasonably long periods — 18 months, for instance — can be challenged as unconscionable. Most brokerages in Delaware default to terms between 90 and 180 days, which DREC has generally viewed as reasonable.

Fourth, agents forget to provide the buyer with a signed copy. This sounds trivial, but it is a frequent audit finding. Under Delaware contract principles and DREC administrative rules, the buyer must receive a fully executed copy of the agreement at the time of signing. Agents who collect signatures on a tablet and never email the completed document are creating a documentation gap that [undermines the entire compliance chain](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Fifth, agents confuse the agency disclosure with the buyer agency agreement. Form 301 and Form 601 serve different functions. Presenting Form 301 alone does not satisfy the requirement for a written buyer agency agreement. Agents who conflate the two documents often discover the error only when a compensation dispute arises or a broker conducts a file audit.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under 24 Del. C. Section 2922, which means an agent's compliance failure is also the broker's problem. DREC can and does hold brokers accountable when agents under their supervision operate without proper buyer agency agreements. The days of assuming agents handle their own paperwork are over.

**Every brokerage in Delaware should be auditing transaction files for three specific documents in this order: Form 301 (agency disclosure), Form 601 or 602 (buyer agency agreement), and proof of delivery to the buyer.** If any of these are missing or out of sequence, the file is non-compliant. Brokers who conduct quarterly file audits — rather than waiting for a DREC complaint — catch issues before they become disciplinary matters.

Brokers should also verify that compensation terms in the buyer agency agreement match what actually gets paid at closing. Discrepancies between the agreement and the settlement statement create liability for the brokerage and can trigger a DREC investigation. This is especially important in deals where the seller is offering a concession toward buyer-agent compensation, because the agreement must reflect that structure accurately.

Training is the other half of the equation. Brokers who hold annual compliance meetings but never address buyer agency agreement procedures specifically are leaving a gap. The [same operational blind spots that cause agents to miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) also cause them to fumble documentation requirements. Quarterly training sessions focused specifically on agency forms, timing, and compensation disclosure will reduce errors more than any annual CE course.

## Compensation Transparency After the NAR Settlement

The NAR settlement fundamentally changed how buyer-agent compensation works in Delaware, even though Delaware law itself did not change. Sellers can still offer concessions, and buyers can still agree to pay their agent directly. What changed is the disclosure and documentation requirement around those arrangements.

Under the settlement terms, buyer agents cannot receive compensation that exceeds what is specified in their buyer agency agreement. If Form 601 states the agent's fee is 2.5% and the seller offers a 3% concession, the agent can only collect 2.5% unless the agreement is amended. This creates a new documentation step that agents frequently overlook, especially in competitive markets where seller concessions vary from listing to listing.

Buyers must also be informed, in writing, that compensation is negotiable. This language appears in the current version of DAR Form 601, but agents need to actually discuss it rather than treating it as boilerplate. DREC has signaled through informal guidance that agents who rush through the compensation discussion without ensuring buyer understanding are at risk of a [misrepresentation finding similar to issues flagged in other states' agency disclosure rules](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026).

This three-step sequence should be a non-negotiable part of every buyer onboarding workflow.
