Wire fraud remains one of the fastest-growing threats in residential real estate, and North Dakota is not immune. Understanding **North Dakota wire fraud prevention requirements 2026** is no longer optional for agents who want to protect their clients, their brokerage, and their license. The stakes are severe: the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported over $145 million in real estate wire fraud losses nationally in 2023, and the trend line has not reversed.

## Why North Dakota Agents Face Increased Scrutiny in 2026

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC) has sharpened its focus on consumer protection in financial transactions. Under [NDCC Chapter 43-23](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t43c23.pdf), the state's primary real estate licensing statute, agents owe fiduciary duties that include reasonable care and full disclosure of material risks. Wire fraud falls squarely within that obligation.

**The NDREC does not treat wire fraud warnings as a courtesy — it treats them as a fiduciary duty.** The commission's guidance, updated in late 2025, clarified that agents who fail to advise clients about wire fraud risks may face disciplinary review under NDCC Section 43-23-11.1, which covers dishonest dealing, negligence, and incompetence. This mirrors a broader national trend where state regulators are folding wire fraud prevention into their existing licensing frameworks rather than passing standalone statutes.

North Dakota's relatively small market — roughly 12,000 residential transactions per year — sometimes gives agents a false sense of security. Fraudsters do not skip rural markets. In fact, smaller brokerages with less formal compliance infrastructure can be easier targets.

## The Forms and Disclosures North Dakota Agents Must Use

North Dakota does not have a single state-mandated wire fraud disclosure form with a fixed form number like some states issue. Instead, the NDREC expects brokerages to implement written wire fraud advisories that meet the standard of care under NDCC Chapter 43-23. Most brokerages accomplish this by adopting one of several widely accepted formats.

The most commonly used document is the **Wire Fraud Advisory**, often adapted from the National Association of Realtors template or created by the brokerage's legal counsel. The North Dakota Association of Realtors (NDAR) distributes a recommended advisory form to member brokerages, typically included in its annual forms package. Agents should confirm with their managing broker which version is approved for their office.

When completing the advisory, agents should include it alongside other transaction disclosures — typically at the same time as the Agency Disclosure (NDREC Form 1) and the Property Condition Disclosure Statement. Timing matters here. Delivering the wire fraud advisory at the offer stage, rather than days before closing, gives clients maximum time to internalize the warnings and establish verification procedures with their lender and title company.

## North Dakota Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Specific Compliance Steps

The compliance obligations break down into distinct duties for agents, brokers, and closing agents. The table below outlines who is responsible for what in a typical North Dakota transaction.

| Responsibility | Buyer's Agent | Listing Agent | Closing/Title Agent | Broker |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deliver written wire fraud advisory | Yes | Yes | Yes | Audit compliance |
| Verify wiring instructions by phone | Recommend to client | Recommend to client | Yes — direct duty | Enforce protocols |
| Report suspected fraud to FBI IC3 | If discovered | If discovered | Yes — immediate | Yes — immediate |
| Retain proof of advisory delivery | Yes — in transaction file | Yes — in transaction file | Yes — in closing file | Audit retention |
| Train agents on fraud red flags | N/A | N/A | N/A | Yes — annual requirement |

**Every agent in the transaction should deliver the advisory independently.** Relying on the title company to handle it alone creates a gap. If the title company's email is compromised — a common attack vector — the buyer may never receive the warning, and the agent who failed to provide a separate copy faces liability exposure.

The practical steps are straightforward: deliver the written advisory, document delivery with a signed acknowledgment or email confirmation, verbally explain the risks during your first meeting with the client, and include a copy of the signed advisory in your transaction file. These steps directly address the standard of care the NDREC evaluates during complaints and audits.

## Common Mistakes North Dakota Agents Make

Five specific errors show up repeatedly in complaints, E&O claims, and brokerage audits. Avoiding them is the simplest way to stay compliant.

**First, agents deliver the advisory too late.** Handing a wire fraud warning to a buyer three days before closing — after the buyer has already received and possibly acted on wiring instructions — defeats the purpose. The advisory should go out with or before the purchase agreement, not as an afterthought stapled to the closing package.

Second, agents rely on email alone to transmit the advisory. Email is the primary attack vector for wire fraud. Sending the advisory exclusively by email, without a verbal conversation and an in-person or phone-based explanation, creates irony and risk in equal measure. The best practice is to discuss wire fraud verbally at the buyer consultation, then follow up with the written document.

Third, agents fail to document delivery. A signed acknowledgment or a time-stamped email with the advisory attached is the minimum. Without documentation, an agent's claim that they warned the client becomes a credibility contest during a complaint proceeding — and the NDREC tends to side with the consumer when documentation is absent.

Fourth, agents forward unverified wiring instructions. This is the most dangerous mistake. If a client receives wiring instructions by email and forwards them to the agent for confirmation, the agent must never simply reply "looks good." The only acceptable response is to direct the client to verify the instructions by calling the title company or attorney at a phone number the client already has on file — not a number included in the suspicious email.

Fifth, agents assume the title company handles everything. Title companies carry their own obligations, but the NDREC holds licensees to an independent standard of care. A title company's compliance does not absolve an agent of their own duty to warn and document. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in North Dakota carry supervisory obligations under NDCC Section 43-23-11.1 and the administrative rules promulgated by the NDREC. Those obligations extend to wire fraud compliance.

**Brokers should audit transaction files quarterly for three specific items.** First, confirm that every closed file contains a signed or acknowledged wire fraud advisory. Second, verify that the advisory was delivered at or before the purchase agreement stage, not at closing. Third, check that agents are not forwarding wiring instructions on behalf of title companies or lenders — a practice that creates unnecessary liability exposure for the brokerage.

Beyond audits, brokers must provide annual training on wire fraud prevention. The NDREC's continuing education requirements under NDAC Section 70-02-04 allow wire fraud training to count toward the 15-hour CE cycle, and several approved providers now offer North Dakota-specific modules. Brokers who document this training create a strong defense in the event of a complaint or lawsuit.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required | Risk if Skipped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wire fraud advisory in file | Every transaction | Signed copy or email acknowledgment | Supervisory liability under NDCC 43-23-11.1 |
| Timing of advisory delivery | Quarterly sample | Date comparison: advisory vs. purchase agreement | Late delivery = potential negligence claim |
| Agent forwarding of wire instructions | Ongoing monitoring | Email review or policy attestation | Brokerage named in fraud loss lawsuit |
| Annual wire fraud training | Once per year minimum | CE certificate or internal training log | Weakened defense in regulatory proceedings |

Brokers who skip these steps expose their entire firm. Under North Dakota's licensing statute, the commission can sanction a brokerage — not just an individual agent — for supervisory failures. Fines, mandatory corrective plans, and license suspension are all on the table.

## Liability, Fines, and Deal Fallout When Compliance Fails

The consequences of ignoring wire fraud prevention requirements split into three categories: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

On the regulatory side, the NDREC can impose fines up to $2,000 per violation under NDCC Section 43-23-11.1, suspend or revoke a license, and require additional education. Repeat violations or cases involving actual client losses tend to result in the harshest penalties.

Civil liability is often more financially devastating than regulatory fines. If a buyer wires $200,000 to a fraudulent account and the agent failed to deliver a wire fraud advisory, the agent and brokerage become targets in a negligence lawsuit. E&O insurance policies increasingly contain exclusions or sub-limits for wire fraud claims, meaning agents may face personal financial exposure. Some carriers now require proof of wire fraud advisory delivery before they will even process a claim.

Transactionally, a wire fraud incident can kill a deal outright. When funds are misdirected, recovery rates are low — the FBI estimates that only about 29 percent of fraudulently wired funds are recovered, and the process takes months. Buyers may not have replacement funds, sellers lose patience, and the deal collapses. The commission earned on that transaction disappears, and the reputational damage to the agent lingers.
