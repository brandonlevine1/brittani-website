## Understanding Idaho Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The rules around buyer representation in Idaho have shifted significantly since the NAR settlement took effect in August 2024, and the Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) has spent the intervening months clarifying how those changes apply to licensees statewide. If you hold an active Idaho license in 2026, you need to know the specific Idaho buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 brings into play—not just the spirit of the rules, but the exact forms, deadlines, and enforcement mechanisms that govern your daily practice. Getting this wrong carries real consequences: fines, license discipline, and deal-killing liability claims.

This post breaks down what IREC expects, which forms satisfy the requirement, what brokers should be auditing, and where agents most commonly trip up.

## The Statutory and Regulatory Foundation

Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20 establishes the licensing framework, while IREC's administrative rules under IDAPA 24.33.01 set the conduct standards that apply to buyer representation. Section 54-2038 specifically addresses the fiduciary duties a licensee owes to clients—duties that only attach once a written agreement is executed. Without that signed document, you are not legally functioning as the buyer's agent under Idaho law, regardless of what you verbally agreed to in the car on the way to a showing.

**IREC's 2025 guidance bulletin (Bulletin 2025-03) made it explicit: no written agreement, no agency relationship, no commission entitlement.** The bulletin reinforced that Idaho follows the NAR settlement's prohibition on offers of buyer-agent compensation through the MLS, meaning your right to be paid now flows exclusively through the buyer agreement or separate negotiation at the offer stage.

The [Idaho Real Estate Commission's official website](https://irec.idaho.gov) publishes all current bulletins, and agents should bookmark it for updates that may drop before the 2026 license renewal cycle.

## Which Forms Satisfy the Requirement

Idaho REALTORS (the state association) publishes the forms most licensees rely on. Here is how the primary buyer representation forms break down:

| Form Number | Form Name | Use Case | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| RE-14 | Buyer Representation Agreement | Non-exclusive representation | Buyer can work with multiple agents simultaneously |
| RE-14A | Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Exclusive representation | One agent/brokerage for a defined term |
| RE-14B | Buyer Touring Agreement | Pre-showing, limited scope | Covers touring only; does not establish full agency |
| RE-46 | Compensation Agreement (Buyer-Broker) | Compensation disclosure | Specifies exact fee buyer owes agent |

**The RE-14A is the most common form used in practice, but agents should not default to it without explaining the exclusivity implications to the buyer.** IREC treats failure to explain material terms as a potential violation of Idaho Code 54-2054(1), which prohibits dishonest dealing.

Non-REALTOR licensees are not required to use Idaho REALTORS forms, but whatever template they use must contain the elements IREC identifies as mandatory: a definite termination date, a clear statement of compensation terms, a description of services to be provided, and the signatures of all parties. Missing any one of these elements can render the agreement unenforceable—and leave you working for free.

## Timing: When the Agreement Must Be Signed

The timing question trips up more agents than any substantive clause. Under the current framework influenced by the NAR settlement and reinforced by IREC Bulletin 2025-03, the buyer representation agreement must be executed before you provide any brokerage services. That includes showing property, writing offers, providing CMAs tailored to a specific property, or attending inspections on the buyer's behalf.

The one narrow exception is the initial consultation or interview where you explain your services and present the agreement for signature. You can have that conversation without a signed agreement. But the moment the buyer says "let's go look at that house on Warm Springs Avenue," you need ink on paper.

Open houses present a gray area that IREC addressed directly. If you are hosting an open house as the listing agent, you do not need a buyer agreement from visitors. But if a buyer contacts you after an open house and asks you to represent them, the agreement must be signed before your next interaction that constitutes brokerage services. Similar timing rules apply in other states—agents working across borders may want to review [how Wisconsin handles the same requirement](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) for comparison.

## What Happens If You Fail to Comply

The consequences of operating without a properly executed buyer agency agreement fall into three categories, and none of them are pleasant.

**Regulatory discipline from IREC can include a formal reprimand, mandatory education, fines of up to $5,000 per violation, or license suspension.** Repeat violations or patterns of non-compliance may result in revocation. IREC's enforcement process begins with a complaint—often filed by a disgruntled buyer or a competing agent—and proceeds through investigation and a hearing before the commission.

Civil liability is the second risk. A buyer who never signed an agreement can argue they never consented to the terms of representation, including any compensation obligation. Idaho courts have consistently held that real estate compensation agreements must be in writing to be enforceable under the statute of frauds (Idaho Code 9-505(5)). If you cannot produce a signed agreement, you cannot sue for your commission—period.

The third risk is transactional. Title companies and lenders in Idaho increasingly request a copy of the buyer representation agreement as part of the closing file, particularly when buyer-agent compensation appears on the settlement statement. If you cannot produce it, the closing may be delayed or the compensation line item removed entirely. This is not hypothetical—it happened repeatedly in the Boise metro area throughout late 2024 and 2025 as the industry adapted.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make

Five errors show up repeatedly in IREC complaint files and brokerage audits. Avoiding them is straightforward once you know what to watch for.

The first mistake is using an expired agreement. Agents sign a 90-day exclusive agreement in March, then write an offer in July without renewing. The agreement was dead for weeks. The fix is simple: calendar the termination date and send a renewal notice at least 10 days before expiration. Tools that track document deadlines—like those built into [pipeline review systems](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system)—eliminate this failure mode almost entirely.

The second mistake is leaving the compensation field blank or ambiguous. IREC requires that compensation be stated as a specific dollar amount or a clearly calculable percentage. Writing "to be determined" or "per MLS" no longer works because MLS-published buyer-agent compensation offers no longer exist in Idaho's MLS systems. You must state the number.

Third, agents fail to provide the buyer with a signed copy. Idaho Code 54-2051 requires that all parties receive copies of executed documents. Handing the buyer a pen, getting their signature, and then filing the original without giving them their copy is a violation—and it looks terrible in front of a commission hearing officer.

Fourth, some agents use the RE-14B touring agreement as a permanent substitute for full representation. The touring agreement is designed for a single showing or a short exploratory period. It does not establish the full fiduciary relationship needed to write offers or negotiate on the buyer's behalf. Once the buyer decides to make an offer, you must transition to the RE-14 or RE-14A.

Fifth, dual agency disclosure failures compound the buyer agreement issue. Under Idaho Code 54-2085, if you represent both buyer and seller, you need written consent from both parties. Agents sometimes execute the buyer agreement but forget to layer on the dual agency consent form (RE-29) when the buyer wants to purchase one of their own listings. That omission can void the entire agency relationship retroactively.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Idaho Code 54-2041. If your agent is operating without proper buyer agreements, IREC can and does hold the supervising broker accountable. A passive "I didn't know" defense has never worked in front of the commission.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signed buyer agreement on file | Every new buyer client | Signature, date, termination date, compensation terms | Missing termination date or blank compensation |
| Agreement expiration tracking | Weekly | Active buyers nearing agreement end date | Showing activity after expiration |
| Touring agreement vs. full agreement | At offer stage | Correct form in use when offer is written | RE-14B used past its intended scope |
| Copy delivery confirmation | At signing | Buyer received executed copy | No delivery record in file |
| Dual agency consent layering | When applicable | RE-29 executed alongside buyer agreement | Buyer agreement alone on dual-agency transaction |

**Brokers should conduct a monthly file review at minimum, with a more intensive quarterly audit that samples closed and active buyer files randomly.** The goal is to catch issues before a complaint forces you to explain them to IREC. Documentation of your audit process also serves as a mitigating factor if discipline is ever assessed against your brokerage.

Consider building your audit workflow into your transaction management system. Many Idaho brokerages have moved to automated compliance tracking that flags missing documents before a file reaches the offer stage. ## Compensation Disclosure and Negotiation Rules

Since the MLS no longer displays buyer-agent compensation offers in Idaho, the buyer agreement has become the single most important document establishing your right to be paid. The RE-46 Compensation Agreement can supplement the buyer agreement, but it does not replace it.

When a seller is willing to contribute toward buyer-agent compensation, that offer must be communicated outside the MLS—through listing remarks on the brokerage website, direct broker-to-broker communication, or at the offer stage. Your buyer agreement should specify what happens if seller-offered compensation exceeds or falls short of the agreed amount. For example, if your agreement states a 2.5% fee and the seller offers 3%, does the excess go to the buyer as a credit? These terms must be spelled out.

IREC has indicated it will treat vague or misleading compensation disclosures as potential violations of Idaho Code 54-2054(2), which prohibits making false promises likely to influence a party's decision. If your buyer believes they owe nothing and your agreement says otherwise, you have a problem.
