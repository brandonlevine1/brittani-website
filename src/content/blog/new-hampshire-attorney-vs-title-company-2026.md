## Why the Question of New Hampshire Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026 Still Confuses Agents

New Hampshire sits in a gray zone that trips up even experienced agents. Unlike Massachusetts, which flatly requires attorney involvement at closing, and unlike states that leave everything to title companies, New Hampshire blends both models. Understanding "New Hampshire attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" is not optional knowledge — it determines whether your transaction survives post-closing scrutiny and whether you personally face liability exposure.

The confusion stems from the fact that no single statute says "an attorney must attend the closing table." Instead, New Hampshire draws its line through the unauthorized practice of law doctrine, governed by RSA 311:7 and enforced by the New Hampshire Supreme Court's Committee on the Unauthorized Practice of Law. If you misread where that line falls, your deal — and your license — can land in jeopardy.

## What New Hampshire Law Actually Requires

**RSA 311:7 prohibits any person who is not a member of the New Hampshire Bar from practicing law, which includes preparing legal instruments, rendering legal opinions, and conducting title examinations.** The statute does not carve out an exception for title companies, settlement agents, or real estate licensees. RSA 311:7-a gives the court authority to enjoin violations and impose civil penalties.

The New Hampshire Supreme Court has interpreted "practice of law" broadly in the real estate context. In the landmark advisory opinion from the Committee on the Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL Opinion 1, reaffirmed multiple times), the Court held that title examination and the issuance of a title certification or opinion constitutes legal practice. Deed preparation — even from a template — also falls within the definition.

Here is where the practical split occurs: a title company may lawfully act as a settlement agent, handling escrow funds, preparing HUD-1 or ALTA settlement statements, coordinating lien payoffs, and recording documents. But the moment that title company reviews title and issues an opinion on marketability, or drafts a deed without attorney supervision, it crosses into unauthorized practice territory.

## The Practical Division of Labor at Closing

Most New Hampshire closings involve both an attorney and a title insurance company working in tandem. The attorney examines title, certifies marketability, prepares the deed, and sometimes conducts the closing. The title company (or its agent) underwrites the title insurance policy, holds escrow, and manages disbursement.

| Function | Attorney Required? | Title Company Permitted? |
|----------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Title examination and opinion | Yes | No (without attorney supervision) |
| Deed preparation | Yes | No |
| Settlement statement preparation | No | Yes |
| Escrow and fund disbursement | No | Yes |
| Recording of documents | No | Yes |
| Notarization of signatures | Licensed notary (either) | Licensed notary (either) |
| Issuance of title insurance policy | No | Yes (underwriter authorization) |

This table explains why you will see attorneys and title companies coexisting at New Hampshire closings rather than one replacing the other. Agents who tell clients "you don't need a lawyer, the title company handles everything" are making a factually incorrect statement that could expose them to liability under the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission's administrative rules.

## What the Real Estate Commission Expects From Agents

The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC) operates under RSA 331-A and enforces conduct standards through administrative rules under N.H. Admin. Rules, Rea 700. Agents are explicitly prohibited from practicing law or advising clients on legal matters under Rea 701.01. This means you cannot tell a buyer that an attorney is unnecessary, recommend skipping a title opinion, or fill in deed blanks yourself.

**The standard New Hampshire purchase and sale agreement form (the NHAR/Bar Association Joint Form) presumes attorney involvement.** This form, revised periodically by the New Hampshire Association of Realtors and the New Hampshire Bar Association, includes an attorney review provision and references to deed preparation by counsel. Agents working with this form should understand that it was drafted to align with the state's UPL framework.

If you use the NHAR Purchase and Sale Agreement form, Section 18 (Deed) specifies that the seller shall deliver a [quitclaim or warranty] deed prepared by the seller's attorney. This is not a suggestion. It is a contractual obligation that assumes an attorney will be involved on at least the seller's side.

## Common Mistakes New Hampshire Agents Make

The first mistake is conflating the settlement agent role with the attorney role. Agents frequently tell buyers that the "closing company" will handle everything, failing to distinguish between settlement services (permissible for title companies) and legal services (attorney-only). When a title defect surfaces post-closing and no attorney issued a title opinion, the buyer's claim against their title insurance may be complicated — and the agent who steered them away from counsel may face a complaint.

The second mistake is preparing or modifying deed language. Some agents, particularly in [transactions where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down), attempt to "save time" by filling in deed forms themselves. Under RSA 311:7, this constitutes unauthorized practice of law regardless of how simple the conveyance appears. The NHREC can impose fines, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely under RSA 331-A:28.

The third mistake involves failing to advise clients of their right to attorney review. Under the NHAR form, both parties have the right to attorney review within a specified period (typically five business days). Agents who pressure clients to waive this review — or who simply fail to mention it — violate their fiduciary duty and expose themselves to malpractice claims.

**The fourth mistake is assuming that a title insurance commitment substitutes for an attorney's title opinion.** A commitment is a conditional offer to insure — it is not a legal opinion on marketability. New Hampshire lenders almost universally require an attorney's title certification before funding. Agents who do not confirm this step risk delaying closings by days or weeks.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the distinction between the buyer's closing attorney and the seller's deed preparation attorney. In New Hampshire practice, these are often different people. The buyer's attorney examines title and conducts the closing; the seller's attorney prepares the deed. Agents who assume one attorney covers both sides create conflicts and delays.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers have direct supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:25-b. If an agent in your office is practicing law, advising clients to skip attorney involvement, or preparing legal documents, you bear responsibility. The NHREC does not treat ignorance of agent activity as a defense.

**Brokers should audit transaction files for three specific compliance markers.** First, confirm that every closed file contains evidence of an attorney-prepared deed — not a template filled in by the agent or a title company employee. Second, verify that the attorney review period was communicated to both parties in writing. Third, check that the title examination was performed by a licensed New Hampshire attorney, not solely by a title company's internal staff without attorney oversight.

| Audit Item | What to Look For | Red Flag |
|------------|-----------------|----------|
| Deed preparation | Attorney letterhead or billing confirmation | Agent-completed deed template |
| Title opinion | Signed attorney certification in file | Only a title commitment, no opinion |
| Attorney review notice | Written acknowledgment from both parties | No documentation of review period |
| Settlement statement | Prepared by licensed settlement agent or attorney | Prepared by unlicensed office staff |
| Escrow handling | Held by attorney trust account or licensed title company | Held in brokerage operating account |

Brokers who fail to enforce these standards face potential disciplinary action from the NHREC, personal liability in civil malpractice suits, and possible referral to the UPL Committee if their agents' actions constitute facilitation of unauthorized practice. ## How 2026 Changes Affect the Attorney-Title Company Dynamic

No new legislation has altered the fundamental framework for 2026, but two developments matter. First, the New Hampshire Bar Association's Ethics Committee issued guidance in late 2025 clarifying that remote or hybrid closings still require attorney involvement for title opinions and deed preparation — videoconference execution does not eliminate the attorney requirement. Second, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's updated TRID guidelines](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/) continue to shape settlement statement preparation, reinforcing the title company's role in that specific function while leaving legal services to attorneys.

The NHREC has also increased its audit frequency of brokerage files, with specific attention to transactions where no attorney appears in the closing documentation. Agents and brokers operating under the assumption that "nobody checks" are operating under an outdated assumption.

## Protecting Yourself and Your Clients

The safest posture for a New Hampshire agent is straightforward: recommend attorney involvement to every client, document that recommendation in writing, and never perform any task that could be construed as legal practice.
