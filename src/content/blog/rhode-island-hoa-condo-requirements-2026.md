# Rhode Island HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Selling or buying a condominium or HOA-governed property in Rhode Island requires more than just a clean title and a signed offer. The **Rhode Island HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026** carry specific statutory obligations that can unravel a deal — or expose an agent to real liability — when overlooked. Rhode Island's condominium framework is tighter than many agents assume, and the consequences of sloppy handling are written directly into the state code.

This post breaks down the statutes you need to know, the forms you need to track, and the mistakes that keep getting agents into trouble.

## The Statutory Foundation: R.I. Gen. Laws Title 34, Chapters 36 and 36.1

Rhode Island governs condominiums under two parallel statutory chapters. The older Rhode Island Condominium Act, codified at [R.I. Gen. Laws Chapter 34-36](https://law.justia.com/codes/rhode-island/title-34/chapter-34-36/), applies to condominiums created before July 1, 1982. The newer Rhode Island Condominium Act of 1982, codified at R.I. Gen. Laws Chapter 34-36.1, governs all condominiums created on or after that date.

Most active listings in 2026 fall under Chapter 34-36.1, but agents handling older legacy buildings — especially in Providence, Warwick, and Cranston — may still encounter Chapter 34-36 properties. The disclosure obligations differ slightly between the two, so knowing which statute applies to your listing is not optional.

**For HOA-governed communities that are not condominiums, Rhode Island does not have a standalone HOA disclosure statute comparable to its condominium laws.** That gap matters. It means agents working with single-family homes in planned communities must rely on contractual provisions in the purchase agreement, the association's own governing documents, and the Rhode Island Association of Realtors (RIAR) standard forms to surface HOA obligations. There is no statutory resale certificate requirement for non-condo HOAs.

## The Resale Certificate: Rhode Island's Core Condo Disclosure Document

**The resale certificate is the single most important disclosure document in any Rhode Island condominium transaction.** Under R.I. Gen. Laws 34-36.1-4.09, a seller must furnish the buyer with a resale certificate prepared by the condominium association before or at the time of the purchase agreement's execution.

The resale certificate must contain at least the following:

The declaration, bylaws, rules, and any amendments. A statement of monthly common expense assessments and any special assessments currently due or anticipated. A statement of any capital expenditure reserves. The association's current operating budget and financial statements, including any pending lawsuits involving the association.

Rhode Island does not prescribe a single state-issued form number for the resale certificate itself — the content is dictated by statute, and each association typically produces its own version. However, the RIAR provides standard purchase and sale agreement forms (commonly referenced as the RIAR Purchase and Sale Agreement) that include condominium addendum provisions requiring receipt of the resale certificate as a condition of the contract.

| Document | Source Statute | Who Prepares It | Delivery Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resale Certificate | R.I. Gen. Laws 34-36.1-4.09 | Condominium Association | Before or at contract signing |
| Public Offering Statement (new condos) | R.I. Gen. Laws 34-36.1-4.02 | Declarant/Developer | Before buyer executes purchase agreement |
| RIAR Condominium Addendum | RIAR Standard Forms | Listing/Buyer Agent | Incorporated into purchase agreement |
| Seller's Disclosure Form (general) | R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.8-2 | Seller/Listing Agent | Before or at contract signing |

The resale certificate is separate from the Seller's Real Estate Condition Report required under R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.8-2. Both must be delivered. They serve different functions: the condition report addresses the physical state of the unit, while the resale certificate addresses the legal, financial, and governance status of the association.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and the 5-Day Window

Rhode Island gives condominium buyers a statutory right to cancel. Under R.I. Gen. Laws 34-36.1-4.06, a buyer who receives a resale certificate (or a public offering statement for new construction) may cancel the purchase agreement within 5 days of receipt without penalty.

This is a hard deadline, and it runs from actual receipt — not from the date the document was mailed or made available electronically. If the resale certificate is never delivered, the buyer's right to cancel does not expire. That open-ended cancellation risk is one of the strongest reasons agents must confirm delivery and document the date of receipt.

**For new condominium units sold by a declarant, the cancellation window extends to 15 days under R.I. Gen. Laws 34-36.1-4.06(a).** Agents representing buyers in new-construction condo projects should calendar this longer window separately from resale transactions.

The cancellation must be made in writing, and the buyer is entitled to a full refund of any deposits paid. Agents who fail to advise buyers of this statutory right are exposing themselves and their brokerages to claims of inadequate representation, similar to risks agents face when [missing critical contractual deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in any state.

## Rhode Island HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Has Changed

The core statutory framework under Chapters 34-36 and 34-36.1 has not been substantially amended in the current legislative session. However, enforcement attention from the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation (DBR), which oversees real estate licensees, has intensified around disclosure compliance.

The DBR has issued guidance reminding brokerages that failure to deliver required condominium documents can constitute a violation of R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5 (the Real Estate Licensing Act), potentially triggering license discipline. This is not new law — it is stricter interpretation of existing law, applied to the condominium context.

Additionally, RIAR updated its standard forms library in late 2025 to include clearer condominium addendum language addressing electronic delivery of association documents and explicit buyer acknowledgment fields. Agents still using outdated forms are missing these acknowledgment checkboxes, which creates an evidentiary gap if a buyer later claims non-receipt.

Rhode Island also lacks a state-mandated HOA registry, unlike states such as Colorado or Virginia. This means agents have no centralized database to verify association contact information, financial health, or governance status. Diligence falls entirely on the agent and the title company.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Condo and HOA Disclosures

**Mistake one: assuming the seller will handle the resale certificate without agent follow-up.** The statute places the obligation on the seller, but in practice, sellers often have no idea what a resale certificate is or how to request one from their association. Listing agents who treat this as a passive obligation end up with closing delays — or worse, a buyer who exercises a cancellation right because the certificate arrived late.

Mistake two: conflating the Seller's Real Estate Condition Report with the condominium resale certificate. These are two distinct documents under two different statutes. Delivering one does not satisfy the requirement for the other. Agents who check off "disclosures complete" after collecting only the condition report are leaving a statutory gap wide open.

Mistake three: ignoring the difference between pre-1982 and post-1982 condominiums. The disclosure obligations under Chapter 34-36 (pre-1982) are less detailed than those under Chapter 34-36.1, but they still exist. Agents listing older condos sometimes assume no resale certificate is required simply because the building predates the 1982 act. That is incorrect — [Section 34-36-26](https://law.justia.com/codes/rhode-island/title-34/chapter-34-36/) still requires certain disclosures for pre-1982 units.

Mistake four: failing to document the exact date the buyer received the resale certificate. Without that date memorialized in writing, there is no way to determine when the 5-day cancellation period began or ended. A timestamped email confirmation or a signed receipt is the minimum standard.

Mistake five: skipping HOA financial review on non-condo properties. Even though Rhode Island does not mandate a resale certificate for non-condo HOAs, a buyer's agent who fails to request governing documents, financial statements, and assessment schedules from a homeowners association is falling below the professional standard of care. The absence of a statutory mandate does not eliminate the fiduciary duty to inform.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerages operating in Rhode Island should be running periodic file audits that specifically check for condominium compliance. The checklist every broker should enforce is straightforward but rarely followed consistently.

| Audit Item | What to Verify | Red Flag If Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Resale Certificate in file | Association-prepared, matches unit being sold | Buyer retains open cancellation right |
| Receipt date documented | Buyer-signed acknowledgment or email timestamp | Cannot prove 5-day window started |
| Correct statute identified | Chapter 34-36 vs. 34-36.1 based on condo creation date | Wrong disclosure standard applied |
| RIAR Condominium Addendum | Current version, all fields completed | Outdated forms lack acknowledgment fields |
| Seller's Condition Report | Separate from resale certificate, on file | Dual statutory violation |

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-audit are asking for trouble.** Transaction files for condo sales should be flagged at intake so that compliance checkpoints are triggered automatically. This is exactly the kind of process where structured oversight prevents expensive errors — and why many brokerages are turning to [checklist-driven systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) rather than agent memory.

The DBR has the authority to investigate complaints, conduct audits, and impose fines or license suspensions under R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-12. Disciplinary actions are public record and can affect a brokerage's errors and omissions insurance rates. The financial exposure goes well beyond the individual transaction.

## Liability When Disclosure Fails

An agent who fails to deliver the required resale certificate does not just risk a canceled deal. The buyer retains the right to rescind the purchase agreement at any point until proper delivery occurs — even after closing, in some interpretations. Rhode Island courts have not tested this boundary frequently, but the statutory language under Section 34-36.1-4.06 does not cap the cancellation window when the certificate was never delivered.

Beyond rescission, an agent may face a DBR complaint for violating the standards of practice embedded in the licensing act. The Rhode Island Real Estate Commission, operating under the DBR, can impose fines, require additional education, suspend, or revoke a license. Monetary penalties vary by severity, but the reputational damage and legal costs of defending a complaint typically exceed the fine itself.

Sellers who suffer damages from a failed closing caused by missing disclosures may also pursue civil claims against the listing agent and brokerage. Errors and omissions policies typically cover these claims, but only if the agent can demonstrate they followed reasonable procedures. A file with no resale certificate and no buyer acknowledgment is difficult to defend.

Request the resale certificate the day the listing agreement is signed. Confirm receipt in writing. Calendar the buyer's 5-day cancellation window with a hard reminder. Use the current RIAR forms, not last year's version.
