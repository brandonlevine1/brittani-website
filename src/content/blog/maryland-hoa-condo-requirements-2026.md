## Why Maryland HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

Maryland ranks among the most prescriptive states when it comes to resale disclosure obligations for properties governed by homeowners associations and condominium regimes. If you hold an active license and list or sell in an HOA or condo community, the **Maryland hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** framework creates deadlines, document delivery requirements, and rescission rights that directly affect whether your deals close or collapse. Ignoring the statutory mechanics is not just sloppy—it exposes you and your brokerage to contract rescission, commission clawback, and potential disciplinary action by the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC).

Two separate bodies of law control these transactions. The Maryland Condominium Act lives in the Real Property Article, Title 11 (Sections 11-101 through 11-143). The Maryland Homeowners Association Act occupies Real Property Article, Title 11B (Sections 11B-101 through 11B-118). Each statute mandates a distinct resale disclosure package with different contents, and confusing the two is one of the fastest ways to blow a settlement date.

## The Two Statutory Frameworks: Condos vs. HOAs

**Understanding which statute applies to your listing is step one, and getting it wrong voids the disclosure entirely.** A condominium is defined by the existence of a recorded declaration creating a condominium regime under Title 11. An HOA-governed community—townhomes, single-family subdivisions with covenants—falls under Title 11B. Some properties sit in both a condo regime and a master HOA, requiring two separate packages.

The Maryland Association of Realtors (MAR) Residential Contract of Sale addresses this through specific addenda. Form 1001, the Residential Contract, includes check boxes indicating whether the property is subject to an HOA, a condominium, or both. The corresponding addenda—MAR Form 1038 (HOA Addendum) and MAR Form 1039 (Condominium Addendum)—spell out the resale package delivery timeline and the buyer's rescission window.

Agents sometimes assume these addenda are optional riders. They are not. When a property falls under Title 11 or Title 11B, attaching the correct addendum is a contractual and statutory obligation, not a courtesy.

## What Must Be in the Resale Disclosure Package

The contents differ depending on which statute controls the property. Below is a side-by-side comparison of the minimum required documents.

| Requirement | Condominium (Title 11, Sec. 11-135) | HOA (Title 11B, Sec. 11B-106) |
|---|---|---|
| Governing documents (declaration, bylaws, rules) | Required | Required |
| Current financial statement or budget | Required | Required |
| Statement of monthly/annual assessments | Required | Required |
| Reserve fund study or balance disclosure | Required | Required (if study exists) |
| Insurance summary (master policy) | Required | Not explicitly mandated but recommended |
| Outstanding liens or special assessments against unit/lot | Required | Required |
| Pending litigation involving the association | Required | Required |
| Capital improvement plans funded by special assessment | Required | Required |

The condominium resale certificate under Section 11-135 is the more detailed of the two. It must also include a statement of any right of first refusal, the percentage of units occupied by owners (versus renters), and any restrictions on leasing. The HOA package under Section 11B-106 carries its own requirements regarding lot-specific violations and architectural control procedures.

**Failure to include any single mandatory item means the package is legally incomplete.** An incomplete package does not trigger the buyer's rescission clock, leaving the transaction in limbo until the deficiency is cured.

## Deadlines, Rescission Rights, and the Five-Day Clock

Under Section 11-135, a buyer who receives a complete condominium resale certificate has five days to rescind the contract. The same five-day rescission window applies to HOA resale packages under Section 11B-106. The clock starts on the date of actual receipt, not the date of mailing.

This creates a practical problem agents often underestimate. If the package arrives at settlement or the day before, the buyer still holds a five-day right to walk. Many title companies will refuse to schedule closing until the rescission period has expired. Smart listing agents order the resale package immediately upon ratification—or even before listing—to avoid last-minute delays.

The rescission must be in writing. No reason is required. If the buyer rescinds within the statutory window, the contract is void and the deposit must be returned in full. Neither agent earns a commission, and the seller returns to active status with lost market time.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Liability, Cancellation, and Fines

Maryland does not treat disclosure failures as minor paperwork oversights. The buyer's right to rescind persists indefinitely if the resale package is never delivered or is materially incomplete. This means a buyer could theoretically rescind weeks after settlement if the association or listing agent failed to provide the package—though practical enforcement after deed transfer is rare and usually pursued through damages rather than rescission.

From a licensing standpoint, MREC considers failure to provide mandatory disclosures a violation of COMAR 09.11.02 (Standards of Conduct for licensees). Disciplinary actions range from a reprimand to fine (up to $5,000 per violation) to license suspension. Brokers bear supervisory liability under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-532 if they fail to ensure their agents comply.

| Non-Compliance Scenario | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|
| Resale package never delivered | Buyer retains indefinite rescission right |
| Incomplete package delivered | Five-day clock never starts; buyer can cancel anytime before cure |
| Agent advises buyer to waive rescission rights | MREC disciplinary action; possible civil liability |
| Broker fails to audit file for addendum/package | Supervisory liability under BOP Section 17-532 |
| Association refuses to produce package | Listing agent must document efforts; buyer may cancel per contract terms |

The financial exposure extends beyond fines. If a buyer cancels post-settlement and sues for damages—moving costs, inspection fees, appraisal costs—the listing agent and brokerage may be named as defendants alongside the association. Errors and omissions policies typically cover these claims, but repeated violations can trigger policy cancellation or premium increases.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Maryland HOA and Condo Disclosures

**Mistake 1: Confusing the HOA addendum with the condo addendum.** A townhome in a condominium regime requires Form 1039, not Form 1038. The distinction depends on how the community was recorded, not the property's physical appearance. Agents must check the declaration type, not assume based on architecture.

**Mistake 2: Relying on the management company to deliver the package directly to the buyer without tracking receipt.** The statute places the obligation on the seller (through their agent) to ensure delivery. If the management company mails the package and it never arrives, the listing agent cannot claim compliance by pointing to a third party's mailing label.

Mistake 3: Ordering the resale package too late in the transaction timeline. Most management companies quote 10 to 15 business days for preparation. Rush fees can exceed $300. Agents who wait until the home inspection contingency expires to order the package risk blowing past the settlement date.

Mistake 4: Advising buyers that the five-day rescission period is "just a formality" or pressuring them to waive it. Maryland law does not permit waiver of the statutory rescission right, and any attempt to contract around it is void. An agent who suggests otherwise faces disciplinary exposure.

Mistake 5: Failing to distinguish between the [resale certificate and the seller's property condition disclosure](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory). These are separate obligations. The resale package comes from the association; the property condition disclosure (Maryland Form) comes from the seller. Both must be delivered, and neither substitutes for the other.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerage compliance programs in Maryland should treat HOA and condo disclosure as a checklist gate that cannot be bypassed before a file moves to settlement coordination. Here is what supervising brokers should verify in every applicable transaction file.

First, confirm the correct addendum (Form 1038 or 1039) is attached to the ratified contract. A missing addendum does not eliminate the statutory obligation—it just removes the contractual framework for enforcing timelines, which creates ambiguity that benefits no one.

Second, require agents to upload proof of resale package order within 48 hours of ratification. This means a confirmation email from the management company or a screenshot of the online ordering portal. Brokerages that track this metric routinely close on time; those that do not routinely scramble at the eleventh hour.

Third, audit for documented delivery and receipt. The best practice is to have the buyer or buyer's agent sign an acknowledgment of receipt with the date. Without this documentation, [proving the rescission clock started becomes difficult in a dispute](/blog/where-deals-break-down). Digital transaction management platforms can timestamp delivery automatically, but only if the agent uploads the package through the platform rather than emailing it separately.

Fourth, train agents annually on the difference between Title 11 and Title 11B. MREC-approved continuing education courses cover this topic, but in-house training with jurisdiction-specific scenarios reduces errors more effectively than generic CE webinars.

Finally, establish a firm policy that no agent may advise a buyer to waive, shorten, or ignore the five-day rescission period. Any such advice—even informal, verbal guidance—creates brokerage liability and potential MREC complaints. ## Staying Current With Statutory Amendments

The Maryland General Assembly routinely introduces bills amending Title 11 and Title 11B. The 2025 session produced amendments to reserve study disclosure requirements, and further refinements are expected during the 2026 session. Agents should monitor the [Maryland Department of Labor's Real Estate Commission page](https://www.dllr.state.md.us/license/mrec/) for regulatory updates and bulletin notices.

Association governance is also evolving. Larger management companies have begun delivering resale packages electronically with embedded e-signature acknowledgments, which satisfies the delivery requirement as long as the buyer consents to electronic delivery under UETA. Smaller self-managed associations remain a pain point—they often lack the infrastructure to produce compliant packages quickly, and agents must budget extra time and follow-up effort for these communities.
