## Iowa Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Rules Agents Cannot Afford to Confuse

Iowa occupies an unusual position among U.S. states when it comes to real estate closings. Understanding "Iowa attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" is not optional knowledge for licensed agents — it is the difference between a clean transaction and an unauthorized-practice-of-law complaint. Iowa's regulatory framework blends attorney oversight with title company functions in ways that differ sharply from neighboring states like Wisconsin, where [title and closing procedures follow a different model](/blog/wisconsin-title-and-closing-procedures-2026).

The confusion is understandable. Iowa has no single statute that says "an attorney must sit at the closing table." Instead, the requirement is buried in case law, Iowa Supreme Court advisory opinions, and the interplay between Iowa Code Chapter 602 (Judicial Branch) and Iowa Administrative Code 193E (Real Estate Commission rules). Agents who rely on assumptions drawn from other states or outdated training risk real consequences.

## The Legal Framework: Why Iowa Is Different

**Iowa's unauthorized practice of law doctrine is broader than most agents realize.** The Iowa Supreme Court has exclusive authority to define what constitutes the practice of law under Article V of the Iowa Constitution. In the landmark case *Iowa Supreme Court Board of Professional Ethics and Conduct v. Jones* (2000), the Court reaffirmed that preparing deeds, mortgages, contracts, and other legal instruments constitutes legal practice — period.

Iowa Code Section 602.10101 grants the Supreme Court power to regulate the practice of law. Unlike states where title companies or escrow officers freely prepare documents, Iowa requires that a licensed attorney draft or supervise the drafting of instruments that transfer or encumber real property. This does not mean an attorney must physically hand you a pen at the closing table, but it does mean an attorney must be involved in document preparation.

The Iowa State Bar Association's Real Property Section has consistently reinforced this position. Title companies in Iowa handle searches, issue commitments, provide title insurance, and often serve as the settlement agent for disbursement. But they cannot prepare the deed, draft custom contract language, or advise either party on legal rights without crossing into unauthorized practice.

## What Title Companies Can and Cannot Do in Iowa

Here is where agents trip up most often. Title companies play a critical role in Iowa closings — they are not sidelined. The distinction is functional, not ceremonial.

| Function | Attorney | Title Company |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| Draft warranty deed or quit claim deed | Required | Cannot perform |
| Prepare title abstract or commitment | Not required | Standard role |
| Issue title insurance policy | Not required | Standard role |
| Review and approve title | Can perform | Can perform (opinion vs. insurance) |
| Disburse closing funds | Can perform | Standard role |
| Advise parties on legal rights | Can perform | Prohibited |
| Fill in blanks on IAR-approved forms | Can perform | Cannot give legal advice about choices |

**Title companies in Iowa routinely act as the settlement agent, but that role is narrower than what agents observe in "title states" like Wisconsin or Minnesota.** The settlement agent receives funds, confirms payoffs, records documents, and issues the final title policy. None of that crosses into legal practice. The problem arises when a closing coordinator at a title company starts explaining to a buyer what a particular clause means or drafts an addendum to resolve a last-minute issue.

Iowa Administrative Code 193E — Chapter 14 governs what real estate licensees can do in this arena. Under Rule 193E-14.1, agents may fill in the blanks on forms approved by the Iowa Real Estate Commission, but they cannot draft legal documents or provide legal advice. The boundary between "filling in blanks" and "drafting" is narrower than most agents assume.

## The Forms Agents Must Use — and Their Limits

The Iowa Association of Realtors (IAR) provides the standard forms Iowa agents rely on. The most relevant include IAR Form 101 (Residential Purchase Agreement), IAR Form 103 (Counter Offer), IAR Form 108 (Amendment to Purchase Agreement), and IAR Form 127 (Seller's Property Condition Disclosure). These forms are pre-approved for agent use precisely because they limit agents to filling in factual blanks rather than creating legal language.

When a transaction requires anything beyond what IAR Form 101 and its companion forms allow — a custom contingency, a unique seller financing arrangement, a boundary dispute resolution — an attorney must draft the language. Agents who improvise contractual language on a blank addendum form are practicing law without a license, regardless of how many transactions they have closed.

The Iowa Real Estate Commission has been clear on this in multiple advisory opinions. Rule 193E-14.1(2) specifically states that licensees shall not draft riders, addenda, or amendments that contain legal language not found on Commission-approved forms unless an attorney has prepared the language.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make at Closing

The gap between what agents think they can do and what Iowa law permits creates recurring problems. Here are the five most frequent errors.

**First, agents draft custom addenda without attorney involvement.** A buyer wants to add a clause requiring the seller to remediate radon to a specific level. The agent writes the language, both parties sign it, and no attorney reviews it. If the clause is ambiguous and leads to a dispute, the agent faces potential liability for unauthorized practice and for the financial harm caused by defective language.

Second, agents tell clients they do not need an attorney because "the title company handles everything." This advice exposes the agent to a negligence claim if a legal issue later arises that an attorney would have caught — an improperly drafted deed, a missed easement not covered by the title commitment's exceptions, or a contract clause that fails to protect the client's interest.

Third, agents schedule closings without confirming attorney document preparation is complete. In Iowa, the closing attorney (or the attorney supervising document preparation) must have the deed, affidavit of consideration, and any corrective instruments ready before the parties sit down. Agents who assume the title company will "take care of it" sometimes discover at the table that no deed has been prepared because no one engaged an attorney.

Fourth, agents confuse the title opinion letter (historically common in rural Iowa) with title insurance. Some Iowa transactions, particularly in less populated counties, still close with an attorney's title opinion rather than a lender-required title insurance policy. Agents who do not understand the difference may fail to inform clients about the gap in protection. An attorney's opinion is not an insurance policy — it does not indemnify against undiscovered defects.

Fifth, agents fail to disclose their own role limitations. Under Iowa Administrative Code 193E-14.1, licensees must inform clients that they are not providing legal advice and should recommend that clients consult an attorney. Skipping this disclosure — especially in FSBO conversions or investor transactions — creates regulatory exposure with the Commission.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Iowa Code Section 543B.29 and Iowa Administrative Code 193E — Chapter 17. If an agent in your brokerage is drafting legal language, advising clients on legal rights, or failing to recommend attorney consultation, the broker faces discipline alongside the agent.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Custom addenda in transaction files | Confirm attorney drafted or approved any non-IAR language | Unauthorized practice exposure, E&O claim |
| Attorney engagement documentation | Verify a closing attorney is identified before contract goes binding | Closing delay, unrecordable deed |
| Client recommendation of counsel | Written disclosure that agent is not providing legal advice | Commission complaint, negligence claim |
| Form version verification | All IAR forms are current 2026 versions | Outdated terms, unenforceable clauses |
| Settlement statement review | Confirm fees align with purchase agreement terms | RESPA violations, client disputes |

**Brokers should implement a file review checkpoint no later than five days after mutual acceptance.** At that point, confirm that the transaction file identifies either a closing attorney or an attorney-supervised title company closing arrangement. If neither is documented, the broker's compliance team must intervene before the deal progresses further. Iowa Real Estate Commission disciplinary actions are public. Violations of the unauthorized practice rules or failure to supervise can result in license suspension, civil penalties under Iowa Code 543B.34, and referral to the Iowa Supreme Court's Attorney Disciplinary Board if the conduct involves a licensed attorney acting improperly.

## How Closings Actually Work in Iowa: The Typical Flow

In practice, most Iowa residential closings involve three parties working in concert: a closing attorney who prepares the deed and reviews legal sufficiency, a title company that issues the commitment and handles settlement, and the lender's closing department that provides loan documents. The real estate agents attend but do not direct the legal process.

Many Iowa attorneys also operate their own title companies, or have ownership interests in affiliated title entities. This is permitted under Iowa law provided proper disclosure is made. When an attorney serves as both document preparer and settlement agent, the closing feels seamless — one office handles everything. Agents who work in markets where this model dominates sometimes forget that the attorney role is legally distinct from the title company role, even when the same person wears both hats.

In markets where the attorney and title company are separate entities, coordination becomes the agent's responsibility. ## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The Iowa Supreme Court can seek injunction against any person engaging in unauthorized practice of law. This is not theoretical — the Court has pursued injunctions against title company employees, real estate agents, and document preparation services in reported cases.

For agents specifically, the Iowa Real Estate Commission can impose sanctions under Iowa Code 543B.29, including formal citation, civil penalty (up to $10,000 per violation under 543B.34), license suspension, or revocation. The Commission does not need a client complaint to initiate an investigation; an audit of transaction files can trigger enforcement.

Beyond regulatory risk, agents face civil liability. If a custom clause an agent drafted fails in litigation, the agent's E&O insurance may deny coverage on the grounds that the agent was acting outside the scope of their license. The client can then pursue a direct negligence action against the agent and the supervising broker personally.

The answer is not either/or — it is both, with distinct roles. Iowa requires attorney involvement in document preparation, and title companies fill the settlement and title insurance functions. No Iowa statute mandates that an attorney physically sit at the closing table for every transaction, but the functional requirement of attorney-prepared documents means attorney involvement is unavoidable in a compliant closing.
