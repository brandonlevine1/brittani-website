## Understanding South Carolina Escrow Regulations 2026 at the Statutory Level

South Carolina escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who mishandle trust funds—even by accident. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) governs these rules primarily through S.C. Code Title 40, Chapter 57, and its accompanying administrative regulations found in S.C. Code of Regulations Chapter 105. Every licensee operating in the state needs to know not just the spirit of these rules but the specific section numbers that SCREC investigators cite when they open a complaint file.

**The foundational statute is S.C. Code Section 40-57-135, which mandates that all earnest money, security deposits, and other trust funds received by a licensee on behalf of another party must be deposited into a properly designated trust account.** This isn't optional guidance—it's black-letter law. Violations carry administrative penalties, license action, and potential civil liability that can exceed the value of the underlying transaction.

SCREC Regulation 105-4 further specifies the mechanics: account labeling, banking institution requirements, record retention, and reconciliation frequency. Brokers-in-charge bear primary responsibility, but individual agents face personal liability when they physically handle funds or direct their disposition outside the proper chain.

## Deposit Timing and the Forms That Govern It

The standard residential contract form in South Carolina—SCR Form 310, published by the South Carolina REALTORS—establishes the contractual framework for earnest money deposits. Paragraph 3 of SCR 310 designates the escrow agent, the deposit amount, and the timeframe for delivery. Most transactions specify 3 banking days from the binding agreement date, though parties can negotiate different terms.

Here's where agents get into trouble: the contractual deadline is separate from the regulatory obligation. Even if the contract says 5 days, SCREC expects prompt deposit consistent with Regulation 105-4. Holding a check in your car console for a week while you wait for counterparty signatures on an addendum is exactly the behavior that triggers complaints.

SCR Form 350 (the Addendum to Contract) and SCR Form 320 (Agreement to Buy and Sell Land) contain parallel escrow provisions with slightly different paragraph numbering. Agents working land deals or commercial-adjacent residential transactions need to verify they're referencing the correct form's deposit timeline, not defaulting to the SCR 310 structure from habit.

## Who Can Hold Escrow in South Carolina

South Carolina restricts escrow custody to specific parties. Unlike some states that allow transaction coordinators or unlicensed assistants to hold funds, South Carolina limits trust fund custody to licensed brokers operating a compliant trust account, attorneys admitted to the South Carolina Bar, or licensed title/closing agents operating under the state's settlement agent framework.

| Authorized Escrow Holder | Governing Authority | Key Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed Broker (Broker-in-Charge) | SCREC / S.C. Code 40-57-135 | Must maintain designated trust account at SC-insured institution |
| SC-Licensed Attorney | SC Bar / Supreme Court Rules | Must use IOLTA or dedicated escrow account |
| Licensed Closing Agent | SC Department of Insurance | Must carry surety bond per state requirements |
| Title Company (through licensed agent) | SC Department of Insurance | Must comply with SC Closing Attorney requirements |

**A critical distinction in South Carolina is the state's insistence on attorney involvement at closing.** South Carolina is one of the few states where the unauthorized practice of law statutes (S.C. Code Section 40-5-310) effectively require an attorney to conduct or supervise real estate closings. This means the escrow holder at closing is almost always an attorney's office, even if the broker held earnest money during the contract period.

Agents who attempt to hold deposits in personal accounts, or who direct buyers to wire funds to any account other than the designated escrow holder named in the contract, are violating both SCREC regulations and potentially criminal statutes related to conversion of funds.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance: What's Actually at Stake

The consequences of escrow violations in South Carolina escalate quickly. SCREC operates under a disciplinary matrix that considers the severity, frequency, and intent behind violations. S.C. Code Section 40-57-145 authorizes the Commission to impose administrative fines of up to $10,000 per violation, and multiple violations in a single transaction can be treated as separate offenses.

Beyond fines, license suspension or revocation remains SCREC's most powerful tool. A 2024 Commission audit cycle resulted in 14 formal disciplinary actions related to trust account deficiencies, and brokers should expect continued scrutiny through 2026. The Commission publishes disciplinary orders on its [official website](https://llr.sc.gov/res/), and these orders become public record.

| Violation Type | Potential Penalty | Additional Exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Late deposit of earnest money | Fine up to $1,000; letter of caution | Contract cancellation by aggrieved party |
| Commingling trust funds with operating funds | Fine up to $10,000; license suspension | Civil liability; potential criminal referral |
| Failure to maintain ledger records | Fine up to $5,000; mandatory audit | Broker-in-charge liability for all agents |
| Unauthorized disbursement | License revocation possible | Civil conversion claim; criminal misappropriation |
| Failure to reconcile monthly | Fine up to $2,500; corrective action plan | Discovery of larger discrepancies during audit |

Deal cancellation is the consequence agents feel most immediately. Under SCR Form 310, a material breach of the earnest money provisions—such as failure to deposit within the stated timeframe—can give the other party grounds to terminate the contract and potentially claim damages. You don't need a SCREC complaint to lose a deal; you just need a buyer's attorney reading the contract literally.

The downstream effect on [pipeline management](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) is significant. One escrow violation can cascade into lost commission, a formal complaint, and increased E&O insurance premiums that affect an agent's business for years.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With South Carolina Escrow

**Mistake number one: treating earnest money checks as leverage during negotiations.** Some agents hold deposits without depositing them, believing this gives their client negotiating flexibility or an easier refund path if the deal falls apart. This directly violates Regulation 105-4 and creates personal liability for the agent even when the broker-in-charge is unaware.

Mistake number two involves improper documentation on disbursement. When a transaction fails and both parties claim the earnest money, South Carolina law (S.C. Code Section 27-18-10 et seq., the SC Uniform Unclaimed Property Act, combined with SCREC interpleader guidance) requires the escrow holder to either obtain mutual written release or file an interpleader action. Agents who pressure escrow holders to release funds without proper authorization from both parties expose everyone to liability.

Third, agents routinely fail to verify that the escrow holder named in the contract is actually the entity receiving the funds. A buyer's agent might complete SCR 310 naming the listing broker's trust account, then verbally instruct the buyer to wire money to the closing attorney's account instead. That mismatch between contract terms and actual deposit creates an audit trail nightmare and potential [wire fraud vulnerability](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process).

Fourth, agents in dual-agency situations sometimes attempt to manage escrow disputes informally rather than following the formal dispute resolution process. South Carolina's dual agency disclosure (SCR Form 201) doesn't grant agents authority to decide escrow disputes. The moment both parties claim funds, the agent must step back and follow statutory procedures.

Fifth, neglecting to communicate escrow status to clients in writing. While not a statutory violation per se, failure to confirm deposit dates and amounts in writing removes the agent's best defense if a complaint is later filed. A simple email confirming deposit creates a timestamp that SCREC investigators rely on.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge in South Carolina carry direct personal responsibility for every dollar in their trust account under Regulation 105-4. This isn't delegable. Even if a bookkeeper or transaction coordinator handles day-to-day reconciliation, the broker-in-charge must sign the monthly reconciliation and maintain oversight.

**SCREC requires monthly trust account reconciliation that balances three records: the bank statement, the check register, and the individual transaction ledgers.** Any discrepancy—even one cent—must be investigated and documented before the next reconciliation cycle. Brokers who delegate this entirely to staff without personal review are creating the conditions for catastrophic audit failures.

Brokers should enforce these specific protocols across their offices: verification that every contract's designated escrow holder matches the actual recipient of funds, confirmation of deposit within the contractual timeframe with date-stamped documentation, written notification to all parties when deposits are received, and immediate escalation procedures when a dispute arises over earnest money.

The [oversight failures that commonly derail transactions](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often trace back to broker-level systems that lack checkpoints. A broker who reviews escrow compliance only during annual SCREC audits—rather than building it into weekly transaction management—is relying on luck rather than process.

Quarterly internal audits that mirror SCREC's own examination procedures are the minimum standard for a well-run brokerage. These audits should verify that no trust funds have been held beyond the contract's closing or termination date without proper documentation, that interest-bearing accounts (if used) comply with S.C. Code Section 40-57-135(B), and that the broker's trust account has never fallen below the aggregate balance of all outstanding escrow obligations.

## Handling Disputes: Interpleader and the 30-Day Clock

When earnest money is disputed in South Carolina, the escrow holder faces a regulatory clock. SCREC guidance instructs brokers that if mutual written release cannot be obtained within a reasonable timeframe—typically interpreted as 30 days from the dispute arising—the broker must initiate interpleader proceedings in the appropriate court of common pleas.

Filing interpleader deposits the disputed funds with the court and releases the broker from liability for the ultimate disposition. The cost of filing is typically recoverable from the escrowed funds per court order. Brokers who simply sit on disputed funds indefinitely, hoping the parties resolve things informally, risk a SCREC complaint from either side.

> S.C. Code Section 40-57-135(E): "A broker holding disputed trust funds shall not disburse such funds except upon written agreement of all parties or court order."

This statutory language means silence from one party—such as a buyer who stops returning calls—does not authorize disbursement to the other party. The interpleader path exists precisely for this scenario, and agents need to counsel their clients that disputed funds will not be released quickly without mutual cooperation.

## Staying Compliant as Regulations Evolve

SCREC updates its regulations through the South Carolina Administrative Procedures Act, and proposed rule changes are published in the South Carolina State Register before adoption. Agents and brokers who monitor the Register—or at minimum, subscribe to SCREC's email notifications—will have advance notice of any modifications to escrow handling requirements.

The 2026 regulatory environment reflects increased Commission attention to wire fraud prevention, digital document storage requirements for trust account records, and electronic signature authentication on disbursement authorizations.
