## Why South Dakota Commission & Licensing Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) has sharpened its enforcement posture heading into 2026, and agents who treat licensing requirements as background noise are the ones most likely to face disciplinary action. Understanding South Dakota commission & licensing rules 2026 is not optional—it is the baseline that keeps your license active, your commissions collectible, and your brokerage out of regulatory crosshairs. The stakes run from monetary fines up through full license revocation.

South Dakota's governing statutes sit in SDCL Title 36, Chapter 21A, and the administrative rules under ARSD Article 20:69. Together, these documents dictate who may hold a license, how commissions must be handled, and what disclosures agents owe to every party in a transaction. If you have not reviewed those provisions since your last renewal, now is the time.

This post walks through the specific statutes, required forms, penalty structures, common violations, and broker-level audit duties that matter most in 2026. Every citation references a real regulation or form number so you can verify it yourself.

## Licensing Structure and Statutory Authority

**South Dakota issues two tiers of real estate licenses: salesperson and broker.** Both are governed by SDCL 36-21A and administered by the SDREC, which operates under the Department of Labor and Regulation. The Commission's nine members—seven appointed by the Governor—hold rulemaking, investigatory, and disciplinary power over every licensee in the state.

To obtain a salesperson license, an applicant must complete 116 hours of pre-licensing education from a Commission-approved provider, pass the state and national portions of the licensing exam, and affiliate with a licensed broker. Broker applicants face a higher bar: at least two years of active salesperson experience and an additional 228 hours of broker-specific coursework under SDCL 36-21A-22.

Renewal happens every two years by November 30 of even-numbered years. Agents must complete 24 hours of continuing education during each renewal cycle, including at least two hours on mandatory topics set by the Commission. Missing that deadline does not grant a grace period for practicing—it simply means you are unlicensed.

## Commission Payment Rules Under SDCL 36-21A-72

The single statute that governs how money changes hands between agents and brokers is SDCL 36-21A-72. It states plainly that no salesperson may receive compensation for real estate services from anyone other than their employing broker. This is not a guideline; it is a statutory prohibition with teeth.

Brokers, in turn, may only pay commissions to other licensed brokers or to their own affiliated salespersons. A South Dakota broker who wires a referral fee directly to an unlicensed individual, or to a licensed agent at another firm without going through that agent's broker, violates the statute. Penalties include fines, license suspension, and potential civil liability if a party to the transaction challenges the payment.

| Payment Scenario | Legal Under SDCL 36-21A-72? | Risk if Violated |
|---|---|---|
| Broker pays own affiliated agent | Yes | None |
| Broker pays cooperating broker | Yes | None |
| Agent pays another agent directly | No | Fine, suspension, deal dispute |
| Broker pays unlicensed referral source | No | Fine, revocation, civil liability |
| Agent accepts payment from a consumer | No | Fine, suspension, commission forfeiture |

**Commission splits, bonuses, and referral fees must all be documented in the broker's records.** The SDREC can audit those records at any time under ARSD 20:69:04:02, and missing documentation turns a compliant transaction into a compliance headache.

## Required Forms and Disclosure Obligations

South Dakota agents work with a specific set of Commission-prescribed forms that are non-negotiable. The most important for commission and agency purposes is the **Brokerage Disclosure Form**, which must be presented to all parties at first substantive contact. This form satisfies the requirements of SDCL 36-21A-69 through 36-21A-71, which define the three recognized agency relationships: seller's agent, buyer's agent, and disclosed dual agent.

The **Brokerage Agreement** (sometimes referred to as the listing agreement for sellers or buyer-broker agreement for buyers) formalizes the scope of services and commission terms. Since the 2024 settlement-driven changes across the industry, South Dakota brokerages have increasingly adopted written buyer representation agreements before showing property—a practice the SDREC encourages though state law technically requires disclosure of agency rather than a signed contract before every showing.

South Dakota also requires the **Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement**, governed by SDCL 43-4-44. While this form relates to property condition rather than commission, failing to deliver it on time can kill a deal and expose the listing agent's brokerage to liability—which directly affects commission collection. Agents who handle these deadlines manually rather than through a tracked workflow are the ones most likely to [miss critical dates](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Another form worth noting is the **Trust Account Record** documentation required of brokers under ARSD 20:69:04. Earnest money deposits must be placed in the broker's trust account within three business days of acceptance unless the purchase agreement specifies otherwise. This is where commission rules intersect with fiduciary duties—mishandled trust funds trigger the most severe SDREC sanctions.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The SDREC does not limit itself to warning letters. Under SDCL 36-21A-46, the Commission may impose civil penalties of up to $2,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, place a licensee on probation, or require additional education. Multiple violations in a single transaction can stack, meaning a single deal gone wrong could generate five-figure exposure.

**License revocation is not theoretical in South Dakota—the SDREC publishes disciplinary actions, and the list grows every year.** Common triggers include practicing on an expired license, mishandling trust funds, failing to disclose agency relationships, and accepting undisclosed compensation. Each of these violations carries independent penalty authority.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer or seller who discovers an undisclosed dual agency or an improper commission arrangement can seek rescission of the contract under general contract law, and courts have upheld those claims when the agent's conduct violated SDCL 36-21A. The brokerage, as the agent's principal, shares that exposure. ## Common Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make

Three to five errors show up repeatedly in SDREC disciplinary files. Knowing them is the cheapest insurance available.

**First, agents forget to renew on time and continue practicing.** The biennial renewal deadline of November 30 catches agents who procrastinate on continuing education. Even one transaction closed on a lapsed license can result in commission disgorgement and disciplinary action. The SDREC has no statutory provision for a post-expiration grace period for active practice.

Second, agents fail to present the Brokerage Disclosure Form at the right time. SDCL 36-21A-69 requires disclosure at first substantive contact, yet agents routinely wait until an offer is being drafted. By that point, the consumer has been operating without knowledge of the agent's agency relationship—a violation that compromises the enforceability of the agency itself.

Third, agents accept referral fees or bonuses from vendors—title companies, mortgage lenders, home warranty providers—without disclosing those payments. South Dakota law and federal RESPA rules both prohibit undisclosed kickbacks. Even a disclosed affiliated business arrangement requires a specific written disclosure; the federal Affiliated Business Arrangement Disclosure form must be provided to the consumer, and the relationship must be documented in the broker's file.

Fourth, agents working on teams blur the line between licensed and unlicensed activity. Unlicensed team assistants who discuss pricing, negotiate terms, or advise clients on contract provisions are performing licensed activities in violation of SDCL 36-21A-14. The supervising broker bears primary responsibility for these violations.

Fifth, agents neglect to keep personal copies of transaction records. SDCL 36-21A-87 requires brokers to maintain records for at least three years. Agents who rely entirely on the brokerage to store files risk being unable to defend themselves in a complaint if the brokerage dissolves, changes hands, or suffers a data loss.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers hold the regulatory center of gravity in South Dakota's licensing framework. Under SDCL 36-21A-73, a supervising broker is responsible for all licensees operating under their license. That responsibility does not dilute because the agent is experienced or because the brokerage runs a "100% commission" model.

| Broker Audit Area | Governing Authority | Audit Frequency Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | ARSD 20:69:04 | Monthly, minimum |
| Agent license status verification | SDCL 36-21A-22 | Quarterly and at each renewal cycle |
| Brokerage Disclosure Form delivery | SDCL 36-21A-69 | Every transaction file |
| Commission payment documentation | SDCL 36-21A-72 | Every disbursement |
| Continuing education compliance | SDCL 36-21A-32 | Annually, well before renewal |
| Advertising and team name compliance | ARSD 20:69:06 | Ongoing, with quarterly spot-checks |

**Brokers who skip monthly trust account reconciliations are gambling with their license and their agents' livelihoods.** The SDREC treats trust account violations with the least tolerance of any infraction category. A single commingling event—where personal or operating funds mix with client trust funds—can trigger immediate suspension.

Advertising is another area where brokers must stay vigilant. ARSD 20:69:06 requires that all advertising include the brokerage's licensed name. Team names, social media handles, and website domains that omit or obscure the brokerage name violate this rule. Brokers should audit digital advertising quarterly, especially on platforms where agents control their own profiles. Understanding [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) becomes obvious once you see how many audit tasks a broker must track across a full roster of agents.

The SDREC also expects brokers to have written office policies covering agency disclosure, trust account handling, and commission disbursement. Those policies should be signed by every affiliated agent at onboarding and re-acknowledged at each renewal. A broker who cannot produce a signed policy manual during a Commission audit faces the inference that no training occurred—an inference that the SDREC regularly uses to justify enhanced penalties.
