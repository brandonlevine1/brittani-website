## Rhode Island Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules Agents Cannot Afford to Ignore

Wire fraud losses in residential real estate exceeded $446 million nationally in 2024, and Rhode Island has not been spared. The Rhode Island wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 impose specific duties on licensed agents, and failing to meet them exposes you and your brokerage to liability that no errors-and-omissions policy will cover without a fight. Rhode Island's regulatory framework combines statutory authority under R.I. Gen. Laws Title 5, Chapter 20.5 with procedural standards enforced by the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation (DBR) and form requirements published by the Rhode Island Association of Realtors (RIAR).

This is not optional guidance. It is a compliance obligation tied to your license.

## The Statutory and Regulatory Framework in Rhode Island

Rhode Island does not have a single standalone wire fraud statute the way some states do. Instead, the obligation flows from several intersecting sources. R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-10 establishes the standard of care for licensed real estate salespersons and brokers, requiring them to act with "reasonable skill and care" in protecting clients from foreseeable harm. The DBR issued Bulletin 2024-3, which explicitly names business email compromise (BEC) attacks as a foreseeable risk that agents must address in writing.

**The Rhode Island Association of Realtors formalized the response by publishing Form WFW-1, the Wire Fraud Warning Advisory, which became part of the standard transaction packet in late 2024.** RIAR updated the form language in January 2026 to reflect FBI IC3 reporting protocols and to add a callback verification acknowledgment section. Agents who are RIAR members are expected to use WFW-1; non-member agents must provide a substantially equivalent written disclosure or face scrutiny during a DBR audit.

The [Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation](https://dbr.ri.gov/divisions/real-estate) maintains oversight authority and has signaled that wire fraud advisory failures will be treated as violations of R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-10(a)(2), which covers failure to disclose material facts affecting a client's interests.

## What Agents Must Do: Step-by-Step Compliance

The compliance requirements break down into three distinct actions: disclose, verify, and document. Each has a specific timing requirement within the Rhode Island transaction timeline.

First, disclosure. You must present Form WFW-1 (or equivalent) to every buyer at or before the signing of the purchase and sale agreement. The form warns buyers that wire instructions received via email may be fraudulent, instructs them to verify all wiring details by phone using a number obtained independently (not from the email containing instructions), and confirms that neither the agent nor brokerage will ever change wire instructions via email. The buyer signs the form and receives a copy.

Second, verification. Before closing, you must remind the buyer in writing (email is acceptable if documented) to call the closing attorney or title company at a verified number to confirm wire routing and account numbers. This reminder must go out at least 48 hours before the scheduled wire transfer. RIAR recommends using the language template in Form WFW-1 Addendum B for this communication.

Third, documentation. The signed WFW-1, any written reminders, and records of callback verification must be retained in the transaction file for a minimum of six years, consistent with R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-17's recordkeeping requirements.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make That Create Liability

Knowing the rule is not the same as following it correctly. Here are the errors that surface most frequently during DBR complaints and brokerage audits.

**Mistake one: presenting the wire fraud advisory too late in the transaction.** Some agents hand the WFW-1 to buyers at the closing table alongside a stack of other papers. By then, the wire has already been sent. The advisory must be presented before the buyer ever receives wiring instructions, which means at or before the P&S signing is the only defensible practice.

Mistake two: relying on a generic brokerage email footer as a substitute for Form WFW-1. A one-line warning in your email signature does not satisfy the DBR's written disclosure standard. The advisory must be a standalone document that the buyer acknowledges with a signature.

Mistake three: sending wire instructions to buyers directly. Agents should never transmit wire routing numbers, account numbers, or any wiring details. That responsibility belongs to the closing attorney or title company. If you forward those details—even as a convenience—you assume liability for their accuracy and for any interception that occurs.

Mistake four: failing to send the 48-hour pre-wire reminder. The initial WFW-1 at contract signing is necessary but not sufficient. The DBR's guidance treats the pre-wire reminder as a separate obligation. Skipping it leaves a gap in your compliance record that opposing counsel will identify immediately in a negligence claim.

Mistake five: not documenting the buyer's verbal callback confirmation. If the buyer tells you they verified the instructions by phone, note the date, time, and number called in your file. Without that notation, you have no evidence the verification loop closed. This connects directly to [best practices for wire instructions verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) that apply across jurisdictions.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, Liability, and License Risk

The penalties for failing to meet Rhode Island wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 are not hypothetical. They fall into three categories.

| Consequence | Triggering Event | Potential Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| DBR disciplinary action | Failure to provide WFW-1 or equivalent | Fine up to $1,000 per violation; license suspension |
| Civil liability (negligence) | Buyer wires funds to fraudster; agent failed to warn | Agent/brokerage liable for actual damages |
| E&O claim denial | Carrier finds agent deviated from standard of care | Agent personally liable for defense costs and judgment |
| Transaction cancellation | Buyer loses funds; deal cannot close | Lost commission; potential breach claim from seller |

Under R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-19, the DBR can impose fines, require additional education, suspend, or revoke a license. A single wire fraud incident where the agent failed to provide the advisory could trigger all of these simultaneously. The buyer's attorney will also file a civil negligence claim against both the agent and the supervising broker, since Rhode Island law imposes vicarious liability on brokers for the acts of their licensees under R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-14.

**The financial exposure dwarfs any commission you would earn on the transaction.** Average wire fraud losses in New England residential transactions range from $75,000 to $350,000. Your E&O policy likely has a $25,000 deductible and may exclude coverage entirely if you failed to follow industry-standard protocols.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry heightened responsibility under Rhode Island law. R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-14 makes the supervising broker responsible for ensuring that affiliated licensees comply with all regulatory requirements. This is not a passive obligation—it requires active oversight systems.

Brokers should audit transaction files quarterly to confirm that every closed deal contains a signed WFW-1, a documented 48-hour pre-wire reminder, and evidence of callback verification. A simple compliance checklist built into your transaction management workflow catches gaps before they become claims. Understanding [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in transaction management applies with particular force to wire fraud protocols.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Signed WFW-1 in file | Every transaction | Original or electronic signature with timestamp |
| 48-hour pre-wire reminder sent | Every transaction | Email copy or text screenshot with date |
| Callback verification noted | Every transaction | Agent's dated file note or buyer confirmation email |
| Agent training completion | Annually | Certificate or attendance record from CE provider |
| Brokerage policy acknowledgment | Annually per agent | Signed policy statement on wire fraud procedures |

Rhode Island continuing education requirements include a mandatory ethics component, and the DBR has approved wire fraud prevention as qualifying CE content. Brokers who make this training mandatory for their agents create an additional layer of defense against vicarious liability claims.

## How Rhode Island Compares to Neighboring States

Rhode Island's requirements sit between the extremes. Connecticut mandates wire fraud disclosure through its Attorneys' Title Standards, while Massachusetts relies primarily on title insurance industry self-regulation. Rhode Island's hybrid approach—statutory duty of care plus RIAR form standardization—gives agents a clear compliance path but also creates clear liability when that path is ignored.

Agents licensed in multiple New England states should note that Rhode Island's 48-hour pre-wire reminder obligation is more specific than what most neighboring states require.
