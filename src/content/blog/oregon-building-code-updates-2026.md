## Oregon Building Code Updates 2026: The Basics Agents Cannot Ignore

The Oregon building code updates 2026 represent the most significant revision to the state's residential construction standards in nearly a decade. Adopted by the Oregon Building Codes Division (BCD) under the Department of Consumer and Business Services, the new rules took effect on October 1, 2025, with full enforcement beginning April 1, 2026. For licensed agents and their brokers, understanding how these changes affect disclosures, listing descriptions, and transaction timelines is not optional—it is a matter of license protection.

Oregon's code adoption cycle is governed by ORS 455.030, which directs the BCD to review and adopt updated editions of the model codes published by the International Code Council (ICC). The 2026 package incorporates the 2024 International Residential Code (IRC), the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), and Oregon-specific amendments addressing seismic resilience, wildfire-resistant construction, and electrification-ready infrastructure. Agents who list new construction, recently renovated homes, or properties with unpermitted additions will feel the impact first.

## What Changed: Energy, Seismic, and Wildfire Standards

**The energy efficiency requirements alone will reshape how agents describe and market newer homes.** Oregon's adoption of the 2024 IECC with state amendments pushes minimum insulation values higher, mandates electric-ready wiring in all new single-family homes, and requires heat pump space conditioning as the baseline mechanical system. Homes permitted after April 1, 2026, must meet these standards or they will not receive a Certificate of Occupancy.

Seismic provisions received the most public attention during the BCD's rulemaking process. The updated code now requires continuous load paths and enhanced hold-down connections for all new wood-frame construction in Seismic Design Categories D and E—essentially the entire western third of the state from Brookings to Astoria. Agents selling new builds in coastal communities should expect buyers and inspectors to ask about these upgrades specifically.

Wildfire construction standards, codified through amendments to ORS 455.610 and aligned with the Oregon State Fire Marshal's Wildfire Risk Map, now require Class A roofing, ember-resistant venting, and ignition-resistant siding for new construction within mapped wildland-urban interface zones. This matters for agents because the [property condition disclosure process](/blog/wisconsin-property-condition-disclosures-2026) now intersects with wildfire-zone designations that buyers may not fully understand without agent guidance.

## Oregon Building Code Updates 2026 and Your Disclosure Obligations

Oregon's seller disclosure framework operates under ORS 105.462 through 105.490. Sellers complete the Seller's Property Disclosure Statement (SPDS), and agents have an independent duty under ORS 696.805(1) to disclose material facts actually known to them. Building code compliance—or non-compliance—falls squarely within this framework when the agent has actual knowledge.

Here is where it gets specific. If you know that a garage conversion, ADU addition, or kitchen remodel was completed without permits, you cannot stay silent simply because the seller checked "unknown" on the SPDS. The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) has disciplined agents under OAR 863-015-0125 for failing to disclose known material facts, including unpermitted construction that predated a code cycle change.

The practical question agents face: does a home built under the 2014 Oregon Residential Specialty Code need to meet 2026 standards? No—grandfathering applies under ORS 455.060, which protects structures lawfully built under prior codes. But unpermitted work completed after October 2025 must comply with the current code, and that distinction will trip up agents who do not ask the right questions during listing intake.

## Forms and Documents: What You Must Get Right

Oregon real estate transactions rely on forms published by Oregon Real Estate Forms LLC (OREF). The forms most directly affected by building code awareness include OREF Form 001 (Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement), OREF Form 003 (Seller's Property Disclosure Statement Addendum), and OREF Form 033 (Property Inspection Contingency Addendum).

| Form | Purpose | Building Code Relevance |
|------|---------|------------------------|
| OREF 001 | Sale Agreement | Section 10 covers property condition representations; code compliance affects "as-is" vs. warranted condition |
| OREF 003 | Disclosure Addendum | Supplements SPDS with additional material facts including permit history |
| OREF 033 | Inspection Contingency | Defines scope of inspections; agents should recommend permit verification here |
| SPDS (Statutory) | Seller's Property Disclosure | ORS 105.464 mandates disclosure of known defects including code violations |
| OREF 049 | ADU Addendum | Specific to accessory dwelling units—directly affected by 2026 standards |

**Agents who skip the OREF 003 addendum when they know about unpermitted work are creating a paper trail of omission.** OREA investigators reviewing complaint files look for exactly this gap between what the agent knew and what the transaction documents reflect. The [deadline management](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) around these disclosures is equally important—late delivery of the SPDS gives buyers an extended revocation period under ORS 105.475.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Building Code Changes

Five errors show up repeatedly in OREA disciplinary actions and E&O claims related to building code compliance. Each one is avoidable with basic awareness and a reliable transaction management system.

**First, agents market unpermitted square footage as living space without qualification.** County assessor records often lag behind actual construction. When your listing describes a "1,800 sq ft home" but permits only cover 1,400 sq ft, you have made a material misrepresentation regardless of whether the seller told you about the addition. Always cross-reference tax lot data with building permit records from the local jurisdiction.

Second, agents assume that a passing home inspection equals code compliance. Home inspectors in Oregon operate under OAR 812-008, and their scope explicitly excludes code compliance verification. An inspector may note a functional defect without identifying that the underlying issue is a code violation. Agents who tell buyers "the inspection came back clean, so there are no code issues" are conflating two entirely different standards.

Third, agents fail to update their knowledge when code cycles change. The 2026 updates to energy and seismic requirements mean that homes permitted in late 2025 may have been built under transitional rules. Agents listing these properties need to confirm which code edition applied at the time of permit issuance—a single phone call to the local building department resolves this.

Fourth, agents in the wildland-urban interface zones neglect to inform buyers about ongoing compliance obligations. Under ORS 476.725 and local ordinances, some wildfire-zone properties carry annual defensible-space maintenance requirements that transfer with the property. Failing to disclose these obligations creates post-closing disputes that generate complaints to OREA.

Fifth, agents do not document their due diligence. When you verify permits, note the date, the jurisdiction, and the person you spoke with in your transaction file. If a [deal breaks down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) months later over a code dispute, your file notes are your defense.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Oregon brokers carry supervisory responsibility under ORS 696.022 and OAR 863-015-0210. The principal broker's obligation extends to ensuring that affiliated agents comply with disclosure requirements, maintain proper documentation, and do not make misrepresentations about property condition—including code compliance claims.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| SPDS delivery timing | Was disclosure delivered within 5 business days of acceptance? | Every transaction |
| OREF 003 usage | Was addendum used when agent had knowledge of material facts beyond SPDS? | Every transaction with known issues |
| MLS listing accuracy | Does square footage match permit records? Are unpermitted features noted? | Listing review before publication |
| Continuing education | Have agents completed required code-related CE in current renewal period? | Annually at renewal |
| Transaction file completeness | Are permit verification notes, inspection reports, and disclosure receipts present? | Quarterly audit minimum |

**Brokers who discover that an agent marketed unpermitted improvements as permitted space face a duty to correct the misrepresentation immediately.** Under OAR 863-015-0125(2), the broker's knowledge is imputed once the information reaches the brokerage. Waiting until closing to address it is not a viable strategy—OREA treats delay as its own violation.

The practical enforcement mechanism most brokers rely on is a standardized [checklist system](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) that flags code-sensitive properties at listing intake. Properties built or renovated within the past 18 months, properties in wildfire zones, properties with ADUs, and properties with visible additions that do not match tax records should all trigger enhanced review protocols.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of non-compliance fall into three categories: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and deal collapse. OREA can impose license suspension or revocation under ORS 696.301 for material misrepresentation or failure to disclose. Fines under ORS 696.990 reach up to $5,000 per violation for individuals.

Civil liability often dwarfs regulatory fines. Oregon courts have awarded damages to buyers who purchased homes with undisclosed code violations, and the agent's E&O insurance does not always cover intentional omissions or knowing misrepresentations. The Oregon Court of Appeals in *Strawn v. Farmers Insurance* (2018) confirmed that policy exclusions for "dishonest, fraudulent, or criminal acts" can deny coverage when an agent knowingly withheld material information.

Deal cancellation is the most common immediate consequence. Buyers who discover unpermitted work or code violations during the inspection period will either renegotiate aggressively or terminate under OREF 033. Sellers lose market time and often face reduced offers on re-listing. The agent loses the commission, the client relationship, and potentially their reputation in a local market where word travels fast.
