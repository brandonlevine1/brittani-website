## Understanding Hawaii Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Every real estate transaction in Hawaii triggers two separate financial obligations at closing that agents routinely underestimate: the conveyance tax and the recording fees. Getting the Hawaii transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 wrong does not just embarrass you at the closing table—it delays recordings, clouds title, and exposes your brokerage to liability. The state legislature has maintained its tiered conveyance tax structure through 2026, but the rates themselves still trip up agents who handle transactions across multiple price points.

Hawaii's conveyance tax is governed by Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 247. Recording fees fall under HRS Chapter 502, administered by the Bureau of Conveyances within the Department of Land and Natural Resources. These two chapters form the statutory backbone that dictates what you owe, when you owe it, and what form you file to prove it.

Unlike many mainland states where transfer taxes are a flat percentage, Hawaii applies a graduated rate schedule that shifts depending on the property's sale price and the seller's residential status. This means a single miscalculation on a $1.2 million Kailua home versus a $4 million Kahala estate produces dramatically different tax obligations.

## The 2026 Conveyance Tax Rate Schedule

**Hawaii's conveyance tax rates have remained unchanged since the 2022 legislative session, but the tiered structure still confuses agents who rarely handle transactions above the $2 million mark.** The tax applies to the actual and full consideration paid for the property, or the fair market value if the consideration is nominal or absent.

Here is the current rate schedule for 2026, split by property value and occupancy status:

| Sale Price Tier | Owner-Occupied Rate | Non-Owner-Occupied Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $600,000 | $0.10 per $100 | $0.15 per $100 |
| $600,001 – $1,000,000 | $0.20 per $100 | $0.25 per $100 |
| $1,000,001 – $2,000,000 | $0.30 per $100 | $0.40 per $100 |
| $2,000,001 – $4,000,000 | $0.50 per $100 | $0.60 per $100 |
| $4,000,001 – $6,000,000 | $0.70 per $100 | $0.80 per $100 |
| $6,000,001 – $10,000,000 | $0.90 per $100 | $1.00 per $100 |
| Over $10,000,000 | $1.00 per $100 | $1.25 per $100 |

The "owner-occupied" rate applies only when the seller has used the property as their principal residence continuously for the three years preceding the sale. The burden of proof rests on the seller, and the claim is made on Form P-64A. Agents who assume their client qualifies without verifying the three-year occupancy requirement create downstream tax disputes with the Department of Taxation.

## Recording Fees at the Bureau of Conveyances

Recording fees in Hawaii depend on whether the property is registered under the Regular System or the Land Court (Torrens) System. Most agents working Oahu transactions encounter both systems, sometimes within the same neighborhood. The distinction matters because Land Court recordings carry higher base fees and additional requirements.

| Document Type | Regular System Fee | Land Court Fee |
|---|---|---|
| Standard document (up to 50 pages) | $41 | $66 |
| Each additional page beyond 50 | $2/page | $2/page |
| Condominium documents | $41 + per-unit fee | $66 + per-unit fee |
| Plan or map (per sheet) | $26 | $51 |

These fees are separate from the conveyance tax and must be paid at the time of recording. The Bureau of Conveyances will not accept a document for recording without simultaneous payment of all applicable fees and taxes. Agents should also anticipate potential ancillary charges for corrective instruments if documents are rejected and must be re-recorded.

## Critical Forms: P-64A, P-64B, and the Recording Package

**Form P-64A (Conveyance Tax Certificate) is the single most important compliance document agents must ensure reaches the closing table correctly.** Filed under HRS Section 247-3, the P-64A accompanies every conveyance document presented for recording. It declares the consideration paid, the applicable tax rate, and whether the transaction qualifies for an exemption or the owner-occupied rate.

Form P-64B exists for transactions claiming a full exemption from conveyance tax under HRS Section 247-3. Common exemptions include transfers between spouses, transfers to trusts where the grantor retains beneficial interest, and government acquisitions. Even when the tax owed is zero, the appropriate form must still be filed—the Bureau will reject a deed submitted without either a P-64A or P-64B attached.

The complete recording package in a standard residential sale includes the deed (warranty or limited warranty), the P-64A or P-64B, FIRPTA documentation where applicable, and the recording fee payment. Missing any component triggers a rejection. The Bureau does not hold incomplete submissions in queue; the entire package comes back, and the agent or title company must resubmit from scratch.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance with Hawaii's conveyance tax requirements carries real teeth. Under HRS Section 247-4, failure to pay the conveyance tax results in a penalty of 25 percent of the unpaid tax, plus interest at the rate of two-thirds of one percent per month. The Department of Taxation can assess these penalties against the grantor, but in practice, delayed closings and rejected recordings create immediate problems for all parties—including the agent who failed to coordinate proper documentation.

> "No document shall be accepted for recordation unless the tax imposed by this chapter has been paid." — HRS Section 247-6

A rejected recording means the deed does not transfer. The buyer does not own the property despite having wired funds. Title insurance cannot issue a final policy. The lender's security interest is not perfected. Every day the recording is delayed exposes the buyer to gap-period risk and the agent to potential claims of negligence.

Agents do not personally pay the conveyance tax, but they bear responsibility for ensuring the transaction file is complete and that the escrow officer or title company has accurate information to calculate the correct tax. Providing wrong sale price figures, misidentifying the seller's occupancy status, or failing to disclose concurrent transactions on the same property can all trigger downstream liability. ## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Hawaii Transfer Taxes

**The first and most frequent error is misclassifying a seller's occupancy status on the P-64A.** An agent lists the property as "owner-occupied" because the seller lived there two years ago, not realizing the statute requires three continuous years of principal residence immediately preceding the conveyance. The result: the Department of Taxation audits the transaction, assesses the difference between the owner-occupied and non-owner-occupied rates, and tacks on the 25 percent penalty.

The second common mistake involves failing to account for the total consideration in multi-parcel or seller-concession deals. HRS Section 247-1 defines "actual and full consideration" broadly. When agents negotiate seller credits, personal property add-ons, or lease-to-purchase arrangements, they sometimes understate the reportable consideration on the P-64A. The Bureau of Conveyances does not audit this at recording, but the Department of Taxation does—sometimes years later.

Third, agents frequently submit recording packages to the wrong system. A property with a Transfer Certificate of Title (TCT) number belongs in the Land Court system. A property with a TMK but no TCT belongs in the Regular System. Submitting to the wrong system results in outright rejection, wasted fees, and resubmission delays that can push closings past contractual deadlines.

Fourth, newer agents forget that Hawaii's conveyance tax is computed on the entire sale price at the applicable tier rate—not incrementally like federal income tax brackets. A $1.5 million non-owner-occupied sale is taxed at $0.40 per $100 on the entire $1,500,000, not at escalating rates across the tiers. This misunderstanding leads to inaccurate buyer cost sheets and seller net estimates that must be corrected mid-transaction.

Fifth, agents neglect to verify whether a concurrent 1031 exchange affects the conveyance tax filing. While exchanges themselves are not exempt from Hawaii conveyance tax, the documentation requirements differ, and failure to properly report the exchange structure on the P-64A can trigger an audit.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Hawaii carry supervisory responsibility under HRS Chapter 467 and the rules of the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HREC). When an agent's transaction file contains an incorrect P-64A or improperly calculated conveyance tax, the broker's license is also at risk if the error reflects a pattern of inadequate supervision.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of closed transaction files, specifically checking that every recording package included either a P-64A or P-64B and that the declared consideration matches the purchase contract.** Discrepancies between the contract price and the P-64A consideration amount represent either an error or a red flag for fraud—both of which demand immediate broker attention.

Compliance checklists work better than memory for this kind of recurring verification. The article on [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) explains the cognitive science behind systematic review versus ad hoc file checks. Brokers who rely on agents to self-report compliance issues will inevitably miss systemic errors until a Department of Taxation notice arrives.

Brokers should also verify that their agents understand the distinction between Regular System and Land Court recordings, maintain current knowledge of the graduated rate schedule, and are not providing tax advice beyond factual disclosure of the statutory rates. Advising a client on whether they qualify for the owner-occupied rate crosses into tax counsel territory that exceeds an agent's scope of practice.

The HREC has disciplined licensees for filing false conveyance tax certificates, treating such conduct as a violation of HRS Section 467-14(20), which prohibits any conduct that demonstrates incompetence, dishonesty, or untrustworthiness. Penalties range from fines to license suspension. The full text of Hawaii's real estate licensing law and administrative rules is available through the [Hawaii Real Estate Commission's official site](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/).
