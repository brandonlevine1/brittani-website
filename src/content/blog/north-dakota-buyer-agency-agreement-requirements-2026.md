## Understanding North Dakota Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The rules governing buyer representation in North Dakota shifted significantly after the NAR settlement reshaped how agents document their relationships with buyers. North Dakota buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 now demand written, signed agreements before agents perform substantive services like touring properties, and the enforcement infrastructure has real teeth. Agents and brokers who treat these changes as optional paperwork are exposing themselves to disciplinary action, lost commissions, and potential civil liability.

North Dakota's regulatory framework sits at the intersection of state statute and industry practice standards. The North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC) administers licensee conduct under [North Dakota Century Code Chapter 43-23.3](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t43c23-3.html), which governs agency relationships, disclosure obligations, and the duties agents owe their clients. The NDAR (North Dakota Association of REALTORS) publishes the standard forms that most brokerages adopt, and MLS policies in the state now align with post-settlement requirements around written buyer agreements.

**Failing to understand the interplay between NDCC 43-23.3, NDAR forms, and MLS rules is the fastest way to land in front of the Commission.** This post breaks down what agents must do, what brokers must enforce, and where the most common compliance failures occur.

## The Statutory Foundation: NDCC 43-23.3 and Agency Disclosure

North Dakota Century Code Chapter 43-23.3 establishes the legal framework for all agency relationships in residential real estate. Section 43-23.3-03 specifically requires licensees to disclose their agency status to all parties in a transaction, and Section 43-23.3-04 outlines the duties owed to clients versus customers. The statute mandates that agency relationships be established through written agreements that specify the scope of representation and the obligations of each party.

Before the NAR settlement took effect, many North Dakota agents relied on informal buyer relationships and oral understandings that technically satisfied the letter of the law but left significant ambiguity around compensation and duties. That era is over. The 2024 settlement terms, adopted by NAR-affiliated MLSs including the statewide North Dakota MLS, now require a written buyer representation agreement to be executed before an agent tours a property with a prospective buyer.

The NDREC has incorporated these expectations into its enforcement posture. Commission staff have confirmed in multiple licensee education sessions throughout 2025 that agents who show properties without signed buyer agreements risk violations of NDCC 43-23.3-03's disclosure requirements, even if no formal complaint is filed.

## Required Forms: What North Dakota Agents Must Use

The NDAR publishes the standardized forms that the vast majority of North Dakota brokerages adopt for buyer representation. The two primary forms agents need to know are:

| Form | Name | Use Case | Key Feature |
|------|------|----------|-------------|
| Form 1-26 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Buyer works with one agent/brokerage exclusively | Requires specific compensation terms and expiration date |
| Form 1A-26 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Buyer retains right to work with multiple agents | Agent compensation only owed if agent is procuring cause |
| Form 2-26 | Agency Disclosure | All transactions regardless of representation type | Must be provided at first substantive contact |
| Form 3-26 | Dual Agency Consent | When listing and buying agents are at same brokerage | Requires informed written consent from both parties |

**Form 1-26 is the workhorse document for most buyer-agent relationships.** It specifies the term of the agreement, the geographic area or property types covered, how the agent will be compensated (including the exact amount or percentage), and the buyer's acknowledgment that compensation is negotiable. The 2026 revision added a mandatory field requiring agents to disclose whether the seller or listing broker has offered to pay any portion of the buyer-agent compensation.

Form 2-26, the Agency Disclosure form, must be presented at or before the first substantive contact with any party. NDCC 43-23.3-03 does not allow agents to wait until an offer is written. If you are meeting a buyer at a coffee shop to discuss their home search, that conversation triggers the disclosure obligation. Agents who are already familiar with [how Wisconsin handles similar disclosure timing](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) will recognize the pattern, though North Dakota's form numbering and specific statutory references differ.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for noncompliance in North Dakota fall into three categories: regulatory, contractual, and civil.

On the regulatory side, the NDREC has authority under NDCC 43-23-11.1 to impose disciplinary sanctions ranging from formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation. Fines can reach $2,000 per violation. The Commission can also require additional continuing education hours as a condition of continued licensure. Between 2024 and mid-2026, the NDREC reported a measurable uptick in buyer-side complaints related to agency disclosure failures, and several cases resulted in consent agreements requiring agents to complete remedial education.

Contractually, a buyer who never signed a buyer agency agreement has no enforceable obligation to compensate the agent. If a dispute arises over procuring cause or commission entitlement, the agent without a signed Form 1-26 or 1A-26 is at a severe disadvantage. North Dakota courts have historically held that oral agreements for real estate brokerage services are enforceable under certain circumstances, but proving the terms of an oral agreement is expensive and uncertain.

**On the civil liability front, an agent who provides substantive services without proper disclosure and agreement documentation may face claims of breach of fiduciary duty or negligent misrepresentation.** The buyer could argue they did not understand the agent was acting as their representative and therefore did not receive informed consent about dual agency or compensation conflicts. These claims are difficult to defend when no written agreement exists.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in North Dakota

Five recurring errors surface in NDREC disciplinary records and brokerage audit findings related to buyer agency agreements.

The first and most frequent mistake is timing. Agents meet a buyer at an open house, start showing additional properties, and only present the buyer agency agreement when an offer is imminent. By that point, the agent has already performed weeks of substantive services without a signed agreement. The obligation attaches before the first tour, not at the offer stage.

The second mistake involves compensation ambiguity. Form 1-26 requires agents to state the specific amount or percentage of compensation owed. Some agents leave this field blank or write "TBD," which renders the compensation term unenforceable and potentially violates MLS participation rules requiring transparent compensation disclosures. Agents who have followed [the evolution of commission structure changes in neighboring states](/blog/wisconsin-commission-structure-changes-2026) know this issue is not unique to North Dakota, but the NDREC has been particularly direct about enforcing it.

Third, agents confuse the Agency Disclosure (Form 2-26) with the Buyer Agency Agreement (Form 1-26). These are separate documents with separate purposes. The disclosure informs the buyer about the types of agency relationships available in North Dakota. The agreement creates the actual agency relationship. Presenting one does not satisfy the requirement for the other.

Fourth, agents fail to specify a reasonable expiration date. The NAR settlement guidelines require buyer agreements to include a defined term. Some North Dakota agents have been writing agreements with 12-month or even indefinite terms, which the NDREC views as potentially unconscionable and which buyers are increasingly refusing to sign. Most experienced agents and legal counsel recommend terms of 90 to 180 days.

Fifth, agents neglect to provide copies. NDCC 43-23.3 and NDREC administrative rules require that all parties receive copies of signed documents promptly. An agent who has the buyer sign Form 1-26 on a tablet at a property showing but never sends the executed copy to the buyer has not fully complied.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear direct supervisory liability under NDCC 43-23-11.2 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. A broker who fails to implement systems for tracking buyer agency compliance is not just risking their agents' licenses — they are risking their own.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency | Risk if Missed |
|------------|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| Signed Form 1-26 or 1A-26 on file | Executed before first property tour | Every new buyer relationship | Commission disputes, NDREC complaint |
| Form 2-26 delivered and signed | Presented at first substantive contact | Every new client and customer interaction | Disclosure violation under NDCC 43-23.3-03 |
| Compensation terms completed | Specific amount or percentage, not blank or TBD | Before any offer is submitted | MLS policy violation, unenforceable agreement |
| Expiration date present and reasonable | Defined term, typically 90-180 days | At agreement execution | Potential unconscionability claim |
| Copy delivered to buyer | Electronic or physical copy sent promptly | Within 24 hours of execution | Administrative rule violation |

Brokers should build a file review checkpoint that triggers before any offer is submitted on behalf of a buyer. If the transaction file does not contain a signed buyer agency agreement with complete compensation terms and a valid expiration date, the offer should not go out. This is not excessive caution — it is the minimum standard the NDREC expects from supervising brokers.

**Quarterly audits of random transaction files remain the most effective way to catch systemic problems before the Commission does.** Brokers who rely solely on agents self-reporting compliance are the ones who end up in consent agreement negotiations with NDREC staff. ## Dual Agency and Designated Agency Complications

North Dakota permits both dual agency and designated agency, but each adds a layer of buyer agreement complexity that agents frequently mishandle. Under NDCC 43-23.3-06, dual agency requires informed written consent from both the buyer and seller using Form 3-26. This consent must be obtained before the dual agency situation arises — not after the buyer has already submitted an offer on a property listed by the same brokerage.

Designated agency, where the broker assigns separate agents within the firm to represent the buyer and seller independently, requires its own documentation. The broker must ensure that the designated buyer's agent operates under a valid buyer agency agreement that does not conflict with the duties owed to the seller client. Agents who have studied [how dual agency is handled under Wisconsin's framework](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026) will find North Dakota's structure similar in principle but distinct in form requirements and Commission enforcement patterns.

The most dangerous scenario occurs when an agent operating under a non-exclusive buyer agreement (Form 1A-26) encounters a property listed by their own brokerage. The non-exclusive agreement may not contain adequate dual agency consent language, forcing the agent to obtain separate dual agency consent and potentially renegotiate compensation terms mid-transaction. Brokers should address this scenario in their office policy manual and train agents to flag it immediately.
