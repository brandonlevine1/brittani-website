## Understanding the Arkansas Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The **Arkansas commission & licensing rules 2026** framework affects every licensee in the state, from newly activated salespersons to managing brokers overseeing dozens of agents. The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) governs licensing, commission disbursement, advertising, and disciplinary actions under the authority of the Arkansas Real Estate License Law, codified primarily in Ark. Code Ann. Title 17, Chapter 42. Agents who ignore these rules risk fines, license revocation, and personal liability that no errors-and-omissions policy will cover.

This year brings heightened enforcement priorities from AREC, particularly around commission transparency following the NAR settlement ripple effects and updated buyer representation agreements. If you hold an active Arkansas license, you need to understand exactly how commissions flow, what forms protect you, and where the regulatory tripwires sit.

## How Commissions Are Legally Disbursed in Arkansas

**Arkansas law is unambiguous: all commission payments must pass through the principal broker.** Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311 prohibits any salesperson or associate broker from receiving compensation directly from a consumer, another brokerage, or a title company. The principal broker receives the funds and then disburses the agreed-upon split to the agent.

This rule applies regardless of how the commission was negotiated. A buyer-broker agreement that specifies a flat fee, a percentage, or a bonus still requires the money to route through the broker's trust or operating account first. Violations are treated as unlicensed activity, which carries penalties separate from standard commission disputes.

| Scenario | Legal? | Statutory Basis |
|----------|--------|-----------------|
| Broker receives commission, splits to agent | Yes | Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311 |
| Agent receives payment directly from seller at closing | No | Violation of 17-42-311 |
| Agent receives referral fee from out-of-state brokerage | No (must go through broker) | AREC Rule 10.10 |
| Broker pays agent via 1099 per ICA agreement | Yes | Standard practice |
| Unlicensed assistant receives transaction bonus | No | Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-103 |

Title companies in Arkansas should be instructed on the HUD or Closing Disclosure to issue commission checks payable to the brokerage entity, not the individual agent. Any deviation puts both the agent and the title company at risk of an AREC complaint.

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Deadlines for 2026

Every Arkansas real estate license expires on September 30. AREC does not grant grace periods, and an expired license means you cannot legally practice, earn commissions, or hold yourself out as a licensee. The renewal process requires completion of six hours of annual continuing education, including the mandatory AREC core course that changes each license year.

For 2026, the core course covers updated buyer representation rules, commission disclosure obligations, and fair housing enforcement trends. The remaining hours can be fulfilled through elective courses approved by AREC. All CE must be completed before you submit your renewal application—not concurrently.

Pre-licensing education in Arkansas totals 60 classroom hours for salespersons and an additional 30 hours for broker candidates. The state exam is administered by PSI and includes both a national portion and an Arkansas-specific portion. Passing scores are 70 percent on each section, and candidates have one year from course completion to pass the exam before their education expires.

## Forms and Documents That Protect Your License

Arkansas agents work with specific AREC-promulgated forms that carry regulatory weight beyond a typical contract template. Failing to use the correct form—or modifying one improperly—can trigger disciplinary action.

**AREC Form 1 (Listing Agreement)** establishes the agency relationship between a seller and the listing brokerage. It must include the commission rate or amount, the listing period, and the broker's authority to cooperate with other brokerages. Omitting the cooperation clause does not relieve you of disclosure obligations under the new buyer-broker compensation rules.

AREC Form 5 (Buyer Agency Agreement) has grown in importance since mid-2024. This form specifies how the buyer's agent will be compensated, whether through seller concessions, direct buyer payment, or a combination. Under current AREC guidance, agents must have a signed Form 5 before showing properties if they intend to represent the buyer in any capacity.

AREC Form 7 (Property Disclosure) is the seller's responsibility to complete, but the listing agent bears an obligation to ensure it is provided to the buyer's side before an offer is accepted. Failure to deliver Form 7 can give buyers a statutory right to rescind within a specified period after receipt.

Other critical forms include AREC Form 3 (Dual/Designated Agency Disclosure) and AREC Form 6 (Transaction Brokerage Disclosure). Using the wrong agency disclosure form for the relationship you actually have with a client is one of the most common audit findings AREC reports.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Under Arkansas Rules

**Mistake 1: Advertising without broker approval or proper identification.** AREC Rule 10.8 requires that all advertising—including social media posts, yard signs, and digital listings—identify the brokerage name in a clear and conspicuous manner. An agent who posts a listing on Instagram with only their personal brand name and no brokerage reference is in violation. Fines start at $500 per occurrence and can escalate to license suspension for repeated offenses.

Mistake 2: Failing to disclose dual agency before it arises. Arkansas permits dual agency, but only with written informed consent from both parties using AREC Form 3. Agents who realize mid-transaction that their brokerage represents both sides and then retroactively seek consent are violating Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-604. This mistake has resulted in deal rescissions and civil suits where buyers claim they would not have waived their right to exclusive representation.

Mistake 3: Holding earnest money in an agent's personal account. Under AREC Rule 10.6, earnest money must be deposited in the broker's trust account within a specific timeframe—typically within three business days of acceptance unless the contract specifies otherwise. Agents who "hold" checks in their desk drawer or deposit them into a personal checking account face immediate license suspension proceedings.

Mistake 4: Practicing on an expired or inactive license. This happens more often than you might expect, particularly among agents who change brokerages. The transfer process requires AREC Form 2 (Transfer of License), and until that form is processed, the agent's license is inactive. Transactions conducted during the gap are considered unlicensed activity, and any commissions earned are subject to disgorgement.

Mistake 5: Modifying AREC-promulgated forms without authorization. Only attorneys may draft or substantially modify contract language in Arkansas. Agents who cross out clauses, insert addenda that create new legal obligations, or rewrite commission terms on standard forms are engaging in the unauthorized practice of law. AREC and the Arkansas Bar Association both investigate these complaints, sometimes simultaneously.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry vicarious liability for their agents' actions under Arkansas law. Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311(c) explicitly states that the principal broker is responsible for the supervision of all licensees affiliated with the brokerage. This is not a theoretical obligation—AREC regularly disciplines brokers for failing to supervise even when the broker had no knowledge of the violation.

**A quarterly audit of the following areas can prevent most enforcement actions.** First, review all trust account records to verify timely deposit of earnest money and proper disbursement logs. AREC requires that trust accounts be reconciled monthly, and records must be available for inspection at any time during business hours.

Second, audit advertising compliance across all platforms. This includes checking agent websites, social media profiles, third-party listing portals, and print materials for proper brokerage identification. One agent's non-compliant Facebook ad can trigger a brokerage-wide investigation if AREC receives a complaint.

Third, verify that every active transaction file contains the correct agency disclosure form signed before substantive negotiations began. Missing disclosures are the single most common deficiency found during AREC random audits. Brokers should implement a file review process [similar to a weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that catches gaps before closing rather than after a complaint.

| Audit Area | Frequency | AREC Rule Reference | Common Deficiency |
|------------|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Rule 10.6 | Late deposits, missing reconciliation logs |
| Advertising compliance | Quarterly | Rule 10.8 | Missing brokerage name on social media |
| Agency disclosure in file | Per transaction | Rule 10.3 | Form signed after offer submitted |
| CE completion verification | Annually (before Sept 30) | Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-305 | Agent practicing with expired CE |
| License transfer documentation | At onboarding | Form 2 requirements | Agent active before transfer processed |

Fourth, confirm that all buyer-agency agreements are executed before property showings. The post-settlement environment has made this a high-visibility enforcement target, and AREC has signaled that complaints related to buyer compensation transparency will receive priority review in 2026.

## Penalties and Enforcement Actions for Non-Compliance

AREC's disciplinary authority includes fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension or revocation, mandatory additional education, and probationary periods with enhanced supervision requirements. The Commission publishes enforcement actions in its quarterly newsletter and on its [official website](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/), which means reputational damage compounds the financial penalty.

Civil liability adds another layer. Buyers or sellers who can demonstrate that an agent's failure to comply with licensing rules caused them financial harm may bring suit under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-501. Courts have awarded actual damages, attorney's fees, and in some cases, voided transactions entirely—returning earnest money and requiring the seller to refund the buyer regardless of the agent's intent.

Criminal penalties are rare but not impossible. Practicing without a valid license is a Class A misdemeanor under Arkansas law, carrying potential jail time and fines separate from AREC's administrative process. Brokers who knowingly allow unlicensed individuals to conduct real estate activities face the same criminal exposure.
