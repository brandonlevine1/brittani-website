## Understanding West Virginia Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of West Virginia who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by a single statute. It is answered by a combination of state law, contract language, lender requirements, and county-level custom that varies from the Eastern Panhandle to the southern coalfields. Agents who treat closing cost allocation as a boilerplate afterthought expose themselves and their clients to last-minute disputes that kill deals.

West Virginia is an attorney-closing state in practice for many counties, though no statute mandates attorney involvement for all transactions. The West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC), operating under [W. Va. Code Chapter 30, Article 40](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/wvcode/code.cfm?chap=30&art=40), governs licensee conduct and sets the standard of care agents owe when advising on transactional costs. Failing to properly allocate and disclose closing costs can trigger license complaints under the Commission's disciplinary authority.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Closing Costs

**West Virginia does not have a single statute dictating who pays which closing cost.** Instead, the allocation arises from a patchwork of laws, each governing a specific fee. The documentary transfer tax under W. Va. Code 11-22-2 imposes a $1.10-per-$500 excise tax on real property transfers, payable at recording. By longstanding custom in most West Virginia counties, the seller pays this tax — but the contract can reassign it.

Recording fees under W. Va. Code 59-1-2 apply to deed and mortgage filings. Buyers customarily pay these because the recorded deed and mortgage protect the buyer's title and lender's lien position. Title examination costs, meanwhile, are typically buyer expenses since the buyer's lender orders the title search.

The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) still governs federal disclosure requirements, meaning the Closing Disclosure must itemize every fee and identify who pays it. Agents should cross-reference the CD against the executed purchase agreement to catch discrepancies before the closing table.

## Forms and Contract Provisions That Control Allocation

**Most West Virginia agents use the West Virginia Association of REALTORS (WVAR) standard purchase agreement forms.** The current WVAR Residential Sales Contract includes specific blanks for closing cost allocation, seller concessions, and prorations. Agents must complete these fields — leaving them blank invites disputes and potential liability under WVREC's standards of practice.

The WVAR form includes a section for seller-paid closing costs expressed as either a fixed dollar amount or a percentage of the sale price. When writing a seller concession, agents should confirm the buyer's lender caps: FHA allows up to 6%, VA up to 4%, and conventional loans cap at 3-9% depending on LTV and occupancy. Writing a concession that exceeds lender limits does not void the contract, but it forces a last-minute amendment that can delay closing.

Agents should also pay attention to the proration section. Real property taxes in West Virginia are paid in arrears, and the proration date (typically the day of closing) determines how credits flow between buyer and seller. Under W. Va. Code 11A-1-3, taxes become a lien on July 1 of each year, which affects the proration calculation. Mishandling this creates title issues downstream.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The WVREC has authority under W. Va. Code 30-40-19 to discipline licensees for incompetence, negligence, or failure to account for client funds. A closing cost error can trigger any of these categories. The Commission can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require continuing education remediation.

Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face civil liability. If a buyer arrives at closing and discovers $4,000 in unexpected costs because the agent failed to explain or document the allocation, that buyer has grounds for a negligence claim against the agent and potentially the brokerage under respondeat superior. The resulting errors-and-omissions insurance claim raises premiums for the entire office.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. When closing cost disputes arise at the settlement table, buyers walk. ## Common Mistakes West Virginia Agents Make on Closing Costs

**The following five errors appear repeatedly in WVREC complaint files and brokerage audit findings.**

First, agents fail to specify the allocation in writing. Verbal assurances that "the seller will cover your closing costs" mean nothing if the purchase agreement does not contain the specific dollar figure or percentage. The WVAR contract form has the fields — agents simply skip them or leave them vague.

Second, agents write seller concessions exceeding lender limits without confirming the cap with the loan officer. This forces an addendum, delays closing, and sometimes reduces the effective purchase price in a way that causes appraisal problems. A five-minute call to the lender before submitting the offer prevents this entirely.

Third, agents confuse the documentary excise tax with recording fees. These are different charges with different payors by custom. Telling a seller they owe recording fees, or telling a buyer they owe excise tax, creates confusion at closing and erodes client confidence. Agents who understand the distinction between these charges — and can cite the relevant code sections — demonstrate competence that protects their license.

Fourth, agents neglect to address the tax proration method in the contract. West Virginia's arrears-based tax system means the proration calculation depends on whether the parties use calendar-year or fiscal-year proration. The WVAR form allows agents to specify this, but many leave it to the closing agent's discretion, which invites inconsistency.

Fifth, agents fail to coordinate with the settlement agent on the Closing Disclosure timeline. TRID requires the buyer receive the CD at least three business days before closing. When agents introduce new cost allocations or concession changes inside that window, it triggers a new three-day waiting period and pushes closing. Proper [deadline management](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) prevents this cascade.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in West Virginia bear supervisory liability under W. Va. Code 30-40-20. The statute holds the broker responsible for the conduct of affiliated licensees, making closing cost compliance a brokerage-level concern rather than merely an individual agent issue.

Brokers should audit every purchase agreement for completed closing cost fields. A blank concession line or an unspecified proration method should trigger a mandatory correction before the contract goes to the other party. This is not micromanagement — it is risk management required by statute.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Seller concession field | Dollar amount or percentage specified | Lender rejection, deal delay |
| Excise tax allocation | Identified payor matches contract | Closing table dispute |
| Proration method | Calendar vs. fiscal year specified | Credit miscalculation |
| CD review before closing | Agent confirmed costs match contract | TRID violation, client complaint |
| Disclosure timing | Agency disclosure delivered per WVREC rules | License complaint, civil liability |

Brokers should also verify that agents are providing [proper agency disclosures](/blog/west-virginia-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) at first substantive contact, because the duty to advise on closing costs flows from the agency relationship itself. Without a properly established agency, the agent's cost guidance may lack the fiduciary foundation that protects both parties.

Training is not optional. Brokers who rely solely on agents' pre-licensing education to cover closing cost allocation are accepting unnecessary risk. Quarterly file reviews that specifically examine how closing costs were handled — documented, communicated, and reconciled against the CD — establish a defensible supervision record if the WVREC ever investigates.

## Seller Disclosure Obligations That Intersect With Closing Costs

West Virginia's [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) under W. Va. Code 36-6A do not directly address closing costs. However, when a property has known conditions that will generate additional costs at closing — such as outstanding liens, HOA transfer fees, or required repairs per contract — the seller's disclosure form may indirectly signal costs that affect the closing statement.

Agents representing sellers should review the Property Condition Disclosure Statement early and identify any disclosed conditions that will generate closing-table charges. A disclosed roof issue that becomes a repair credit, for example, must appear on the CD and should be documented in an addendum before the disclosure deadline expires.
