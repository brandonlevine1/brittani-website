## North Dakota Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026: Statute, Forms, and Real Liability

Getting property condition and environmental disclosures wrong in North Dakota does not just embarrass you at the closing table — it can unwind an entire deal and expose your license to disciplinary action. The rules around North Dakota property condition & environmental disclosures 2026 have not changed dramatically from prior years, but enforcement patterns and buyer expectations have shifted enough that agents who coast on memory alone are taking unnecessary risks. This post breaks down the statutes, the forms, the consequences, and the specific mistakes that keep showing up in broker audits across the state.

North Dakota's disclosure framework is thinner than many states, which paradoxically makes compliance harder. When a state hands you a 10-page form with 150 checkboxes, the path is obvious. North Dakota gives you a shorter form and broader statutory language — and expects you to fill in the gaps with professional judgment.

## The Statutory Foundation: N.D.C.C. 47-10-02.1

North Dakota Century Code Section [47-10-02.1](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t47c10.pdf) is the backbone of residential property condition disclosure in the state. It requires sellers of residential real property to deliver a written disclosure statement to prospective buyers before or at the time an offer is accepted. The statute applies to properties with one to four dwelling units.

**The disclosure must cover the condition of the property's structure, systems, and any known material defects.** This includes roofing, foundation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, water and sewer systems, and any history of flooding, drainage problems, or environmental hazards. Sellers are not required to conduct inspections — the obligation is limited to disclosing what they actually know.

Exemptions exist under the same statute for specific transaction types. Foreclosure sales, transfers pursuant to court order, transfers between spouses or co-owners, and sales by executors or administrators of an estate are all carved out. Agents should never assume an exemption applies without verifying the transaction type against the statutory list.

## The Forms Agents Actually Use

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission does not mandate a single statewide disclosure form by form number the way some states do. Instead, most agents rely on the disclosure form published by the North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR), which is typically titled the "Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement." Brokerages that use transaction management platforms often have this form loaded into their system as a standard template.

| Form / Document | Source | Required By |
|---|---|---|
| Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement | NDAR / brokerage template | N.D.C.C. 47-10-02.1 |
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (pre-1978 homes) | EPA / federal form | 42 U.S.C. 4852d; 40 CFR 745 |
| Seller's Disclosure of Flood Zone Status | Typically included in NDAR form | Best practice; may be material fact |
| Environmental Hazard Addendum (if applicable) | Brokerage-specific | Recommended for properties near oil/gas operations |

For pre-1978 properties, the federal lead-based paint disclosure is mandatory under 42 U.S.C. 4852d. This is not a state form — it comes from the EPA — but North Dakota agents are just as bound by it as agents in any other state. The form requires the seller to disclose any known lead-based paint or hazards, provide available records or reports, and give the buyer a 10-day opportunity to conduct a lead inspection unless the buyer waives that right in writing.

## Environmental Disclosures: Oil, Gas, and the Bakken Factor

North Dakota's unique energy economy makes environmental disclosure a more nuanced issue here than in most states. Properties in the western part of the state — particularly in Williams, McKenzie, Mountrail, and Dunn counties — may sit near active oil wells, saltwater disposal sites, pipeline corridors, or land that has experienced produced-water spills. None of these conditions trigger a standalone state-mandated environmental disclosure form, but they almost certainly qualify as material facts under N.D.C.C. 47-10-02.1.

**Agents representing sellers near active oil and gas operations should proactively ask about mineral rights severance, surface use agreements, and any known contamination events.** The North Dakota Department of Environmental Quality (NDDEQ) maintains public records of spill reports and remediation sites. A seller who knows about a spill on or adjacent to the property and fails to disclose it is exposed to fraud claims — and so is the agent who knew or should have known.

Radon is another environmental concern that agents frequently overlook. The EPA classifies much of North Dakota as Zone 1, meaning predicted average indoor radon levels exceed 4 pCi/L. There is no state law requiring radon testing or disclosure, but failing to advise buyers about radon risk — especially when the property has no mitigation system — can generate post-closing complaints. Similar issues arise in other states; for comparison, you can see how [Wisconsin handles property condition disclosures](/blog/wisconsin-property-condition-disclosures-2026) with a more prescriptive form.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of disclosure failures in North Dakota break into three categories: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

On the regulatory side, the North Dakota Real Estate Commission has the authority under N.D.C.C. 43-23-11.1 to impose disciplinary action for violations of real estate law, including failure to disclose material facts. Penalties range from formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation. The Commission can also impose fines, and disciplinary actions become part of the agent's public record.

Civil liability is where the real financial damage happens. A buyer who discovers undisclosed defects after closing can sue the seller and, depending on the facts, the listing agent and brokerage. North Dakota courts have recognized claims for fraud, negligent misrepresentation, and breach of the implied duty of good faith in real estate transactions. Damages can include repair costs, diminution in property value, and in egregious cases, punitive damages.

| Failure Type | Potential Consequence | Who Bears Liability |
|---|---|---|
| Seller omits known defect on disclosure form | Buyer rescinds or sues post-closing | Seller; listing agent if they knew or should have known |
| Agent fails to deliver lead paint disclosure | EPA fine up to $19,507 per violation | Agent and brokerage individually |
| No disclosure delivered at all (non-exempt sale) | Buyer may void purchase agreement | Seller; listing agent for procedural failure |
| Agent misrepresents property condition | License discipline; civil damages | Agent and brokerage under vicarious liability |

On the transactional level, a missing or materially incomplete disclosure gives the buyer a right to cancel in many cases. If the buyer discovers the omission before closing, they can walk away — and potentially recover earnest money. If they discover it after closing, the litigation clock starts. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the transaction timeline can help agents spot disclosure gaps before they become deal-killers.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With North Dakota Disclosures

**First, agents routinely treat the disclosure form as the seller's problem alone.** They hand it over, tell the seller to fill it out, and never review what comes back. Under North Dakota law, agents have an independent duty not to misrepresent material facts. If a listing agent sees a water stain on a basement wall but the seller marks "No" next to water intrusion, that agent has a problem. You do not get to hide behind the seller's signature.

Second, agents confuse "as-is" sales with disclosure-exempt sales. Selling a property "as-is" does not eliminate the seller's obligation to disclose known defects under N.D.C.C. 47-10-02.1. An "as-is" clause affects the buyer's right to demand repairs — it does not waive the seller's duty to tell the truth. This misconception has been the basis of multiple complaints filed with the Commission.

Third, agents in western North Dakota skip environmental due diligence questions because they assume the buyer will handle it during inspection. That is not a legal defense. If the seller knows that a saltwater spill was remediated on the property in 2019, that fact belongs on the disclosure form regardless of what the buyer's inspector might find independently.

Fourth, agents fail to get the disclosure signed and delivered before or at the time the offer is accepted. Late delivery — handing it over at closing or, worse, after — violates the statute's timing requirement and gives the buyer an argument for rescission. The timing issue is one of the most common reasons [agents miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) throughout a transaction.

Fifth, agents neglect to keep a copy of the signed disclosure in the transaction file. If a dispute arises two years later, the agent who cannot produce a signed, dated disclosure is at a serious disadvantage — even if one was actually delivered.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in North Dakota bear supervisory responsibility under N.D.C.C. 43-23-11.1 and the Commission's administrative rules. That means disclosure compliance is not optional oversight — it is an affirmative duty.

**Every transaction file should be audited for three things: a signed seller disclosure form with a date that precedes or matches the accepted offer date, a lead-based paint disclosure for any property built before 1978, and documentation of any environmental issues the agent flagged or discussed with the seller.** If any of these are missing, the broker needs to catch it before closing, not after a complaint lands on their desk.

Brokers should also verify that their agents are using current versions of disclosure forms. The NDAR periodically updates its forms, and using an outdated version — one that omits a category of defects now considered standard — creates a gap in coverage. A quarterly form audit takes less than an hour and can prevent months of legal headaches.

Training is the other enforcement lever. Brokers who run a 30-minute disclosure refresher at the start of each year — covering statutory requirements, common pitfalls, and recent Commission enforcement actions — build a compliance culture that reduces risk across the office. The North Dakota Real Estate Commission publishes enforcement actions and advisory opinions that make excellent training material.

## Tying It All Together for 2026

The disclosure framework in North Dakota rewards agents who are methodical and punishes those who are casual. The forms are not complicated. The statute is not ambiguous. The failures almost always come down to process — someone skipped a step, assumed the other party would handle it, or did not read what the seller actually wrote.
