## Understanding Kentucky Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Kentucky treats agency relationships with a statutory specificity that catches agents off guard, especially those licensed in multiple states. The Kentucky dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 framework is governed primarily by KRS Chapter 324 and the administrative regulations issued by the [Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC)](https://krec.ky.gov). Failing to comply does not merely risk a slap on the wrist—it can unwind an entire transaction and expose both the agent and the brokerage to civil damages.

The distinction between dual agency and designated agency in this state is not academic. It determines what you can say, what you must withhold, and how your broker's supervisory responsibilities shift mid-transaction. Agents who treat the two as interchangeable are misreading the statute and gambling with their license.

Kentucky permits both forms, but each carries a different set of obligations and a different disclosure path. The rest of this post breaks down what the statutes actually say, which forms you must use, and where agents routinely make errors that KREC investigators flag during audits.

## The Statutory Foundation: KRS 324.360 and 201 KAR 11:121

**KRS 324.360 is the backbone of Kentucky's agency disclosure scheme, and it does not allow for shortcuts.** The statute mandates that licensees disclose their agency relationship to all parties at the earliest practicable time and before any confidential information is exchanged. This is not a suggestion tied to offer presentation—it triggers the moment you begin substantive conversation with a party.

Under KRS 324.360(5), dual agency requires the informed written consent of all parties to the transaction. The consent must be obtained before the dual agency relationship begins, not after. Retroactive consent is not recognized, and KREC has issued guidance letters confirming that post-hoc ratification does not cure a disclosure failure.

Administrative regulation 201 KAR 11:121 supplements the statute by specifying the duties owed under each type of agency. A dual agent owes limited duties to both parties—primarily confidentiality, accounting, and disclosure of material facts—but cannot advocate for either side's negotiating position. A designated agent, by contrast, retains full fiduciary duties to their assigned client, including the duty to promote their client's best interests during negotiations.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: A Side-by-Side Comparison

The practical differences between these two relationships determine how you communicate with your client once both sides of a transaction involve your brokerage. The table below clarifies the operational boundaries.

| Obligation | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Written consent required | Yes, from both parties | Yes, from both parties |
| Agent can advocate on price | No | Yes, for assigned client only |
| Confidential info shared across sides | Prohibited | Prohibited between designated agents |
| Broker supervision role | Neutral oversight | Must ensure no information leakage |
| Form required | Form 300 (Consent to Dual Agency) | Broker designation letter + Form 200 disclosure |

**Designated agency is the safer path for most brokerages because it preserves advocacy.** Under designated agency, the principal broker assigns one licensee to represent the buyer and a separate licensee to represent the seller. Each designated agent then owes full fiduciary duties—loyalty, obedience, disclosure, confidentiality, accounting, and reasonable care—exclusively to their assigned party.

The broker, however, steps into a neutral supervisory position. The broker cannot coach either designated agent on negotiation strategy and must implement systems to prevent confidential information from passing between the two sides. This is sometimes called an "information barrier" or "ethical wall," and KREC expects brokers to document how they enforce it.

## Required Forms and Timing of Disclosure

Kentucky agents work within a form framework prescribed by KREC. The primary documents relevant to agency disclosure and consent are:

Form 200 serves as the initial agency disclosure document. It must be presented to prospective buyers and sellers at first substantive contact—not at the showing, not at the offer, but at the point where a party could reasonably believe representation exists. Form 200 confirms the type of relationship being offered and the duties that flow from it.

Form 300 is the Consent to Dual Agency. Both the buyer and the seller must sign this form before dual agency commences. The form explicitly states that the agent will not be able to advocate for either party's negotiating position and that confidential information previously shared under a single-party representation cannot be disclosed to the other side.

When a brokerage elects designated agency instead, there is no single KREC-prescribed "Form 301" equivalent. The broker must issue a written designation letter identifying which licensee represents which party, and each party must acknowledge the arrangement. Many brokerages incorporate this acknowledgment into their listing agreements and buyer agency agreements, referencing the designated agency provisions of KRS 324.360. If your brokerage uses a custom form for designated agency, it must conform to the statutory requirements—KREC has stated in advisory opinions that omitting any element of the disclosure voids the consent.

## Common Mistakes Kentucky Agents Make

Five errors appear repeatedly in KREC disciplinary actions and audit findings. Each one is preventable with better systems and earlier attention to paperwork.

**First, agents obtain dual agency consent too late.** The most common violation KREC documents involves agents who present Form 300 alongside the purchase agreement rather than before substantive negotiations begin. By the time an offer is being drafted, confidential information has already been exchanged, and the consent cannot retroactively cure that breach. The statute is clear: consent must precede the dual agency relationship.

Second, agents confuse "in-house" transactions with dual agency. When two agents from the same brokerage represent opposite sides, that is not automatically dual agency—it is designated agency, assuming the broker properly designates each licensee. Agents who fail to recognize this distinction often skip the disclosure step entirely, assuming the firm's listing agreement covers it. It does not.

Third, agents disclose verbally but never follow up with the required written form. Kentucky law demands written consent. A conversation at a kitchen table, no matter how thorough, does not satisfy KRS 324.360(5). If the signed form is missing from the file, KREC treats it as if no consent was obtained.

Fourth, brokers fail to designate in writing. The designation must be documented—not simply assumed because two different agents hold the buyer and seller relationships. Without a written designation letter, the brokerage defaults to dual agency status, which means neither agent can advocate. This often surprises agents who believed they were operating as designated agents the entire time.

Fifth, agents share pricing strategy across sides. Even sophisticated agents occasionally let confidential information slip when both parties are clients of the same brokerage. Under dual agency, the agent cannot reveal either party's bottom line. Under designated agency, the agents cannot share that information with each other—and the broker cannot serve as a conduit. Any breach here triggers both disciplinary exposure and potential civil liability for damages.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, License Actions, and Deal Death

KRS 324.160 authorizes KREC to impose disciplinary action against any licensee who violates the agency disclosure requirements. Penalties include license suspension or revocation, fines of up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory education requirements, and public censure that appears on the licensee's KREC record.

Beyond regulatory penalties, the civil exposure is significant. A party who was not properly informed of a dual agency relationship can seek rescission of the purchase contract. Kentucky courts have upheld rescission claims where the agent failed to obtain written dual agency consent, reasoning that the party could not have given informed consent to terms negotiated under a conflicted representation. The agent's commission is forfeited in such cases, and the brokerage may face liability for the other party's damages—including lost appreciation, inspection costs, and relocation expenses.

Errors and omissions insurance policies typically cover negligent acts, but many policies contain exclusions for "knowing violations" of licensing law. If KREC determines the agent knowingly operated as a dual agent without consent, the E&O carrier may deny coverage, leaving the agent personally exposed. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Principal brokers in Kentucky carry an independent statutory duty to supervise agency compliance.** Under KRS 324.085, the principal broker is responsible for ensuring that all licensees affiliated with the brokerage comply with Chapter 324's disclosure requirements. This is not delegable to a team lead or office manager in a way that relieves the broker of liability.

Brokers should audit the following elements on every in-house transaction:

Timing verification is the first audit point. The broker must confirm that Form 200 was signed before substantive contact and that Form 300 (or the designated agency acknowledgment) was signed before dual or designated agency commenced. Date stamps matter—if the Form 300 bears the same date as the purchase agreement, that file is a red flag.

Designation documentation is the second priority. For every transaction where two licensees from the firm represent opposite sides, the file must contain a written broker designation letter. The letter must identify both agents by name, the property, and the date of designation. Generic firm policies posted on an intranet do not satisfy this requirement.

Information barrier protocols must be documented and followed. KREC expects brokers to demonstrate how they prevent confidential information from crossing between designated agents. This can include physical file separation, restricted access in transaction management software, or written instructions to administrative staff. The protocol must exist in writing, and staff must be trained on it.

Commission reconciliation is the third audit item. When a brokerage earns both sides of a commission in a dual or designated agency transaction, the disbursement must be documented in a way that confirms both parties consented to the arrangement. KREC auditors look at commission splits as a secondary confirmation that the proper agency structure was in place.
