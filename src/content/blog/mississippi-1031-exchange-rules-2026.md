# Mississippi 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: What Agents Must Know to Protect Their Clients

Understanding Mississippi 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is not optional for agents handling investment property transactions in this state. Mississippi conforms to federal IRC Section 1031 rules in most respects, but the state adds its own reporting requirements, withholding considerations, and recapture provisions that can blindside both sellers and their agents. The consequences of getting this wrong range from deal delays to personal liability claims against your license.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, compliance obligations, and audit checkpoints that Mississippi agents and brokers must master before facilitating any like-kind exchange this year.

## How Mississippi Conforms to Federal 1031 Rules—and Where It Diverges

Mississippi generally follows the federal treatment of like-kind exchanges under [Miss. Code Ann. Section 27-7-15](https://www.dor.ms.gov/individual-income-tax), which incorporates the Internal Revenue Code as the starting point for computing state taxable income. That means the 45-day identification period and 180-day closing window apply at the state level without modification. Deferred gains flow through to the Mississippi return just as they do on the federal return.

**The divergence shows up in two critical areas: sourcing rules and nonresident withholding.** Mississippi treats gains from the sale of real property located within its borders as Mississippi-source income under Miss. Code Ann. 27-7-23, regardless of where the taxpayer resides. If a nonresident investor sells a Mississippi property and acquires replacement property in Tennessee, the deferred gain remains a Mississippi tax obligation that will eventually come due.

Additionally, the Mississippi Department of Revenue (MDOR) requires nonresident sellers to either pay withholding at closing or file Form 89-350 to claim an exemption—including when the transaction is structured as a 1031 exchange. Agents who fail to flag this requirement risk closing delays of weeks, not days.

## Mississippi-Specific Forms and Reporting Requirements

The paperwork trail for a Mississippi 1031 exchange involves both federal and state filings, and agents should know which forms their clients will need so they can coordinate with CPAs and qualified intermediaries early.

| Form | Purpose | Who Files | When Due |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|
| Form 80-108 | Like-Kind Exchange Information | Taxpayer (individual or entity) | Attached to annual return |
| Form 80-105 | Mississippi Resident Individual Income Tax Return | Resident taxpayers | April 15 or extension |
| Form 80-110 | Corporate Income Tax Return | Corporate entities | March 15 or extension |
| Form 89-350 | Nonresident Withholding Exemption Certificate | Nonresident seller at closing | Before or at closing |
| Federal Form 8824 | Like-Kind Exchanges | Taxpayer | Attached to federal return |

Form 80-108 requires a description of both the relinquished and replacement properties, the dates of transfer, the identity of the qualified intermediary, and the amount of boot received. Missing or incomplete information on this form is one of the most common triggers for MDOR audit inquiries.

Nonresident sellers must also contend with the 5% withholding requirement under Miss. Code Ann. 27-7-308. The closing agent (typically the title company or attorney) is responsible for collecting this withholding unless the seller provides a completed Form 89-350 certifying that the transaction qualifies for an exemption—such as a properly structured 1031 exchange.

## Mississippi 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: The Withholding Trap

**Agents routinely underestimate how the nonresident withholding requirement interacts with 1031 exchanges.** Here is the scenario that causes problems: a nonresident investor sells a $900,000 commercial property in Hattiesburg, intending a 1031 exchange. The closing attorney, unsure whether the exchange qualifies, withholds $45,000 (5% of the sales price) and remits it to MDOR.

That $45,000 is now locked up in the state tax system. The investor's qualified intermediary never receives the full exchange proceeds, potentially disqualifying the exchange under the "constructive receipt" doctrine. The federal exchange fails, the Mississippi deferral fails, and the investor faces a six-figure capital gains tax bill on both returns.

To prevent this outcome, the seller's CPA or tax attorney must file Form 89-350 before closing, and the agent must confirm with the closing attorney that the exemption has been accepted. This is a coordination issue, and agents who treat it as "someone else's problem" end up in the crosshairs when deals collapse.

The distinction matters because [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) is rarely about ignorance—it is about failing to build coordination checkpoints into the transaction timeline.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make on 1031 Exchanges

Five errors account for the majority of agent-caused problems in Mississippi 1031 exchange transactions.

**First, agents advise on tax consequences without qualification.** Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) Rule 3.1 prohibits agents from providing tax advice. When an agent tells a client "you won't owe any Mississippi taxes if you do a 1031," they have crossed a licensure boundary and created potential liability exposure under both MREC disciplinary rules and civil malpractice standards. The correct approach is to refer the client to a licensed CPA or tax attorney and document that referral in writing.

Second, agents fail to flag the 45-day identification deadline during listing or buyer representation. This deadline is federal and applies at the state level. If an agent is representing the buy-side of a replacement property acquisition and does not verify whether the buyer is operating under a 1031 clock, inspection and financing delays can destroy the exchange entirely.

Third, agents neglect to confirm that the qualified intermediary is properly set up before the relinquished property closes. Mississippi does not license or regulate qualified intermediaries, which means there is no state safety net if the QI is undercapitalized or fraudulent. Agents should verify the QI's fidelity bond and segregated account practices as part of their due diligence.

Fourth, agents working with out-of-state buyers or sellers forget the Form 89-350 withholding exemption. As discussed above, this single form can make or break the exchange.

Fifth, agents assume that a partial exchange—where the client receives some cash (boot)—is fully tax-deferred at the Mississippi level. It is not. Mississippi taxes boot as ordinary income or capital gain in the year received, and the partial deferral must be properly reported on Form 80-108. Misrepresenting this to a client creates liability.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under MREC regulations for ensuring their agents do not provide unauthorized tax advice or cause compliance failures in exchange transactions. Here is a practical audit framework for managing 1031-related risk at the brokerage level.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Frequency |
|------------------|----------------|-----------|
| Transaction file review | Written CPA/attorney referral present for all exchange deals | Every 1031 transaction |
| Agent communications | No tax advice in emails, texts, or contract addenda | Quarterly random sample |
| Closing coordination log | Form 89-350 filed before closing for nonresident sellers | Every applicable closing |
| QI verification | Fidelity bond and segregated account documentation on file | At QI engagement |
| Timeline tracking | 45-day and 180-day deadlines calendared and monitored | Every 1031 transaction |

**Brokers who do not audit these checkpoints expose the entire brokerage to vicarious liability.** Under Mississippi law, the supervising broker can face MREC disciplinary action—including license suspension—if an agent's conduct demonstrates a pattern of non-compliance that the broker should have caught. MREC Rule 3.3 explicitly places supervisory duties on designated brokers regarding transactional compliance.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of 1031 exchange mishandling in Mississippi fall into three categories: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

On the regulatory side, MREC can initiate disciplinary proceedings under Miss. Code Ann. 73-35-21 for violations including misrepresentation, incompetence, or failure to disclose material facts. Penalties range from formal reprimand to license revocation, with fines up to $5,000 per violation. Providing tax advice without a CPA or attorney license also potentially violates the Mississippi Board of Public Accountancy's unauthorized practice rules.

Civil liability arises when a client suffers financial harm—such as a failed exchange resulting in a $150,000 tax bill—and sues the agent for negligence or breach of fiduciary duty. Mississippi courts apply a standard negligence framework, and an agent's written communications (especially texts and emails containing tax representations) become exhibit evidence. Errors and omissions insurance may cover some claims, but many E&O policies exclude tax advice from coverage.

Transactional consequences are the most immediate. A missed withholding exemption can delay closing by 30+ days. A QI that was never properly engaged can cause the exchange to fail at the moment of closing, forcing the seller to either proceed with a taxable sale or cancel the contract entirely. Either outcome damages the agent's reputation and referral pipeline.
