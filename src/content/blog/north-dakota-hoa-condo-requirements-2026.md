# North Dakota HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Understanding **North Dakota hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** is no longer optional homework — it is the difference between a clean closing and a rescinded deal. Roughly one in four residential transactions in the state's metro markets now involves a property governed by a homeowners association or condominium regime. When agents treat HOA and condo disclosures as afterthoughts, they invite cancellation rights, commission clawbacks, and disciplinary complaints filed with the North Dakota Real Estate Commission.

This post breaks down the statutes that control the disclosure process, the exact forms and documents agents must track, the penalties for noncompliance, and the internal audit steps brokers should build into their compliance programs.

## The Statutory Framework Behind North Dakota Condo Disclosures

North Dakota's primary condo disclosure authority sits in **NDCC Chapter 47-04.1**, the North Dakota Condominium Act. Sections 47-04.1-16 and 47-04.1-17 establish the seller's obligation to deliver a resale certificate to the buyer before the buyer becomes contractually bound. The resale certificate must contain specific financial and governance data about the association.

For properties within HOA-governed subdivisions that are not condominiums, North Dakota does not have a standalone HOA disclosure statute comparable to the Condominium Act. Instead, disclosure obligations arise from the [North Dakota Real Estate Commission's](https://www.realestatend.org/) license law (NDCC Chapter 43-23), the duty of honest dealing, and the terms negotiated in the purchase agreement. Agents who assume that non-condo HOA properties have zero disclosure duties are making a dangerous bet.

**The gap between condo and non-condo HOA disclosure requirements is where most agent errors live.** A condo resale certificate is mandated by statute. An HOA disclosure package for a non-condo subdivision depends on what the parties agree to in the contract and on the agent's independent duty to disclose material facts under NDCC 43-23-12.1.

## What the Condo Resale Certificate Must Include

Section 47-04.1-17 of the Condominium Act spells out the contents of a resale certificate. The association — not the seller, not the listing agent — is the entity that prepares and furnishes it. However, the seller is responsible for requesting it, and the listing agent is responsible for making sure it actually reaches the buyer's side.

The resale certificate must include the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments that have been approved or are pending, the amount of the association's reserves, and any outstanding judgments or pending litigation involving the association. It also must disclose any capital expenditures anticipated within the current or next fiscal year and the status of the unit's account — specifically, whether the seller owes any unpaid assessments.

| Pending litigation or judgments | NDCC 47-04.1-17 | Association |
| Anticipated capital expenditures | NDCC 47-04.1-17 | Association |
| Seller's unpaid assessment balance | NDCC 47-04.1-17 | Association |
| Insurance coverage summary | NDCC 47-04.1-17 | Association |

Agents should also review the association's governing documents — the declaration, bylaws, and rules — even though these are technically separate from the resale certificate. Buyers routinely conflate the two, and listing agents who hand over only the certificate without the covenants and restrictions invite post-closing disputes.

## The Five-Day Cancellation Right and Its Consequences

**Buyers have a statutory five-day cancellation window after receiving the resale certificate.** Under NDCC 47-04.1-17, the purchase agreement is voidable by the buyer until five days after the certificate is delivered. If the certificate is never delivered, that cancellation right never expires — it remains live through closing and arguably even after.

This is a hard deadline that agents on both sides must calendar. Listing agents should document the exact date of delivery with a signed receipt or email confirmation. Buyer's agents should flag the five-day window to their clients in writing and track the expiration date as they would an inspection contingency deadline. Missing this window or miscounting the days can mean the difference between a binding contract and a buyer who walks away with their earnest money, no questions asked.

## Non-Condo HOA Disclosures: The Gray Zone

North Dakota does not have a statute equivalent to the Condominium Act for standard homeowners associations. That does not mean there is nothing to disclose. The North Dakota Association of REALTORS (NDAR) purchase agreement forms — specifically the Residential Purchase Agreement — include sections where HOA information can and should be filled in. The current NDAR form requires the seller to identify whether the property is subject to a homeowners association and to provide governing documents upon request.

The NDAR Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement (which agents in North Dakota commonly refer to by its general name rather than a numbered state form, since North Dakota statute at NDCC 47-10-02.1 authorizes but does not mandate a specific state-issued form) includes a section addressing association fees and assessments. Agents who leave this section blank on an HOA property are inviting trouble.

**Material fact disclosure obligations under NDCC 43-23-12.1 fill whatever gaps the purchase agreement leaves.** If an agent knows — or reasonably should know — that the HOA is financially unstable, has pending litigation, or has passed a special assessment, that information is a material fact. Silence is not a defense.

| Disclosure Obligation | Condo (NDCC 47-04.1) | Non-Condo HOA |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate required by statute | Yes | No |
| Buyer cancellation right (5 days) | Yes (statutory) | Only if in purchase agreement |
| Governing documents delivery | Included in certificate requirement | Negotiated in purchase agreement |
| Agent material fact duty | Yes (NDCC 43-23-12.1) | Yes (NDCC 43-23-12.1) |
| Financial disclosure (reserves, assessments) | Mandated in certificate | Agent must disclose if known |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA and Condo Disclosures

**First: treating the resale certificate as the seller's problem alone.** The listing agent has an independent duty to ensure the buyer receives it. If the association drags its feet, the listing agent cannot simply shrug and hope the title company catches it. The five-day cancellation clock does not start until actual delivery, so delays directly extend the buyer's exit window.

Second: failing to distinguish between the resale certificate and the full governing document package. Buyers and lenders often need the declaration, bylaws, rules, and amendments — not just the one-page financial summary. Agents who deliver partial packages create confusion at underwriting and risk last-minute closing delays.

Third: ignoring special assessments that have been discussed but not yet formally approved. Under NDCC 47-04.1-17, pending special assessments must be disclosed. Some agents interpret "pending" too narrowly and omit assessments that are in active board discussion but not yet voted on. The safer practice is to disclose anything the agent knows is under consideration, especially when it would affect the buyer's monthly cost projections.

Fourth: not confirming the HOA's management contact information early in the listing process. Many North Dakota associations are self-managed by volunteer boards. Response times for resale certificate requests can stretch to weeks. Agents who wait until after an offer is accepted to request the certificate often find themselves scrambling against contract deadlines. If you want to understand [why checklists consistently outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in transaction management, HOA document tracking is exhibit A.

Fifth: using outdated NDAR forms. Form revisions happen, and the association-related sections of the purchase agreement and disclosure forms are frequently updated. Agents who pull last year's PDF off their desktop instead of downloading the current version from the NDAR forms library risk missing new required fields.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under NDCC 43-23-11.1 for the actions of their affiliated agents. A single missed resale certificate can become a Commission complaint that names both the agent and the supervising broker. Audit protocols should not be aspirational — they should be documented and enforced.

Every brokerage handling condo or HOA transactions in North Dakota should maintain a checklist that verifies three things before closing: the resale certificate (for condos) was delivered and signed for, the five-day cancellation period expired without buyer objection, and the seller's assessment account is current. These three items should appear as required line items in the transaction file.

Brokers should also spot-check whether agents are correctly filling out the HOA-related sections of the NDAR purchase agreement and the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement. A blank HOA section on a property that is clearly within a governed community is a red flag that should trigger a conversation with the agent before the file closes.

Quarterly file audits are the minimum standard. ## Liability When Disclosures Go Wrong

If a condo resale certificate is not delivered, the buyer can rescind the contract and recover their earnest money deposit. That alone can cost the listing agent their commission and damage the client relationship beyond repair. But the financial exposure does not stop there.

Under NDCC 43-23-12.1, an agent who fails to disclose a material fact — including known HOA financial problems — can face disciplinary action by the North Dakota Real Estate Commission, including fines, mandatory education, license suspension, or revocation. The Commission has broad authority under NDCC 43-23-11.1 to investigate complaints and impose sanctions.

Civil liability runs in parallel. A buyer who closes on a condo unit and later discovers an undisclosed $15,000 special assessment has a viable claim for damages against the seller and potentially the listing agent. Courts in North Dakota have recognized that agents owe duties beyond what the contract itself spells out — the statutory duty of honest dealing is independent of any contractual disclaimer.
