## Maryland Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Distinction That Trips Up Agents

The question of Maryland attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 is not academic. It dictates who can legally prepare deeds, who certifies title, and where agent liability begins if the wrong party handles settlement functions. Maryland occupies a unique middle ground among states: it is neither a pure "attorney state" nor a freewheeling "title company state," and that hybrid reality creates compliance gaps agents stumble into every week.

Maryland's framework flows from the Business Occupations & Professions Article, Title 10 (the Maryland Unauthorized Practice of Law provisions), combined with guidelines from the Maryland State Bar Association and operational oversight by the Maryland Insurance Administration for title insurers. Understanding where one authority ends and another begins is the difference between a smooth closing and a deal that unravels at the settlement table.

## The Legal Framework: What Maryland Law Actually Requires

**Maryland does not have a single statute that says "an attorney must sit at the closing table."** Instead, the requirement is functional. Under Business Occupations & Professions Article Section 10-101, the "practice of law" includes preparing legal instruments affecting title to real property. The Court of Appeals of Maryland reinforced in *Attorney Grievance Commission v. Hallmon* (2012) that title examinations and deed preparation constitute the practice of law.

This means a title company can handle escrow, coordinate documents, and issue title insurance policies. But the actual title search, title certification, deed drafting, and preparation of documents like deeds of trust must be done by or under the direct supervision of an attorney admitted to the Maryland Bar. A title company that employs or contracts with a Maryland-barred attorney satisfies this requirement — and most large title companies in the state do exactly that.

The Maryland Insurance Administration regulates title insurance companies under the Insurance Article, Title 11. Title agents must hold a license from the MIA, and their fiduciary handling of settlement funds is governed by COMAR 31.01.04. None of this replaces the attorney supervision requirement; it layers on top of it.

## Attorney State, Title Company State, or Something Else?

Maryland is often categorized as an "attorney state," but that label oversimplifies reality. The more accurate description: Maryland requires attorney involvement in the legal functions of closing, while permitting title companies to manage the transactional and insurance functions.

| Function | Who May Perform It | Authority |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Title search and examination | Attorney (or under attorney supervision) | Bus. Occ. & Prof. Art. §10-101 |
| Deed and deed of trust preparation | Attorney | Bus. Occ. & Prof. Art. §10-101 |
| Title insurance issuance | Licensed title insurer/agent | Insurance Art. Title 11 |
| Settlement fund disbursement | Licensed settlement agent (title co. or attorney) | COMAR 31.01.04 |
| Conducting the settlement meeting | Attorney or authorized settlement agent | Contract terms / local practice |
| Recording documents | Any authorized party (typically title co. or attorney) | Real Property Art. §3-104 |

In practice, most Maryland closings feature an attorney who also acts as the settlement agent, or a title company whose in-house attorney supervises the legal work. The parties to the contract designate who they want, typically using [Maryland Association of REALTORS (MAR) forms](https://www.mdrealtor.org/).

## How the MAR Contract Forms Address Settlement Selection

The Maryland Association of REALTORS Residential Contract of Sale (Form 710, revised 2025) includes a dedicated section on settlement. Paragraph 18 of Form 710 identifies the settlement agent and specifies who selects them. The addendum for Settlement Agent Selection (Form 710SA) further clarifies whether the buyer, seller, or lender designates the closing entity.

Agents must understand that the contract language controls. If the contract names a title company as the settlement agent, that title company is still obligated to have attorney supervision over legal functions. The form does not override state law — it merely records the parties' preference for who manages the process. Agents listing a settlement provider without confirming that provider has proper attorney supervision expose themselves and their clients to risk.

Form 710 also intersects with the Residential Property Disclosure and Disclaimer Statement (Form 510-R), which must be delivered prior to contract execution. Settlement complications often arise when [disclosure requirements weren't met cleanly](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), creating title clouds that require attorney intervention at or before closing.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Regarding Maryland Closings

**First, agents frequently assume any title company can independently handle the full closing.** They refer buyers to an out-of-state title company's local branch without verifying that a Maryland-barred attorney supervises the title work. When problems surface — an unrecorded lien, a boundary dispute, a deed defect — and no supervising attorney exists, the transaction can be voided and the agent faces a MREC complaint.

Second, agents confuse the settlement agent role with legal representation. Telling a client "the title company will take care of everything, you don't need your own attorney" may constitute improper legal advice. Under COMAR 09.11.02.05, agents must not provide legal counsel. The settlement agent works for the transaction; they do not represent either party's legal interests unless separately retained as counsel.

Third, agents fail to confirm that the settlement agent is properly licensed with the Maryland Insurance Administration. MIA licensure is separate from Bar admission. A closing attorney who is not also a licensed title insurance producer cannot issue an owner's title policy. Agents who don't verify this create last-minute scrambles that delay recordings and endanger rate locks.

Fourth, agents in dual-agency or designated-agency scenarios under Maryland Real Property Article Section 17-530 sometimes let one side's preferred settlement attorney handle everything without disclosing the potential conflict. If that attorney has a referral relationship with only one agent, the undisclosed arrangement can trigger MREC disciplinary proceedings.

Fifth, agents neglect to verify that the settlement agent carries the required fidelity bond and errors-and-omissions coverage under COMAR 31.01.04. A settlement agent's insolvency or defalcation can leave clients without recourse — and the referring agent holding a professional liability claim.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory obligations under Maryland Business Occupations & Professions Article Section 17-322. The standard of supervision includes ensuring agents within the brokerage follow lawful closing procedures. A broker who ignores systemic settlement compliance failures faces license suspension or revocation from the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC).

**Brokers should maintain a vetted list of approved settlement providers.** Each provider on that list should have verified MIA licensure, confirmed attorney supervision credentials, current E&O coverage, and a clean disciplinary record with both the Attorney Grievance Commission and the MIA. Reviewing this list quarterly is not overkill; it is baseline risk management.

Transaction files should document which settlement agent was selected, by whom, and when. MREC audits routinely examine whether the brokerage's file contains the signed settlement agent designation (Form 710SA or equivalent). Missing documentation does not just look sloppy — it creates an inference that the agent steered the client to a specific provider without proper disclosure, potentially violating RESPA Section 8 as well.

Brokers should also audit for [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) at the settlement stage. Maryland has seen a spike in business email compromise schemes targeting settlement funds, and a broker who fails to implement verification procedures may face negligence claims when funds are misdirected.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Get This Wrong

The consequences of noncompliance are layered. At the state level, MREC can impose fines, require additional continuing education, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely under Business Occupations & Professions Article Section 17-322. At the civil level, a client whose transaction fails because of an improperly supervised closing can sue the agent for negligence, and potentially for aiding the unauthorized practice of law under Section 10-601.

Title insurance claims that arise from defective title work performed without proper attorney supervision may be denied by the underwriter. When that happens, the buyer is left with an uninsurable title defect and a cause of action against everyone in the chain — including the agent who referred them to the flawed settlement provider. The agent's E&O carrier may contest coverage if the agent knowingly or negligently facilitated an unlawful closing arrangement.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. Lenders increasingly require confirmation that settlement will be conducted by a properly authorized entity. If the lender's closing instructions specify attorney supervision and the file arrives at closing without it, the lender will refuse to fund. The buyer's rate lock expires, the seller walks, and the commission evaporates.

## Practical Guidance for 2026 and Beyond

Maryland's regulatory posture has not loosened. The Attorney Grievance Commission continues to prosecute unauthorized practice cases, and the MIA has increased audits of title agency trust accounts. Agents should treat the attorney-supervision requirement as non-negotiable and verify compliance before contract ratification — not at the pre-closing stage when it is too late to switch providers.
