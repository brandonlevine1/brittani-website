## Understanding Nebraska Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The **Nebraska buyer agency agreement requirements 2026** landscape shifted dramatically after the NAR settlement terms took full effect in August 2024, and the Nebraska Real Estate Commission has since tightened enforcement. Agents who treated the initial rollout as a soft suggestion are now facing real consequences—citations, fines, and deal unwinds. This post breaks down what Nebraska law demands, which forms satisfy those demands, and where agents and brokers are still getting tripped up heading into the second half of 2026.

Nebraska operates under the Nebraska Real Estate License Act, codified at [Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2401 through 76-2430](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=76-2401). The Nebraska Real Estate Commission (NREC) administers licensing and enforcement, and its Title 299 regulations provide the procedural backbone for agency relationships. Every buyer-side agent must understand how these statutes, the NAR settlement requirements, and NREC's enforcement posture intersect in practice.

## The Legal Foundation: What Nebraska Statutes Actually Require

Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2417 establishes that a licensee owes agency duties only when an agency relationship exists, and that relationship must be created through a written agreement. This isn't new—Nebraska has required written agency agreements for years—but the timing obligation has changed. Before the NAR settlement, many agents operated in a gray zone, providing showings under a vague "customer" relationship before formalizing representation.

**NREC now interprets "substantive real estate services" broadly, meaning the written agreement must be signed before an agent identifies specific properties, arranges showings, or provides pricing analysis for a particular buyer.** The Commission's 2025 guidance letter clarified that casual conversations about market conditions don't trigger the requirement, but the moment an agent pulls listings tailored to a buyer's criteria, the line has been crossed.

Title 299, Chapter 6 of the Nebraska Administrative Code spells out the required disclosures within any agency agreement. The document must identify the licensee and brokerage, the scope of services, the duration of the agreement, how compensation is structured, and the buyer's right to terminate.

## Required Forms and Documentation in Nebraska

The Nebraska REALTORS Association (NRA) publishes standardized forms that most brokerages adopt. For buyer agency, the two primary documents are NRA Form 20 (Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement) and NRA Form 21 (Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement). Both were revised in late 2024 to incorporate NAR settlement compliance language.

| Form | Type | Exclusivity | Key Feature |
|------|------|-------------|-------------|
| NRA Form 20 | Exclusive Buyer Agency | Yes — buyer works with one agent | Compensation terms fully disclosed; agent earns commission regardless of property source |
| NRA Form 21 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency | No — buyer may work with multiple agents | Agent only earns compensation on transactions they directly facilitate |
| Custom Brokerage Form | Varies | Depends on language | Must still satisfy Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2417 and Title 299 requirements |

Some brokerages draft proprietary agreements reviewed by their legal counsel. That's permissible, but the form must still include every element mandated by statute. Missing even one required disclosure—such as the specific compensation amount or the agreement's definite expiration date—can void the contract's enforceability and expose the brokerage to Commission discipline.

Agents should also be aware of NRA Form 22 (Agency Disclosure and Consent), which is used to explain the different agency relationships available in Nebraska. This form does not replace the buyer agency agreement but is typically presented alongside it during the initial consultation. NREC expects agents to present this disclosure before or at the same time the buyer agreement is signed.

## Compensation Disclosure and the NAR Settlement Intersection

**The NAR settlement prohibits buyer-agent compensation from being communicated through MLS fields, which means Nebraska agents must address compensation head-on within the buyer agency agreement itself.** NRA Form 20 now includes a dedicated section where the agent specifies their fee—whether a flat dollar amount, a percentage, or an hourly rate—and explains how that compensation might be satisfied through seller concessions, buyer payment, or a combination.

Nebraska law doesn't cap what agents can charge. However, the agreement must state the amount with enough specificity that the buyer understands their potential financial obligation before signing. Vague language like "customary commission" won't survive NREC scrutiny. The Commission's 2025 enforcement actions confirm they're looking for a dollar figure or a clear percentage tied to the purchase price.

This is where many agents familiar with [Wisconsin's buyer agency agreement structure](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) assume Nebraska works the same way. It doesn't. Wisconsin's forms evolved from a different statutory framework, and Nebraska's Title 299 imposes its own disclosure sequence. Agents licensed in both states need to track these distinctions carefully.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

Failing to secure a proper buyer agency agreement before providing substantive services triggers multiple risk categories. The consequences aren't theoretical—NREC issued 23 citations related to buyer agency violations in the first quarter of 2026 alone, according to their published disciplinary actions.

| Violation | Potential Consequence | Statutory Authority |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|
| No written agreement before substantive services | Fine up to $10,000; license suspension | Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2417; 76-2421 |
| Missing required disclosures in agreement | Contract may be voidable; Commission censure | Title 299, Ch. 6 |
| Agreement lacks definite expiration date | NAR settlement non-compliance; potential MLS suspension | NAR Settlement Terms, Section III |
| Failure to present agency disclosure form | Fine; required additional education | Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422 |

Beyond regulatory penalties, a buyer can potentially rescind the entire purchase agreement if they can demonstrate they were not properly informed of the agency relationship. Nebraska courts have not yet produced appellate case law on this specific scenario post-settlement, but the statutory framework supports the argument. An improperly documented agency relationship also jeopardizes the agent's ability to collect compensation if a dispute reaches litigation.

Transaction coordinators handling Nebraska deals should flag any file missing the signed buyer agreement before the first showing date—[this is exactly the kind of breakdown point that costs agents money](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## Common Mistakes Nebraska Agents Make

After reviewing NREC disciplinary records and speaking with compliance officers at several Nebraska brokerages, five recurring errors emerge.

**First, agents present the agreement at the offer stage rather than before showings.** This was tolerable before 2024. It's now a violation. The agreement must precede substantive services, and a showing is unambiguously substantive.

Second, agents use outdated form versions. NRA updated Forms 20 and 21 in October 2024. Agents still pulling pre-revision PDFs from their files are missing required language about compensation transparency and definite termination dates. NREC doesn't grandfather old forms—if the current version exists, you're expected to use it (or a custom equivalent that covers the same ground).

Third, agents leave the compensation field blank or write "TBD." The NAR settlement and Nebraska's disclosure statutes both require specificity at the time of signing. A blank field means the agreement is incomplete and potentially unenforceable. If your compensation structure varies by property type or price point, build a tiered schedule into the agreement.

Fourth, teams assign the agreement to a specific agent but then have a different team member conduct showings without proper introduction or agency disclosure. Under Nebraska law, the buyer must know which licensee represents them. If a showing agent isn't the named agent on the agreement, the brokerage needs internal documentation showing proper delegation and the buyer's awareness.

Fifth, agents fail to document the expiration date or use open-ended language like "until buyer purchases a home." The NAR settlement explicitly requires a definite duration. Nebraska doesn't prescribe a maximum, but open-ended agreements won't satisfy settlement compliance and invite NREC scrutiny.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2423 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. If an agent violates buyer agency requirements, the broker's license is also at risk. Passive oversight isn't a defense—NREC expects brokers to maintain systems that catch violations before they become disciplinary matters.

**At minimum, brokers should audit every active buyer file for a signed agency agreement dated before the first showing or substantive service.** This means building a workflow where the agreement upload triggers a timestamp check against showing activity. Manual spot-checks are insufficient when you're managing 30 or more agents.

Brokers should verify that every agreement in their files uses a current-version form (or an approved custom form), contains a specific compensation figure, includes a definite start and end date, and bears signatures from both the buyer and the affiliated licensee. A quarterly audit of 10-15 percent of active files is the floor; monthly is better.

Training is the other half. Brokers who simply email a policy memo and assume compliance are fooling themselves. NREC has signaled that broker disciplinary outcomes will be harsher when the Commission finds no evidence of active training or compliance systems. Document your training sessions, attendance, and any corrective actions taken when errors surface.

For brokerages [already struggling with deadline tracking and document management](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), adding buyer agency compliance to the pile can feel overwhelming. This is precisely where automated compliance checking adds value—flagging files that are missing required documents before they become liability events.

## Duration, Termination, and Renewal Considerations

Nebraska law doesn't specify a maximum agreement duration, but industry norms have converged around 60 to 90 days. Some luxury and relocation agents negotiate 120-day terms when the buyer's search is expected to be lengthy. The key statutory requirement is that the agreement must state a definite end date—no evergreen clauses.

Termination provisions must also be clearly articulated. NRA Form 20 includes a section addressing early termination, including any fees the buyer may owe if they terminate without cause. Nebraska permits these "protection periods" (sometimes called tail periods) where the agent retains a compensation claim on properties shown during the agreement term, even if the buyer purchases after expiration. However, the protection period must be explicitly stated and reasonable in length.

Renewal isn't automatic unless the agreement says so—and even then, NREC expects that any auto-renewal provision is conspicuously disclosed and accompanied by a definite new end date. Agents who let agreements lapse and continue providing services are operating without a valid agency relationship, which resets the entire compliance clock.
