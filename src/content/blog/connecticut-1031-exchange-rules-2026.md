## Understanding Connecticut 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026

Connecticut treats 1031 exchanges differently than the federal government, and the gap between federal and state rules is exactly where agents get burned. If you handle investment property transactions anywhere in the state, the Connecticut 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 should be on your radar well before listing agreements are signed. The Connecticut Department of Revenue Services (DRS) imposes its own withholding requirements, gain recognition rules, and filing obligations that do not automatically mirror IRC Section 1031 deferral at the federal level.

Agents who assume that a properly structured federal 1031 exchange eliminates all state tax obligations are wrong. Connecticut conforms to Section 1031 for income tax purposes under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-701(a)(20), but conformity does not eliminate the withholding obligation at closing. The withholding must still be addressed procedurally, and failure to do so can create personal liability for the parties involved in the transaction.

## How Connecticut's Withholding Rules Apply to Exchanges

**Connecticut imposes a mandatory withholding on real property sales by nonresident individuals, estates, trusts, and certain entities under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-728.** The standard withholding rate is 6.99% of the gain recognized on the sale. When gain cannot be readily determined at closing, the DRS allows an alternative withholding of 2.5% of the total sales price.

Here is where it gets tricky for 1031 exchanges: even though the exchange may result in zero recognized gain at the federal and state level, the closing agent still needs documentation proving the transaction qualifies. Without that documentation, the withholding obligation stands. The DRS does not simply take the seller's word that they intend to complete a like-kind exchange.

To request an exemption or reduction of the withholding, the seller must provide one of the following at or before closing: a completed Form AU-963 (Application for Withholding Exemption/Reduced Withholding), or proof that the transaction will result in no Connecticut-sourced gain. The closing attorney or title company typically handles this, but agents who do not flag the requirement early enough in the timeline risk delaying closings or forcing last-minute scrambles.

## Key Forms and Filing Obligations

The form landscape for Connecticut 1031 exchanges involves both the seller's income tax return and the withholding mechanics at closing. Below is a breakdown of the most relevant forms agents should recognize.

| Form | Purpose | Who Files | When |
|------|---------|-----------|------|
| CT-NRPY | Nonresident/Part-Year Resident Income Tax Return | Nonresident seller | By filing deadline (April 15 or extension) |
| AU-963 | Application for Withholding Exemption or Reduction | Seller (via closing agent) | Before or at closing |
| CT-1120 | Corporation Business Tax Return (for entity sellers) | Corporate seller | By filing deadline |
| CT-1065/CT-1120S | Partnership/S-Corp Return | Pass-through entity seller | By filing deadline |
| Form CT-8801 | Credit for Prior Year Connecticut Minimum Tax | Seller (if applicable) | With annual return |

Agents do not file these forms themselves, but they need to know which ones apply so they can alert the seller's CPA or attorney early in the transaction. A seller who does not file Form AU-963 before closing will have the withholding collected and remitted to the DRS, requiring them to seek a refund later—a process that can take months and frustrate clients who expected full deferral.

## The Conformity Question: Does Connecticut Fully Recognize Federal 1031 Deferral?

**Connecticut does conform to IRC Section 1031 for income tax purposes, but conformity is not the same as a free pass.** The state's conformity means that if you properly complete a like-kind exchange at the federal level, Connecticut will also defer the gain for state income tax purposes. However, two scenarios regularly trip up agents and their clients.

First, partial exchanges. If boot is received (cash or non-like-kind property), Connecticut taxes the recognized gain just as the IRS does, but applies the state's own income tax rate schedule. For individuals, that rate can reach 6.99% on the gain. Second, if the replacement property is located outside Connecticut but the relinquished property was in-state, the state still has a claim on the gain if the exchange later fails or is unwound.

The [Connecticut Department of Revenue Services](https://portal.ct.gov/drs) provides administrative guidance on these issues, though it has not published a dedicated ruling specific to 2026 exchange scenarios. Agents should monitor DRS Informational Publications, particularly IP 2011(29) and its successors, which outline nonresident withholding procedures.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance fall into three buckets: direct financial liability, transaction disruption, and regulatory exposure.

Under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-728(c), if the required withholding is not collected at closing, the transferee (buyer) becomes liable for the tax. In practice, this means the buyer's attorney or closing agent who facilitated the transaction without ensuring proper withholding documentation may face DRS enforcement action. Interest accrues from the date the withholding should have been remitted, and penalties of up to 10% of the underpaid amount can apply.

For agents, the risk is less about direct DRS penalties and more about professional liability. If an agent advised a client that no state tax would be due on a 1031 exchange and that advice turned out to be wrong—causing the client to owe taxes plus interest—the agent could face an errors and omissions claim. Brokers can also face Connecticut Real Estate Commission scrutiny if a pattern of non-compliance suggests inadequate supervision under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-312.

Deal cancellation is a real possibility when withholding issues surface at the eleventh hour. Buyers who learn they could be personally liable for uncollected withholding may walk away from the transaction or demand price adjustments. This is entirely preventable with proper early disclosure and coordination.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Connecticut 1031 Exchanges

**The following errors appear repeatedly in Connecticut transactions, and each one is avoidable with proper awareness and process.**

The first mistake is assuming the qualified intermediary handles all state tax issues. QIs are responsible for structuring the federal exchange mechanics—holding funds, meeting the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines. They do not file Connecticut withholding exemptions, and most QI agreements explicitly disclaim responsibility for state tax compliance.

The second mistake is failing to identify nonresident sellers early in the listing process. Connecticut's withholding rules only apply to nonresidents selling in-state property, but agents sometimes do not discover residency status until days before closing. By then, filing Form AU-963 may not be feasible, forcing the seller into a refund claim scenario.

Third, agents conflate "tax-deferred" with "tax-free." Clients hear "1031 exchange" and believe they owe nothing. Agents who reinforce that belief without qualifying it for Connecticut's withholding mechanics are setting up a fiduciary problem. The exchange defers gain; it does not eliminate the procedural withholding obligation at closing.

Fourth, agents forget that Connecticut applies its own statute of limitations. The DRS has three years from the filing date (or the date the return was due, whichever is later) to assess additional tax. If a 1031 exchange is later disqualified, the state can come back for years.

Fifth, failing to document the exchange intent in the transaction file. If [your deal falls apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down) because of a missed exchange deadline, having documentation that the seller intended a 1031 exchange—and that the agent communicated the state's withholding requirement—protects the agent from liability claims.

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under Connecticut law, and 1031 exchange transactions deserve heightened file review. Here is what brokers should be checking.

| Audit Point | What to Look For | Red Flag |

| Seller residency status | Documented in listing file within first 5 days | No residency confirmation on file |
| QI engagement letter | Copy in transaction file | No QI identified for investment property sale |

Brokers who do not enforce these audit points risk vicarious liability if an agent's negligence causes client harm. The Connecticut Real Estate Commission expects supervising brokers to maintain reasonable oversight of transactions involving complex tax structures. A quarterly file audit specifically targeting 1031 exchange deals is a defensible best practice.

Beyond auditing, brokers should establish a written office policy requiring agents to disclose in writing to any investment property seller that Connecticut imposes withholding obligations separate from federal 1031 requirements. ## Timeline Pressure: State Deadlines That Differ from Federal

Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on standard transactions already know the stress of time-sensitive compliance. The 1031 exchange adds layers. While the federal 45-day identification period and 180-day exchange period are well-known, Connecticut's withholding mechanics introduce their own timing pressure.

Form AU-963 should be submitted to the DRS before closing. The DRS does not guarantee a turnaround time on exemption approvals, meaning sellers who file the form one week before closing may not receive confirmation in time. Best practice is to submit AU-963 at least 30 days before the anticipated closing date. If the exemption is not approved in time, the closing agent must collect the withholding, and the seller claims a refund on their CT-NRPY return.

This timing issue alone explains why investment property listings in Connecticut benefit from longer closing timelines than typical residential deals. Agents who price in a 60-day close on a 1031 exchange property—rather than the standard 30 to 45 days—give their sellers room to secure the withholding exemption without disrupting the transaction.
