Connecticut is one of a handful of states where attorneys run the closing table, not title companies acting alone. Understanding Connecticut title & closing procedures 2026 is non-negotiable for agents who want to protect their clients, their commissions, and their licenses. The rules here differ meaningfully from neighboring states, and the consequences of getting them wrong range from deal collapse to personal liability exposure.

## Why Connecticut Closings Operate Differently

Connecticut is classified as an "attorney-close" state. Under Connecticut General Statutes Section 51-88, the preparation of deeds, mortgages, releases, and other legal instruments constitutes the practice of law. That means a licensed Connecticut attorney must be involved in every residential closing, period.

**This distinction matters because agents who overstep into document preparation risk unauthorized practice of law charges.** The Connecticut Bar Association has historically pursued enforcement actions against non-attorneys who draft closing documents, and the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection can initiate disciplinary proceedings against licensees who facilitate such arrangements.

Title insurance in Connecticut is regulated by the Connecticut Insurance Department under Title 38a of the General Statutes. Unlike states where title companies handle everything from search to settlement, Connecticut requires the title search to be certified by an attorney, even when a national title insurer underwrites the policy.

## The Timeline from Contract to Recording

Once a purchase agreement is executed using the Connecticut Association of Realtors (CAR) Residential Purchase and Sales Agreement, the closing timeline in a standard transaction follows a predictable sequence. Agents should expect 45 to 60 days from contract execution to recording, though cash transactions can compress this window.

The attorney orders a title search through a title searcher or abstractor, who examines land records at the relevant town clerk's office. Connecticut's 169 municipalities each maintain their own land records, which means search timelines vary widely depending on the town's record-keeping systems. Some towns have digitized records back decades; others still require manual searches of physical volumes.

After the search is complete, the attorney issues a title opinion or certification. If the buyer's lender requires title insurance, the attorney works with a title insurance company to issue a commitment for title insurance, which lists any exceptions or requirements that must be satisfied before closing. The commitment is not the policy itself—it is a conditional promise that the insurer will issue a policy once all requirements are met.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

**Connecticut agents need to know which forms trigger which obligations, even though attorneys prepare most closing documents.** The CAR form library includes several that directly impact closing procedures.

| Form/Document | Who Prepares It | Agent's Role |
|---|---|---|
| Residential Purchase and Sales Agreement (CAR Form) | Agent | Draft, present, execute |
| Residential Property Condition Disclosure Report (CGS 20-327b) | Seller | Ensure timely delivery |
| Lead Paint Disclosure (Federal/State) | Seller/Agent | Provide and obtain signatures |
| Settlement Statement (TRID Closing Disclosure) | Lender/Attorney | Review for accuracy |
| Warranty Deed or Quitclaim Deed | Attorney | Not agent's role to prepare |
| Connecticut Real Estate Conveyance Tax Return (OP-236) | Attorney | Verify tax amount with client |

The Residential Property Condition Disclosure Report is required under CGS Section 20-327b and must be delivered to the buyer before or at the time of contract execution. Failure to deliver it gives the buyer a right of rescission. Agents who skip this step expose themselves and their clients to post-closing claims.

The conveyance tax return (Form OP-236) is filed with the town clerk at the time of recording. The current rate structure imposes 0.75% on the first $800,000 of consideration and 1.25% on amounts above that threshold for most residential transactions, with additional surcharges applicable in certain price ranges as updated for 2026. Sellers pay this tax, and agents should confirm the amount appears correctly on the Closing Disclosure.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Connecticut Closings

Experienced agents still make errors that delay or derail closings. Here are the most frequent ones seen in Connecticut transactions.

**First, agents fail to calendar the mortgage contingency deadline independently of the lender's timeline.** The CAR purchase agreement specifies a date by which the buyer must obtain a mortgage commitment. If that date passes without either an extension or a commitment letter, the seller may have grounds to terminate. Agents who rely on the loan officer to track this date are gambling with the deal. For more on [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), the root cause is almost always a system failure, not a knowledge gap.

Second, agents neglect to verify that the property's legal description matches across all documents. Connecticut's metes-and-bounds descriptions can contain errors that propagate from the listing through to the deed. A mismatch between the contract description and the title search can halt closing while the attorney resolves the discrepancy.

Third, agents assume the title search will be completed faster than it actually takes. Ordering a search in a town with manual records two weeks before closing is a recipe for adjournments. Best practice is to confirm the search has been ordered within five business days of attorney engagement.

Fourth, agents provide wire instructions to clients without following verification protocols. Wire fraud remains rampant in Connecticut closings, and agents who forward instructions via unverified email channels may face liability if funds are misdirected. The proper procedure is to direct clients to call the attorney's office using a known phone number—never a number embedded in an email. Our guide on [wire instructions verification processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) covers the exact steps.

Fifth, agents fail to confirm that the seller has arranged for municipal lien certificates (known in Connecticut as tax and water/sewer status letters) in advance of closing. These certificates confirm outstanding balances owed to the municipality and must be clear or accounted for at settlement. Some towns take two to three weeks to issue them.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerage compliance in Connecticut extends beyond transaction files. The Connecticut Real Estate Commission, operating under the Department of Consumer Protection, conducts audits and can impose fines or license suspensions for violations.

| Audit Area | What Brokers Must Verify | Statute/Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure delivery | Property condition report delivered before or at contract | CGS 20-327b |
| Earnest money handling | Deposits held in designated attorney or broker escrow account | CGS 20-324a |
| Commission agreements | Written buyer/seller representation agreements in file | Commission Regulation 20-325a-7 |
| Document retention | Transaction records maintained for minimum 3 years | CGS 20-325a |
| Advertising compliance | All ads include brokerage name and license status | Regulation 20-325a-6a |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file reviews that specifically check for the property condition disclosure form, the signed agency agreement, and confirmation that earnest money was deposited within the timeframe specified in the contract.** The standard CAR contract requires deposit within two business days of acceptance unless otherwise specified. Late deposits violate both the contract terms and potentially the broker's fiduciary obligations.

The Connecticut Real Estate Commission has the authority under CGS Section 20-320 to revoke, suspend, or refuse to renew a license for negligence, incompetence, or failure to comply with statutory requirements. Fines can reach $25,000 per violation for egregious conduct. Brokers are jointly responsible for the conduct of their agents under the supervisory obligations outlined in Regulation 20-325a-9.

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

The consequences for agents who fail to follow Connecticut title and closing procedures extend beyond professional discipline. Civil liability attaches when a buyer or seller suffers financial harm due to an agent's negligence or misrepresentation during the closing process.

Under Connecticut common law, agents owe fiduciary duties that include reasonable care and competence in facilitating the transaction. An agent who fails to ensure proper disclosure delivery, misrepresents the status of title, or negligently communicates incorrect closing figures may face a malpractice claim under their errors and omissions policy—or personally if the conduct falls outside policy coverage.

> "Every person who engages in the practice of real estate shall be responsible for exercising reasonable skill and care in the performance of any act undertaken in that capacity." — CGS Section 20-325a(b)(2)

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. If a title defect surfaces because the agent pressured the attorney to skip steps or failed to allow adequate time for the search, the buyer can terminate under their contractual contingencies. The agent loses the commission, the client loses time, and the brokerage absorbs reputational damage.
