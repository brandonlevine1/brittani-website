## Maryland Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026: The Statute-Level Details Agents Keep Getting Wrong

Missing a single date on a Maryland residential transaction can unravel a deal worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. Understanding Maryland buyer contingency deadlines 2026 is not optional knowledge for licensees—it is the baseline expectation set by the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) and enforced through both regulatory action and civil liability. The Maryland Association of Realtors (MAR) contract forms set default timelines that most agents accept without modification, and that passivity creates risk.

Maryland's regulatory framework sits in Title 17 of the Business Occupations and Professions Article of the Annotated Code of Maryland. MREC has disciplinary authority under Section 17-322, which allows suspension, revocation, or fines up to $25,000 per violation for incompetent or negligent conduct. A blown contingency deadline that causes client harm easily falls within that definition.

## The Core MAR Forms That Govern Deadlines

The primary document controlling buyer contingency deadlines in most Maryland residential transactions is the MAR Residential Contract of Sale (Form 710). This form contains fill-in-the-blank fields where agents specify the number of days allotted for each contingency. If an agent leaves a blank unfilled, the form's printed default controls—and many agents do not realize what those defaults actually say.

Supporting addenda include the MAR Home Inspection Contingency Addendum (Form 712), the Financing Contingency Addendum (Form 720), and the Appraisal Contingency Addendum (Form 721). Each addendum layers its own timeline on top of the base contract, and the deadlines in these addenda run independently of one another. An agent who calendars only the inspection date while ignoring the financing deadline is inviting a malpractice claim.

**The Sale of Buyer's Property Contingency Addendum (Form 730) introduces a separate kick-out clause timeline that interacts with—but does not pause—other contingency periods.** Agents must track the kick-out notice period (typically 72 hours) alongside underlying deadlines. Failure to understand this interaction is one of the top reasons deals collapse in Maryland's suburban markets around the D.C. metro corridor.

## Default Timelines Every Maryland Agent Should Memorize

Below is a comparison of the default contingency periods as printed in the 2025-2026 edition MAR forms. Agents can and should negotiate different periods, but when the blanks are left empty, these are the operative deadlines.

| Contingency | Default Period | Governing Form | Starts Running From |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home Inspection | 15 calendar days | Form 712 | Date of ratification |
| Financing | 21 calendar days | Form 720 | Date of ratification |
| Appraisal | Tied to financing deadline | Form 721 | Date of ratification |
| Sale of Buyer's Property | Negotiated (no printed default) | Form 730 | Date of ratification |
| HOA/Condo Resale Package Review | 5 calendar days | Form 710, Paragraph 12 | Receipt of resale package |

Note the distinction: most deadlines run from ratification, but the HOA/condo review period runs from the date the buyer actually receives the resale package. If the listing agent delays delivery of that package, the buyer's clock does not start. Agents on both sides need to document the delivery date in writing.

**Calendar days means every day counts—including weekends.** The only exception under MAR form language is when the final day of a period lands on a Saturday, Sunday, or Maryland state holiday. In that case, the deadline rolls to the next business day. This rule trips up agents who assume "15 days" gives them until Monday when day 15 is a Friday.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences of missing or mismanaging a contingency deadline operate on three levels: contractual, regulatory, and civil.

At the contractual level, a buyer who fails to act within the stated period typically waives the contingency. Under the MAR Form 712 inspection contingency, if the buyer does not deliver a written request for repairs or a notice of voiding within the 15-day window, the buyer is deemed to have accepted the property's condition. The deposit goes hard on that contingency, and withdrawal means forfeiture.

At the regulatory level, MREC can pursue discipline under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-322(b)(22), which covers "incompetence or untrustworthiness in a manner that endangers the interests of the public." An agent who repeatedly mismanages deadlines—or who fails to advise a client about an approaching deadline—faces investigation. Penalties range from mandatory continuing education to license revocation.

At the civil level, buyers and sellers can sue their agents for negligence. Maryland courts have consistently held that a licensee owes a duty of care that includes proper deadline management. The [duty to track deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) is not merely an administrative convenience—it is a fiduciary obligation under Maryland common law agency principles.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Maryland Contingency Deadlines

**First, agents routinely fail to calculate ratification date correctly.** Ratification occurs when the last party signs and the last party's agent communicates acceptance—not when the document is uploaded to a transaction management system. If a buyer signs on Monday and the listing agent does not communicate acceptance until Wednesday, the clock starts Wednesday. Agents who assume Monday lose two critical days.

Second, agents conflate "delivery" with "receipt." Under MAR contract terms, notices must be delivered to the other party's agent (or directly to the party if unrepresented). An email sent at 11:58 PM on the deadline date may satisfy the requirement, but an email stuck in a spam filter that the recipient never sees creates an evidentiary nightmare. Agents should confirm receipt in writing.

Third, agents forget that contingency removal is not automatic in Maryland. Unlike some states where contingencies expire silently, several MAR addenda require affirmative written notice to remove a contingency or to invoke it. Silence does not always equal waiver—it depends on which addendum controls. Reading the actual form language, paragraph by paragraph, is the only safe approach.

Fourth, agents miscalculate the interaction between the appraisal contingency and the financing contingency. Under Form 721, the appraisal contingency deadline is often tied to the financing deadline unless a separate date is specified. If an agent extends the financing deadline via an amendment but forgets to extend the appraisal contingency, the buyer may lose appraisal protection while still technically having time to secure a loan.

Fifth, agents fail to document deadline extensions properly. A verbal agreement to "give the buyer a few more days" is unenforceable. Maryland requires amendments in writing, and the MAR Amendment/Addendum to Contract form must be executed by all parties before the original deadline expires. An extension signed one day late is legally meaningless—the original deadline controlled.

Brokers bear supervisory liability under COMAR 09.11.02.02, which mandates that brokers "exercise reasonable and adequate supervision" over all licensees operating under their authority. A broker who permits sloppy deadline management across their office is not merely running a disorganized shop—they are violating a regulatory mandate.

## The Financing Contingency: Maryland's Most Litigated Deadline

The financing contingency under Form 720 deserves special attention because it generates more disputes than any other deadline in Maryland residential transactions. The default 21-day period requires the buyer to obtain a written loan commitment—not merely a pre-approval letter—within the specified timeframe. Agents often confuse pre-approval with commitment, and that confusion has cost buyers their earnest money deposits.

Under the MAR form language, if the buyer cannot obtain financing within the stated period, the buyer must provide written notice of inability to obtain financing before the deadline expires. Failure to provide this notice means the contingency is waived. The buyer who simply goes silent—assuming the deal will "work itself out"—has contractually accepted the risk.

Listing agents have a corresponding obligation here. When a financing deadline passes without written notice from the buyer, the listing agent should advise the seller that the contingency has been waived. Failing to inform the seller of this contractual shift is a breach of the listing agent's fiduciary duty. Both sides carry deadline-related obligations, and both sides face liability for inaction.
