## Why Alaska Teams & Supervision Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The growth of real estate teams across Alaska has outpaced many agents' understanding of the rules governing them. Alaska teams & supervision rules 2026 carry specific statutory requirements that differ from the looser frameworks found in the Lower 48, and the Alaska Real Estate Commission has shown increasing willingness to enforce them. Getting this wrong does not just risk a slap on the wrist—it can mean license suspension, deal cancellations, and personal liability that follows you for years.

Alaska's regulatory framework for team operations sits primarily within Alaska Statutes Title 8, Chapter 88 (AS 08.88) and the Alaska Administrative Code at 12 AAC 64. These are not suggestions. They are the binding rules that the [Alaska Real Estate Commission](https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/cbpl/ProfessionalLicensing/RealEstateCommission.aspx) enforces through its disciplinary process, and every agent operating on a team—or thinking about forming one—needs to know exactly what they require.

## How Alaska Defines Teams and Team Structures

Alaska does not have a standalone "team license" category. Every licensee on a team must hold either an active real estate salesperson license or an associate broker license, and every one of those licenses must be affiliated with a single supervising broker under AS 08.88.091. The team itself has no independent legal standing with the Commission.

**This means the broker is the legal hub of every team, period.** A team leader who is a salesperson—not an associate broker—cannot independently supervise other licensees. The supervising broker retains all supervisory authority and liability regardless of internal team hierarchies or profit-sharing arrangements.

Team names also face strict requirements under 12 AAC 64.550. Any advertising, signage, or marketing material used by a team must include the licensed brokerage name in a manner that is "clearly visible and reasonably prominent." A team that markets under its own brand without the brokerage name is in violation, and the Commission treats this as a serious advertising infraction rather than a technicality.

## Supervision Standards Under AS 08.88.396

The core supervision statute, AS 08.88.396, places an affirmative duty on brokers to supervise all licensees operating under their brokerage. This is not a passive obligation. The Commission expects brokers to maintain written policies, review transactions, and ensure every team member complies with disclosure and documentation requirements.

For teams specifically, brokers must confirm that the team's internal agreements—compensation splits, lead distribution, administrative roles—do not create situations where unlicensed individuals perform activities that require a license. Administrative assistants on a team can handle scheduling and paperwork logistics, but they cannot negotiate terms, show property with the intent to sell, or discuss pricing strategy with clients. Crossing that line violates AS 08.88.900, which defines the activities requiring licensure.

**Brokers who delegate supervision to a team leader without maintaining their own oversight are still liable.** The Commission has made clear in past disciplinary actions that a broker cannot contractually transfer supervisory responsibility to a non-broker licensee. Internal team structures that attempt to do so offer zero protection when a complaint is filed.

## Forms and Documentation Alaska Teams Must Use

Alaska's transaction documentation requirements apply equally to team members and solo agents, but the team context introduces additional complexity around who signs what and whose name appears where. The Alaska Real Estate Commission requires specific forms at defined points in every transaction.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Team-Specific Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Licensee Disclosure (AS 08.88.396) | Disclose licensee status to all parties | Each team member involved must be identified by name and license number |
| Agency Disclosure (12 AAC 64.110) | Confirm agency relationship | Must name the brokerage, not just the team |
| Consent to Dual Agency (12 AAC 64.120) | Authorize dual agency if applicable | Dual agency within a team triggers this—both buyer and seller agents on the same team require written consent |
| Transfer Disclosure (AS 34.70.010-.200) | Residential property condition disclosure | Supervising broker must ensure timely delivery regardless of which team member handles the listing |

The dual agency scenario is particularly dangerous for teams. When one team member represents the buyer and another represents the seller in the same transaction, dual agency is triggered at the brokerage level under Alaska law. The Consent to Dual Agency form at 12 AAC 64.120 must be executed before the team proceeds. Many agents assume that being on the same "team" but working different sides avoids dual agency—it does not. The Commission evaluates agency at the brokerage level, and all team members share the same brokerage affiliation.

## Common Mistakes Alaska Team Agents Make

Five recurring errors account for the majority of team-related complaints filed with the Alaska Real Estate Commission. Avoiding them is not difficult, but it requires intentional attention to detail.

**First, teams fail to include the brokerage name in advertising.** This is the single most common violation flagged during Commission reviews. Social media posts, yard signs, and even email signatures that display only the team name without the brokerage violate 12 AAC 64.550. The fix is simple: every piece of marketing must include the brokerage name prominently.

Second, teams allow unlicensed assistants to perform licensed activities. An administrative assistant who answers client questions about contract terms or conducts a showing without a licensed agent present is performing work that requires a license under AS 08.88.900. The team leader and broker both face discipline when this occurs, and the Commission does not accept ignorance of the assistant's activities as a defense.

Third, teams mishandle dual agency disclosures. As noted above, two members of the same team representing opposite sides of a transaction creates dual agency at the brokerage level. Failure to obtain written consent before proceeding is a violation of 12 AAC 64.120 and can void the transaction entirely if a party later objects.

Fourth, team members fail to identify themselves individually on transaction documents. Alaska requires that each licensee involved in a transaction be identifiable by name and license number. Signing documents with only the team name or the team leader's information when another licensee performed material work on the deal violates disclosure requirements.

Fifth, brokers rubber-stamp team transactions without meaningful review. The Commission distinguishes between a broker who reviews files and one who simply signs off. Disciplinary records show that brokers who cannot demonstrate active supervision—file notes, documented reviews, training records—face harsher penalties when team members make errors.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce for Team Compliance

Brokers carry the heaviest regulatory burden when it comes to Alaska team operations. The Commission's enforcement posture places the broker at the center of every compliance failure, so proactive auditing is not optional—it is self-preservation.

**Every broker supervising a team should conduct quarterly audits covering at minimum five areas.** These audits need to be documented in writing and retained for at least three years, the standard record-retention period the Commission references in investigations.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | All team marketing materials for brokerage name inclusion | Team name appears without brokerage name on any platform |
| Agency disclosures | Every transaction file for properly executed disclosure forms | Missing signatures, unsigned Consent to Dual Agency forms |
| Licensee identification | Transaction documents for individual licensee names and numbers | Only team leader listed when multiple agents worked the deal |
| Unlicensed activity | Administrative staff duties vs. licensed activity boundaries | Assistants communicating contract terms or negotiating with parties |
| Continuing education | Each team member's CE status and renewal deadlines | Lapsed licenses operating under the team without active status |

Brokers should also maintain a written supervisory plan that specifically addresses team operations. This document should outline how the broker reviews team transactions, how often reviews occur, and what corrective actions apply when violations are discovered. The Commission has referenced the existence or absence of such plans in multiple disciplinary proceedings as evidence of whether a broker met the supervisory standard under AS 08.88.396.

Record retention matters as well. Alaska requires brokers to maintain transaction records for a minimum of three years after closing under 12 AAC 64.540. For teams, this means the broker—not the team leader—must have access to and custody of all files. Team leaders who maintain their own filing systems outside the broker's control create a compliance gap that becomes a serious problem during Commission investigations.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance: Fines, Suspensions, and Deal Fallout

The consequences of violating Alaska's team supervision rules are concrete and escalating. The Alaska Real Estate Commission has authority under AS 08.88.071 to impose civil fines up to $2,500 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional education as a condition of reinstatement.

For advertising violations under 12 AAC 64.550, the Commission typically begins with a written warning for first offenses but moves to fines quickly upon repeat violations. License suspension becomes a real possibility when advertising violations are paired with other infractions, such as missing agency disclosures.

**Dual agency violations can unravel closed transactions.** If a party discovers that proper consent was never obtained under 12 AAC 64.120, they may have grounds to rescind the contract. The financial fallout extends beyond the commission—both parties may pursue damages against the licensees and the brokerage for losses incurred due to the failed transaction.

Brokers face the highest exposure. Under AS 08.88.396, a broker found to have inadequately supervised team members may lose their broker license entirely. Reinstatement after revocation is not guaranteed and requires a formal petition to the Commission. The reputational damage alone—disciplinary actions are public record—can be devastating to a brokerage's ability to recruit and retain agents.

## Building a Team That Survives a Commission Review

The teams that operate cleanly in Alaska share a few traits: documented processes, clear role definitions, and a broker who treats supervision as a daily responsibility rather than an annual checkbox. Written team agreements should specify which agents handle which functions, how dual agency situations will be identified and managed, and how files will be stored and reviewed.

Training is another differentiator. Teams that hold regular internal compliance sessions—covering form updates, advertising rules, and agency disclosure refreshers—generate far fewer complaints. The Commission looks favorably on brokerages that can demonstrate proactive education efforts when isolated violations do occur.
