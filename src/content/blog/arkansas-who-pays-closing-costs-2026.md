## Understanding Arkansas Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of "Arkansas who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026" is not answered by a single statute or bright-line rule. Instead, it depends on contract language, local custom, and the specific forms agents use in each transaction. Agents who assume custom equals law expose themselves and their brokers to liability.

Arkansas is somewhat unusual in that it has no real estate transfer tax and no statutory mandate assigning most closing costs to one party or the other. The allocation lives almost entirely inside the purchase agreement. That makes form selection and accurate completion the single highest-risk compliance point for agents handling closings in this state.

The [Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC)](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/) regulates agent conduct and enforces disclosure requirements. If you write a contract that misallocates costs or fails to disclose who is responsible for a specific fee, AREC has enforcement authority under Arkansas Code Annotated Section 17-42-311.

## The Default Framework: What Arkansas Law Actually Says

**Arkansas has no documentary stamp tax, no state-mandated seller concession cap, and no statute requiring either party to pay title insurance.** That void means agents cannot fall back on "the law says the seller pays" language when advising clients. Every cost allocation must be documented in writing.

Arkansas Code 17-42-311(a)(1) lists "making any substantial misrepresentation" as grounds for disciplinary action. Telling a buyer that "sellers always pay the owner's title policy in Arkansas" is a misrepresentation if the contract does not specify it. The same applies to agents who verbally promise seller-paid closing costs without memorializing the agreement in the contract.

The Arkansas Real Estate Commission's Standard Form 1 (Purchase Agreement) and Form 3 (Counter Offer) are the primary vehicles for allocating closing costs. AREC revised these forms in 2024 and again in early 2026, adding clearer blanks for cost allocation following the NAR settlement changes around buyer-agent compensation.

While no law dictates the split, strong local customs have developed across the state. These customs vary by county and even by title company territory. The table below reflects the most common allocation patterns agents encounter in 2026 Arkansas transactions.

## Buyer-Agent Compensation and Its Impact on Closing Costs

The 2024 NAR settlement reshaped how buyer-agent compensation flows through Arkansas transactions. Under the new framework, offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer appear in MLS listings. This means agents must address compensation in the purchase agreement or in a separate written buyer-broker agreement.

AREC adopted a revised Buyer Agency Agreement form (Form 201) in late 2024 that requires agents to disclose how and by whom their compensation will be paid. If a buyer requests that the seller cover buyer-agent compensation as part of closing costs, that request must appear in AREC Form 1 or a counter offer. Verbal side agreements are insufficient and constitute a violation of Commission Rule 10.14, which mandates that material terms be reduced to writing.

**Agents who fail to document compensation terms in the contract risk both AREC discipline and loss of their right to collect.** Arkansas courts have consistently held that oral agreements for real estate commissions are unenforceable under the state's Statute of Frauds, codified at Arkansas Code 4-59-101.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Cost Allocation

Experienced agents still get burned by these errors. The five most frequent mistakes AREC investigators encounter in closing cost disputes involve form completion failures, not substantive law misunderstandings.

**First, leaving Section 8 of AREC Form 1 blank or incomplete.** When agents skip cost allocation fields, the contract is ambiguous. Title companies will call the listing agent for clarification, but any verbal instruction is unenforceable. The result is a last-minute dispute that delays closing or kills the deal outright.

Second, promising seller concessions that exceed lender limits. FHA, VA, and USDA loans each cap seller concessions differently. An agent who writes a $15,000 seller concession on a $200,000 FHA transaction (where the cap is 6% or $12,000) creates an immediate contract problem. The deal must be amended, re-submitted to underwriting, and often renegotiated.

Third, conflating "seller-paid closing costs" with "seller credit toward buyer's closing costs." These are legally distinct. A seller-paid cost means the seller directly pays a specific vendor. A seller credit is a dollar amount credited to the buyer at closing. Mixing them up on the HUD-1 successor (the TRID Closing Disclosure) triggers compliance issues with the lender.

Fourth, failing to account for proration disputes. Arkansas property taxes are paid in arrears. If agents do not specify in the contract how taxes will be prorated, sellers and buyers argue at closing over who owes what portion of the current tax year.

Fifth, using outdated forms. AREC updates forms regularly. Agents using a 2023 version of Form 1 in 2026 are working with an obsolete document that may omit fields for buyer-agent compensation allocation. AREC Rule 10.8 requires agents to use the most current Commission-approved forms.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under Arkansas Code 17-42-311(a)(7) for the acts of their affiliated agents. A single agent's form error can generate a complaint against the broker's license. Broker-level compliance requires active auditing, not passive trust.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Form version control | All agents using current AREC Form 1 and Form 201 | AREC citation, contract voidability |

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

AREC has authority to impose fines up to $1,000 per violation under Arkansas Code 17-42-311. Repeated violations or those involving consumer financial harm can result in license suspension for up to one year or permanent revocation. The Commission publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and closing cost misrepresentation appears in roughly 12% of recent enforcement actions.

Beyond regulatory discipline, agents face civil liability. If a buyer arrives at closing expecting the seller to pay $8,000 in costs and the contract is silent on the issue, the buyer's recourse may include a claim against the agent for negligent misrepresentation. Arkansas courts have awarded damages in cases where agents made verbal promises about cost allocation that contradicted or were absent from the written contract.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate consequence. When a closing cost dispute surfaces 48 hours before closing, neither party wants to absorb an unexpected $5,000-$10,000 expense. ## Practical Steps for Getting It Right Every Time

Start with the current AREC Form 1. Fill every field in Section 8. If a cost is not applicable, write "N/A" rather than leaving it blank. Specify dollar amounts for seller credits rather than percentages when possible, because lender guidelines reference specific dollar caps.

Confirm the buyer's loan program and its concession limits before drafting the offer. A two-minute phone call to the lender's processor prevents a two-week contract amendment cycle. Document that confirmation in your file notes.

Use AREC Form 201 (Buyer Agency Agreement) to establish how buyer-agent compensation will be paid and by whom. If the buyer wants to request that the seller cover that compensation, include the request in the purchase offer. Never rely on a separate verbal understanding, regardless of how cooperative the other side appears.
