South Dakota sits in a small club of states that still call their conveyance levy a "transfer fee" rather than a transfer tax, but the distinction is mostly semantic—money still changes hands at every closing. Understanding South Dakota transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 is not optional knowledge for licensed agents and brokers operating in the state. Miscalculating these numbers can delay recordings, blow up settlement statements, and expose your brokerage to liability claims from clients who expected different bottom-line figures.

This post breaks down the exact statutes, forms, fee amounts, common agent errors, and broker-level audit obligations you need to have locked down before your next closing.

## How South Dakota's Transfer Fee Actually Works

South Dakota's conveyance transfer fee is governed by [SDCL Chapter 43-28](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/Codified_Laws/DisplayStatute.aspx?Type=Statute&Statute=43-28), specifically sections 43-28-21 through 43-28-29. The fee is $0.50 for every $500 (or fraction thereof) of the actual consideration paid for the real property being conveyed. That translates to a rate of $1.00 per $1,000 of sale price—simple math, but agents still get it wrong more often than you would expect.

The fee must be paid to the county Register of Deeds at the time of recording. Payment is evidenced by documentary stamps or, more commonly today, a direct payment noted on the deed itself. If no stamps or payment accompany the deed, the Register of Deeds is required by SDCL 43-28-22 to refuse the document.

**Certain transfers are exempt from the fee entirely.** Exemptions are listed in SDCL 43-28-24 and include conveyances between spouses, transfers to or from governmental entities, deeds executed to correct a prior instrument, and transfers where the actual consideration is $100 or less. Agents handling estate sales, divorces, or government-acquired properties should verify exemption eligibility before drafting the settlement statement.

Here is a side-by-side breakdown of the primary costs agents need to account for at recording:

| Fee Type | Statutory Authority | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer fee | SDCL 43-28-21 | $0.50 per $500 of consideration | Applies to all non-exempt conveyances |
| Base recording fee (first page) | SDCL 7-9-7 | $30.00 | Applies to deeds, mortgages, liens |
| Additional pages | SDCL 7-9-7 | $2.00 per page | Each page beyond the first |
| Non-standard document surcharge | Varies by county | $0–$25 | Applies when formatting requirements are not met |
| Mortgage registration fee | SDCL 43-28-17 | $0.50 per $500 of debt | Paid when recording a new mortgage |

For a $350,000 residential sale, the transfer fee alone comes to $700. Add in the recording fee for a standard two-page warranty deed ($32), and the buyer's new mortgage registration fee on a $280,000 loan ($560), and you are looking at $1,292 in combined recording-related charges before you even factor in title insurance or other settlement costs. Agents who ball-park these numbers on a net sheet instead of calculating them precisely invite problems at the closing table.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

South Dakota does not mandate a single statewide closing form the way some states do, but several documents are directly relevant to transfer fee and recording compliance.

The South Dakota Real Estate Commission publishes and regulates standard disclosure and agency forms, but closing documents typically flow through title companies or attorneys. The key form for transfer fee purposes is the deed itself—usually a Warranty Deed or Quit Claim Deed—which must include the consideration statement or be accompanied by a Declaration of Consideration (sometimes called a Statement of Full Consideration) so the Register of Deeds can calculate the correct transfer fee. Some counties provide their own fillable declaration forms; Minnehaha and Pennington counties each have local versions available at their Register of Deeds offices.

Agents should also be familiar with the South Dakota Real Estate Commission's Property Condition Disclosure Statement and the Lead-Based Paint Disclosure form (for pre-1978 homes), as incomplete disclosure packages can hold up recording when title companies refuse to proceed. For a deeper look at how [missing deadlines torpedo deals](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), consider how a rejected recording cascades backward through a transaction timeline.

The mortgage registration fee is calculated separately from the transfer fee and applies to the debt instrument. If a buyer's lender records a mortgage alongside the deed, the Register of Deeds collects $0.50 per $500 of the principal amount of the mortgage under SDCL 43-28-17. Agents do not usually handle this payment directly, but you need to reflect it accurately on buyer cost estimates.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

**Failing to account for the correct transfer fee can produce consequences that go well beyond an awkward phone call.** Under SDCL 43-28-22, a deed submitted without the proper fee payment simply will not be recorded. That means the buyer does not have a recorded instrument establishing their interest in the property, and the title company's commitment conditions remain unsatisfied.

A delayed recording creates a gap in the chain of title. During that gap, a judgment lien, a second conveyance by a dishonest seller, or even a bankruptcy filing can intervene. The agent who caused the delay by providing incorrect figures on a net sheet or estimated closing statement may face a professional liability claim from the client or the title company seeking indemnification.

Beyond civil liability, the South Dakota Real Estate Commission has authority under SDCL 36-21A to discipline licensees for negligence, incompetence, or conduct that harms a client's interest. Repeated fee miscalculations could trigger a complaint, an investigation, and ultimately a fine, license suspension, or required continuing education. ## Five Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Fees and Recording Costs

**First, agents miscalculate the transfer fee by using the listed price instead of the actual consideration.** The statute says "actual consideration," which can differ from the listing price after credits, concessions, or personal property carve-outs. If the buyer receives a $10,000 seller concession applied to closing costs but the sale price stays at $350,000, the transfer fee is still based on $350,000 because the consideration for the real property did not change. But if personal property (say, $5,000 in farm equipment) is separated from the real estate sale and documented in a bill of sale, that amount may legitimately reduce the consideration subject to the fee.

Second, agents forget that the mortgage registration fee exists. It shows up on the buyer's side, and newer agents routinely omit it from preliminary cost worksheets. On a $300,000 mortgage, that is a $600 surprise at settlement.

Third, agents assume transfer fee responsibility defaults to the buyer. South Dakota custom in most markets places the transfer fee on the seller, but "custom" is not statute. The purchase agreement controls. If the contract is silent, a dispute can erupt at the closing table over who owes the $700 or $1,000 charge.

Fourth, agents submit documents that do not meet county formatting requirements—wrong margins, missing legal descriptions, or type that is too small. The Register of Deeds can impose a non-standard document surcharge or reject the filing outright. Check SDCL 43-28-9.1 for base formatting standards and your specific county's supplemental rules.

Fifth, agents fail to verify exemptions before claiming them. A transfer between former spouses after a divorce decree is exempt under SDCL 43-28-24, but a transfer between unmarried co-owners dissolving a joint investment is not. Claiming a false exemption can result in the deed being rejected or, worse, the county pursuing the unpaid fee after recording.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under SDCL 36-21A-69 for every transaction their agents touch. That means transfer fee and recording cost accuracy is a brokerage compliance issue, not just an individual agent problem.

Start with net sheet and estimated closing statement reviews. Every listing-side net sheet your agents produce should show the correct transfer fee calculated at $1.00 per $1,000 of anticipated sale price, with a notation that the final amount depends on actual consideration. Buyer-side estimates should include the mortgage registration fee on the anticipated loan amount. If your agents are using boilerplate net sheet templates that have not been updated for 2026 county-level recording fees, those templates are a liability.

**Brokers should also audit purchase agreements for clear transfer fee allocation language.** A contract that says nothing about who pays the transfer fee is a contract waiting to blow up at closing. Your office's standard addenda or contract provisions should address this point explicitly, even if local custom dictates a particular allocation.

Finally, implement a post-closing file review that verifies the deed was actually recorded and the recording information (book, page, or document number) is in the transaction file. A signed deed sitting in a drawer because someone forgot to submit it—or because it was rejected for an unpaid fee—is a ticking time bomb. ## Keeping Your Practice Clean Going Forward

South Dakota's transfer fee and recording fee structure has remained relatively stable, but county-level surcharges and administrative fees can shift without fanfare. Subscribe to your county Register of Deeds' updates or check their website at the start of each quarter. Pennington, Minnehaha, Lincoln, and Brown counties each maintain online fee schedules that reflect current charges.

The [South Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/) is the licensing authority that governs agent and broker conduct statewide. If you have a question about whether a particular fee calculation or exemption applies, the Commission's office can point you to the right statutory section, and your title company's closer should be your first phone call on any ambiguous recording issue.
