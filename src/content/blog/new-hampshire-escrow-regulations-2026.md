## Understanding New Hampshire Escrow Regulations 2026

The rules governing escrow accounts in New Hampshire are not suggestions—they are statutory obligations that carry real consequences for agents and brokers who fail to comply. **New Hampshire escrow regulations 2026 remain rooted in RSA 331-A (the New Hampshire Real Estate Practice Act) and the administrative rules published by the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC) under Rea 700.** If you hold a license in this state, your ability to handle client funds correctly is not just an ethical imperative; it is a condition of maintaining that license.

This year brings no sweeping legislative overhaul, but several enforcement patterns from 2025 disciplinary actions signal that NHREC is increasing scrutiny on deposit timelines and record retention. Agents who relied on informal practices—verbal agreements about deposit timing, for example—are finding themselves on the wrong side of formal complaints. The stakes are clear: fines, license actions, deal collapse, and civil litigation all sit on the table when escrow goes sideways.

## The Statutory Framework: RSA 331-A and Rea 700 Rules

New Hampshire's escrow requirements originate in two primary sources. RSA 331-A:26 establishes the legal duty for principal brokers to maintain trust accounts, deposit funds promptly, and disburse only upon lawful authorization. The NHREC's administrative rules under [Rea 700](https://www.gencourt.state.nh.us/rules/state_agencies/rea.html) flesh out the operational details—what bank qualifications matter, how records must be maintained, and what constitutes a violation.

RSA 331-A:26, III mandates that earnest money deposits be placed into an escrow or trust account within 5 business days of receipt by the broker or the broker's agent. The account must be held at a federally insured financial institution located in New Hampshire. Brokers cannot earn interest on client funds unless a separate written agreement exists between the parties, and even then, the interest arrangement must comply with Rea 701.02.

Rea 701.01 specifies that escrow accounts must be clearly labeled as trust or escrow accounts, separate from any operating funds. The principal broker's name must appear on the account. Failure to maintain this separation is the textbook definition of commingling, which remains the single most common cause of license revocation in NHREC disciplinary proceedings.

## Deposit Timelines and Form Requirements

**New Hampshire does not give agents the luxury of ambiguity when it comes to deposit deadlines.** The 5-business-day rule under RSA 331-A:26, III begins on the date the agent physically or constructively receives the funds—not the date the offer is accepted, and not the date the broker becomes aware of the deposit.

Agents in New Hampshire typically document earnest money terms using the NHAR (New Hampshire Association of Realtors) Form 109, the standard Purchase and Sale Agreement. The deposit amount, delivery method, and holder identity are specified in this form. The NHAR Form 68 (Deposit Receipt) serves as the written acknowledgment that funds were received, and it should be completed at the moment of receipt with a copy provided to the depositor.

| Requirement | Statute/Rule | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit into trust account | RSA 331-A:26, III | 5 business days from receipt |
| Record retention | Rea 701.04 | 7 years from final disposition |
| Account reconciliation | Rea 701.03 | Monthly, documented |
| Interest disclosure (if applicable) | Rea 701.02 | Before deposit acceptance |
| Trust account location | RSA 331-A:26, II | NH-based, FDIC-insured institution |

The monthly reconciliation requirement under Rea 701.03 is not optional. Brokers must reconcile the escrow account balance against individual ledger cards for each transaction, and the reconciliation must be in writing. This is a frequent target of NHREC audits.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, License Actions, and Civil Exposure

Violations of escrow rules in New Hampshire trigger a cascade of consequences that extend well beyond a sternly worded letter. NHREC has authority under RSA 331-A:28 to impose disciplinary sanctions ranging from a written reprimand to outright license revocation. Fines can reach $2,000 per violation, and each day a violation continues may constitute a separate offense.

**License suspension is not theoretical.** In 2024 and 2025, NHREC published multiple disciplinary orders involving agents who failed to deposit earnest money within the statutory window or who allowed personal funds to comingle in trust accounts. In at least two cases, the violations surfaced only after deals collapsed and buyers filed complaints—by that point, the broker's exposure included both the regulatory penalty and a civil claim for conversion or breach of fiduciary duty.

Beyond the commission's jurisdiction, agents face civil liability under common law. A buyer whose deposit is mishandled can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, negligence, or conversion. The brokerage's Errors and Omissions policy may or may not cover the claim depending on whether the insurer classifies the agent's conduct as a knowing violation. Courts in New Hampshire have historically treated escrow mishandling as a serious breach of the agent's duties under RSA 331-A:25-a, which defines the fiduciary obligations owed to clients.

Deal cancellation is another practical outcome. If a buyer or seller discovers that escrow funds were not deposited on time or were placed in the wrong account, the aggrieved party may assert a material breach of the purchase agreement. This can unwind an otherwise clean transaction, leaving the agent liable for damages, lost commissions, and the cost of remarketing the property.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With New Hampshire Escrow

Patterns emerge from NHREC disciplinary files and E&O claims. These five errors account for the majority of escrow-related violations in the state.

**First: holding checks "pending acceptance."** Some agents believe they should not deposit earnest money until the seller accepts the offer. RSA 331-A:26, III does not condition the deposit obligation on acceptance. Unless the purchase agreement explicitly states otherwise—and NHAR Form 109 includes language allowing parties to modify the default—the 5-business-day clock starts at receipt regardless of the offer's status.

Second: using a personal account as a temporary holding vehicle. This is commingling, full stop. Even if the agent intends to transfer the funds to the brokerage trust account the next morning, the deposit into a personal checking or savings account violates Rea 701.01. No exceptions exist for weekends, holidays, or broker unavailability.

Third: failing to document cash deposits. When a buyer tenders earnest money in cash—rare but not unheard of in certain rural NH markets—agents sometimes neglect to issue NHAR Form 68 or to create a paper trail showing the chain of custody. Without documentation, the broker cannot reconcile the deposit, and the agent becomes the weak link in an audit.

Fourth: miscommunicating the escrow holder's identity. Form 109 requires clear identification of who holds the deposit. Agents who fill in "broker" without specifying the principal broker's name and brokerage, or who leave the field blank with the intent to "figure it out later," create ambiguity that can ripen into a complaint if the deal sours. The remedy is simple: complete the form fully at the time of drafting, as discussed in [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Fifth: releasing escrow funds without written mutual authorization. Under RSA 331-A:26, IV, no funds may be disbursed from the trust account except upon written agreement of all parties or by court order. Agents who release deposits based on a phone call, a text message, or a verbal instruction from one party expose themselves and their brokerage to immediate liability.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

The principal broker bears ultimate responsibility for escrow compliance under RSA 331-A:26. This is non-delegable. Even if the brokerage employs a transaction coordinator or bookkeeper to manage trust account logistics, the principal broker remains personally accountable for every dollar in that account.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Source Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | Rea 701.03 |
| Individual transaction ledger review | Per transaction | Rea 701.03 |
| Verification of deposit timing | Per transaction | RSA 331-A:26, III |
| Confirmation of proper account labeling | Annually (minimum) | Rea 701.01 |
| Record retention verification | Ongoing | Rea 701.04 |
| Agent training documentation | Annually | Best practice / NHREC guidance |

Brokers should implement a system—whether manual or automated—that flags any deposit not confirmed within 3 business days of an agent reporting receipt of funds. The 2-day buffer gives the broker time to intervene before the statutory deadline passes. This is an area where tools like those covered in [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) become relevant to preventing compliance failures.

Monthly reconciliation must produce a written record showing that the total of all individual transaction ledgers matches the bank statement balance. Discrepancies, even small ones, must be investigated and resolved within 5 business days. NHREC auditors will review these reconciliations without advance notice, and an incomplete or missing reconciliation for any month creates a presumption of non-compliance.

**Brokers who supervise teams face additional exposure.** If an agent on a team receives earnest money and the team leader—rather than the principal broker—is managing the deposit process, the principal broker must still verify compliance. Team structures do not create a buffer between the agent's actions and the broker's liability. The chain of accountability runs straight to the top of the brokerage, and NHREC has made clear in recent guidance bulletins that delegation does not equal absolution.

## Handling Disputed Escrow: Interpleader and the 10-Day Rule

When a transaction collapses and both parties claim the earnest money, the broker faces a decision point. RSA 331-A:26, IV provides that the broker may not disburse disputed funds to either party without mutual written consent or a court order. In practice, this means the funds sit in escrow until the dispute resolves.

New Hampshire brokers may file an interpleader action in superior court to deposit the disputed funds with the court and extract themselves from the conflict. This is the cleanest path when the parties refuse to agree. The broker should retain counsel for the filing, and the cost of the interpleader may be deducted from the escrowed funds if the purchase agreement permits it—a clause worth including in your standard addenda.

The informal "10-day demand rule" referenced by many NH practitioners is not statutory—it is a common contractual provision in NHAR Form 109 variants that allows one party to demand release and gives the other party 10 days to object in writing. If no objection is received, the broker may release the funds to the demanding party. Agents must read their specific contract language carefully; this provision is not automatic and does not override RSA 331-A:26, IV where the contract is silent.
