## Understanding New Hampshire Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of New Hampshire who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 trips up agents more often than you might expect. Despite decades of local custom, the answer is never purely "custom" — it is a combination of statute, contract language, and lender requirements that agents must track at every stage. Getting this wrong exposes your brokerage to liability, delays closings, and erodes client trust at the worst possible moment.

New Hampshire is an attorney-state for closings, meaning licensed attorneys typically conduct the settlement. That structural reality shifts certain cost responsibilities compared to title-company states. Agents who transplant assumptions from other jurisdictions — or rely on outdated boilerplate — frequently miscalculate who owes what and when.

## The Statutory Framework: RSA 78-B and RSA 331-A

**New Hampshire's real property transfer tax is governed by RSA 78-B, which sets the rate at $15.00 per $1,000 of the sale price, split evenly between buyer and seller at $7.50 each.** This statutory split applies by default. However, RSA 78-B:2 permits the parties to contractually reallocate the tax in the purchase and sale agreement, meaning agents must read — and correctly draft — that provision every single time.

Beyond transfer tax, the [New Hampshire Real Estate Commission](https://www.oplc.nh.gov/real-estate-commission) enforces professional standards under RSA 331-A. Section 331-A:26 enumerates prohibited conduct, including misrepresentation of material facts related to a transaction. Misstating or failing to disclose closing cost allocation qualifies as a material misrepresentation, and the Commission can impose fines of up to $3,000 per violation, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely.

RSA 331-A:25-b also requires licensees to provide written disclosure of agency relationships, which includes fiduciary duties around financial guidance. If you advise a buyer that "the seller always pays X" without contractual backing, you are making a representation that could later be deemed misleading.

## Standard Cost Allocation: Who Pays What by Default

New Hampshire does not have a single statute that assigns every closing cost to one party. Instead, local custom — reinforced through the New Hampshire Association of Realtors (NHAR) standard forms — creates the baseline. The NHAR Form 20 (Standard Purchase and Sale Agreement) contains specific paragraphs addressing cost allocation, and agents who deviate without clear addenda language invite disputes.

| Cost Item | Typically Buyer | Typically Seller |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|
| Transfer Tax (RSA 78-B) | 50% ($7.50/thousand) | 50% ($7.50/thousand) |
| Owner's Title Insurance Premium | Yes | No |
| Lender's Title Insurance Premium | Yes | No |
| Attorney Fee (own counsel) | Yes | Yes |
| Deed Preparation | No | Yes |
| Recording Fees (deed) | Yes (some split) | No |
| Recording Fees (mortgage) | Yes | No |
| Property Tax Proration | Adjusted at closing | Adjusted at closing |
| Real Estate Commission | No | Yes |
| Septic/Well Inspection | Negotiable | Negotiable |

**This table reflects prevailing custom, not law.** Every item except the transfer tax default can be shifted by contract. Agents must ensure the NHAR Form 20 language — or a properly drafted addendum — reflects what was actually negotiated, not what the parties assumed.

## Key Forms Agents Must Use Correctly

The NHAR publishes the forms most New Hampshire agents rely on. The critical ones for closing cost allocation include NHAR Form 20 (Purchase and Sale Agreement), NHAR Form 70 (Estimated Closing Costs), and NHAR Form 60 (Addendum to Purchase and Sale). Agents should also cross-reference the federal TRID Closing Disclosure (CFPB form), which lenders provide but agents must review for accuracy against the contract.

NHAR Form 70 is where many agents stumble. This estimated cost sheet is not a binding contract provision — it is an estimate provided for buyer or seller education. If the numbers on Form 70 conflict with the allocation language in Form 20, the purchase and sale agreement controls. Agents who hand a buyer Form 70 without reconciling it to the signed agreement create confusion that surfaces at the closing table.

Form 60, the general addendum, is the appropriate vehicle for any cost-shifting negotiation that deviates from the Form 20 defaults. Writing "seller to pay buyer's transfer tax share" on a napkin or in an email does not satisfy the [requirements for enforceable contract modifications](/blog/where-deals-break-down) under New Hampshire law.

## Seller Concessions and Lender Limits

Seller concessions — where the seller credits the buyer for a portion of closing costs — are common in buyer's markets. In New Hampshire, these credits appear in Paragraph 10 of NHAR Form 20 or in a Form 60 addendum. Agents must understand that lender guidelines cap the permissible concession amount, typically between 2% and 6% of the purchase price depending on loan type and down payment.

Exceeding the lender cap does not just reduce the credit — it can kill the deal entirely if the appraiser or underwriter flags the concession as artificially inflating the purchase price. Agents who promise buyers a 6% seller credit on a 3%-down conventional loan without checking current Fannie Mae guidelines are setting up a last-minute renegotiation or cancellation.

**Document every concession in the contract itself, not in side agreements or verbal understandings.** The closing attorney will prepare the settlement statement from the signed purchase and sale agreement. If the concession is not in writing there, it does not exist at the closing table.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in New Hampshire Closings

The following errors appear repeatedly in disciplinary actions, E&O claims, and deal blow-ups across New Hampshire brokerages.

First, agents assume the transfer tax split is non-negotiable. Because RSA 78-B defaults to a 50/50 split, many agents tell clients "that is the law" without explaining that the contract can reallocate it. This becomes a problem when a seller agrees to pay the full transfer tax as a concession and the listing agent never drafted the language, leaving the buyer's side scrambling at closing.

Second, agents fail to update NHAR Form 70 when terms change during negotiation. A buyer receives an initial cost estimate at offer, the inspection negotiation shifts $3,000 in repair credits to the seller, and nobody revises the estimate. The buyer arrives at closing expecting to bring $12,000 and the actual figure is $15,000. This breeds complaints to the Commission.

Third, agents confuse "recording fees" with "transfer tax." Recording fees in New Hampshire are set by the county Registry of Deeds — typically around $25 for the first page and $4 per additional page — and are separate from the RSA 78-B transfer tax. Lumping them together on an estimate misleads clients and can trigger a violation under RSA 331-A:26(II)(g) for inaccurate representation of transaction costs.

Fourth, agents in dual-agency transactions fail to ensure both parties independently understand their cost obligations. Under RSA 331-A:25-d, a disclosed dual agent owes both parties limited fiduciary duties, but cannot advocate for one side's financial advantage. If the dual agent structures cost allocation that favors one party without clear written consent from both, the Commission views this as a breach.

Fifth, agents neglect to account for [prorated property taxes](/blog/west-virginia-who-pays-closing-costs-2026) at the time of offer. New Hampshire property taxes are billed semi-annually, and the proration at closing can represent thousands of dollars. Failing to estimate this accurately on Form 70 means the buyer's cash-to-close figure is wrong.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under RSA 331-A:16. The Commission holds designated brokers accountable for the acts of their licensees, and "I didn't know" is not a defense when an agent misallocates closing costs or fails to use proper forms.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of closed transaction files to verify that NHAR Form 70 estimates were updated to reflect final negotiated terms.** Check that every seller concession references a specific dollar amount or percentage in Form 20 or a Form 60 addendum. Confirm that dual-agency transactions include signed acknowledgment from both parties regarding cost allocation.

Additionally, brokers must verify that agents are not providing legal advice about closing cost allocation. New Hampshire's unauthorized practice of law rules (enforced by the NH Supreme Court under Rule 35) prohibit non-attorneys from advising clients on the legal effect of contract provisions. An agent can explain custom and fill in NHAR forms, but cannot advise a client on whether a particular cost-shifting clause is enforceable. Brokers who catch agents crossing this line need to intervene immediately — the liability extends to the brokerage.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Form 70 accuracy | Final estimate matches closing disclosure within 10% | Client complaints, E&O claims |
| Concession documentation | Dollar amount in Form 20 or Form 60 | Deal cancellation, Commission complaint |
| Dual-agency cost disclosure | Both parties signed acknowledgment | RSA 331-A:25-d violation, fine up to $3,000 |
| Transfer tax allocation | Contract language matches HUD/CD line items | Escrow shortage, delayed closing |
| Attorney referral disclosure | Written disclosure if agent refers client to specific attorney | RSA 331-A:26(II)(o) kickback violation |

Brokers should also track whether agents are [missing critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) around cost disclosures. The TRID three-day rule means buyers must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. If an agent's delay in providing final contract terms to the closing attorney pushes the CD delivery past deadline, the closing gets postponed — and the brokerage bears the reputational cost.

## Handling Disputes When Cost Allocation Breaks Down

Disputes over closing costs account for a disproportionate share of New Hampshire real estate complaints. When a buyer and seller disagree at the eleventh hour about who pays a particular fee, the contract language is the only arbiter. Agents who allowed ambiguous language — or worse, conflicting language between the offer and a later addendum — have created the problem.

> RSA 331-A:26(II)(g): A licensee shall not "make any material misrepresentation or permit or fail to prevent any material misrepresentation to be made in connection with any real estate transaction."

If the dispute cannot be resolved and the deal collapses, both agents face potential Commission scrutiny. The aggrieved party can file a complaint, and the investigator will pull the transaction file looking for exactly the documentation gaps described above. Agents who maintained clear, updated paperwork using proper NHAR forms will fare far better than those relying on text messages and verbal agreements.
