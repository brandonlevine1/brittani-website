# Delaware Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Get Right

Getting agency relationships wrong in Delaware does not just risk a scolding from your broker. It risks license suspension, deal rescission, and civil liability that no errors-and-omissions policy will cheerfully cover. Understanding **Delaware dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** is not optional knowledge — it is the baseline requirement for lawful practice in the state.

Delaware's framework sits in a different place than neighboring states. The rules are codified under Title 24, Chapter 29 of the Delaware Code and enforced by the Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC), which has shown an increasing willingness to audit files and impose penalties for disclosure failures. This post breaks down the specific statutes, required forms, liability consequences, and audit points that agents and brokers need to act on right now.

## How Delaware Defines Dual Agency and Designated Agency

**Delaware law distinguishes between dual agency and designated agency, and the difference matters more than many agents realize.** Under 24 Del. C. Section 2913, dual agency arises when a single licensee — or the brokerage itself — represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. The licensee owes fiduciary duties to both parties simultaneously, which creates an inherent tension around confidentiality and negotiation advice.

Designated agency, sometimes called "appointed agency," is the alternative structure permitted under the same chapter. Here, the qualifying broker assigns one licensee in the firm to represent the buyer and a different licensee to represent the seller. Each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties to their respective client, and confidential information is not supposed to flow between the two agents except as authorized.

The qualifying broker in a designated agency arrangement occupies a supervisory role but does not owe fiduciary duties to either party on the substantive terms of the deal. This is the structure most Delaware brokerages use when two agents under the same roof end up on opposite sides of a transaction. If your brokerage has not explicitly adopted a designated agency policy in its office manual, the default under DREC rules is that dual agency applies — and that triggers its own disclosure obligations.

## Required Forms and Timing of Disclosure

Delaware mandates a specific disclosure form for agency relationships. The form most agents encounter is the **Disclosure of Agency Relationships** form, which DREC requires to be presented at the first substantive contact with a consumer. "First substantive contact" is defined as the point where confidential information could be exchanged or where specific real estate needs and financial qualifications are discussed.

For dual agency specifically, written informed consent must come from both the buyer and the seller before the agent proceeds. The standard practice in Delaware involves using the **Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR) Form — Agency Disclosure and Consent**, which is updated periodically and must match the current version approved by DREC. Agents should verify the form version number against the current year's release before every transaction.

| Disclosure Element | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Written consent required | Both buyer and seller | Both buyer and seller |
| Timing of disclosure | First substantive contact | First substantive contact |
| Confidentiality of client info | Limited — agent cannot advocate for one side | Preserved between designated agents |
| Broker's fiduciary role | Owes duties to both parties | Supervisory only, no fiduciary duty to either on deal terms |
| Permitted negotiation advice | Restricted — cannot advise on price strategy | Full — each agent advises their own client |

If a dual agency situation arises mid-transaction — say, a buyer client walks into your listing's open house and wants to write an offer through you — you cannot simply proceed. You must stop, disclose the dual agency situation in writing, obtain both parties' informed written consent, and document the consent in the file before any offer is presented. This is where many agents in Delaware get tripped up, as we will cover in the common mistakes section below.

## Liability and Consequences for Non-Compliance

**Failing to properly disclose and obtain consent for dual agency or designated agency in Delaware triggers a cascade of consequences.** The [Delaware Real Estate Commission](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/) has disciplinary authority under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 and can impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation, mandate additional education, suspend or revoke a license, or issue a public reprimand.

Beyond administrative penalties, the affected party in the transaction can seek civil remedies. Courts in Delaware have recognized that undisclosed dual agency may constitute a breach of fiduciary duty, which opens the door to contract rescission, disgorgement of commission, and compensatory damages. In rare cases involving willful concealment, punitive damages are also on the table.

The practical risk is equally serious. Title insurance underwriters increasingly flag transactions where agency disclosures are missing from the file, similar to how they scrutinize [title and closing issues in other states](/blog/west-virginia-title-and-closing-procedures-2026). A missing or improperly executed disclosure form can stall a closing, trigger a title exception, or cause a lender to pull funding. None of those outcomes are recoverable with a quick phone call.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make

Even experienced licensees stumble on agency disclosure. Here are five errors that consistently show up in DREC complaints and brokerage audits.

**First, agents delay dual agency disclosure until the offer stage.** The statute requires disclosure at first substantive contact, not at the point of offer submission. If you have been discussing property preferences, financial qualifications, or showing homes to a buyer who later wants to write on your listing, the disclosure clock started well before the offer. Waiting until the purchase agreement is a violation, full stop.

Second, agents use outdated forms. The DAR updates its agency disclosure forms periodically, and using a prior year's version — even if the substantive language seems identical — can be cited as a deficiency. DREC auditors check form version numbers, and a mismatch between the form used and the form current at the date of execution is a findable violation.

Third, agents in designated agency arrangements share confidential client information with each other, sometimes casually in the office. The entire point of designated agency is information segregation. If Agent A tells Agent B that their buyer will go $15,000 above list, the designated agency wall has been breached, and the brokerage may be treated as having operated under undisclosed dual agency. This mirrors challenges seen in [supervision and team structures in other states](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026), where information barriers are critical.

Fourth, agents fail to explain the limitations of dual agency in plain language. Signing the form is not enough. The consent must be "informed," which means the agent has an obligation to explain — verbally and in writing — that the dual agent cannot fully advocate for either party's financial interests. Rushed form-signing in a parking lot before a showing does not meet the standard.

Fifth, agents neglect to retain copies of executed disclosure forms in the transaction file. DREC requires that records be maintained for at least five years. A signed form that cannot be produced during an audit is treated the same as a form that was never signed.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability for agency disclosure failures within their office. Under 24 Del. C. Section 2906, the qualifying broker is responsible for ensuring that all licensees affiliated with the brokerage comply with the agency disclosure requirements. A pattern of disclosure failures by individual agents can result in disciplinary action against the broker's own license.

**Every brokerage in Delaware should be running quarterly file audits that specifically check for the following.** Confirm the presence of a signed Disclosure of Agency Relationships form in every transaction file. Verify the form version matches the current DREC-approved version. Confirm the date on the form predates or matches the date of first substantive contact, not the date of the offer.

For designated agency transactions, brokers need to document the assignment of designated agents in writing. This documentation should be in the file and should identify which licensee was designated to represent the buyer and which was designated for the seller. An oral assignment is not sufficient to demonstrate compliance during an audit.

| Broker Audit Checklist Item | What to Verify | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Agency disclosure form present | Signed copy in file | Missing or unsigned form |
| Form version current | Matches DREC-approved version for transaction date | Outdated form number |
| Disclosure timing | Form date at or before first substantive contact | Form dated after offer submission |
| Designated agent assignment | Written designation memo in file | No documentation of which agent was assigned to which party |
| Information barrier compliance | No evidence of cross-sharing between designated agents | Email threads or notes showing confidential info exchanged |
| Consent language completeness | Both parties signed; limitations of dual agency explained | One signature missing or no acknowledgment of limitations |

Brokers should also maintain an office policy manual that explicitly addresses dual agency and designated agency procedures. DREC expects this manual to exist and to be updated when rules change. The manual should specify whether the brokerage permits dual agency at all — some Delaware firms prohibit it entirely as a risk management decision — and outline the exact steps agents must follow when a designated agency situation arises.

## How Dual Agency Rules Intersect with Buyer Agency Agreements

The post-NAR settlement environment has sharpened the focus on buyer representation agreements across the country. In Delaware, the buyer agency agreement and the dual agency disclosure are separate documents with separate requirements, but they interact. If a buyer has signed a buyer agency agreement with Agent A, and Agent A then wants to act as a dual agent on Agent A's own listing, the buyer agency agreement must be amended or supplemented to reflect the change in relationship.

Agents cannot simply layer a dual agency consent form on top of an existing buyer agency agreement and assume both are enforceable. The buyer agency agreement may contain provisions about exclusive representation that conflict with the limited representation inherent in dual agency. Reviewing these documents together — and ensuring the client understands the shift — is part of the informed consent obligation.
