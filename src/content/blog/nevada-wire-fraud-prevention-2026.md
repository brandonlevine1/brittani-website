## Nevada Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules Have Teeth Now

Wire fraud losses in real estate topped $446 million nationally in 2024, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Nevada's real estate regulators responded aggressively, and understanding the Nevada wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional for any licensee facilitating transactions in the state. The Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED) has made clear that agents who skip required disclosures face real consequences—from license discipline to civil liability when clients lose six figures to spoofed emails.

This is not a theoretical risk. Clark County alone saw dozens of reported wire fraud attempts targeting residential closings in 2025, with several resulting in total loss of buyer down payments. The regulatory framework now in place puts affirmative duties on agents, not just title companies.

## The Statutory Foundation: NRS 645.252 and NRED Compliance Standards

**Nevada Revised Statutes Section 645.252 establishes the baseline duty of disclosure that every licensee must satisfy during a transaction.** While this statute broadly governs material disclosures an agent must make, NRED interpretive guidance issued in late 2025 explicitly included wire fraud risk as a material fact that agents must disclose in writing. The Division's position is that wire fraud vulnerability is a known risk of the transaction process, and failing to warn clients constitutes a breach of the licensee's duty under NRS 645.252(1)(b).

Additionally, NRS 645.633 enumerates the grounds for disciplinary action against licensees. Subsection (1)(h) covers "failure to disclose to a party any material fact that the licensee knows or reasonably should know." NRED has confirmed through advisory opinions that wire fraud risk qualifies under this standard. An agent who closes a deal without providing the required advisory is exposed to discipline under this subsection regardless of whether the client actually suffered a loss.

The [Nevada Real Estate Division](https://red.nv.gov) maintains a compliance resource page that agents should bookmark and review quarterly, as guidance updates arrive without fanfare.

## Required Forms and Documentation

Nevada agents working within Greater Las Vegas Association of REALTORS (GLVAR) or Reno/Sparks Association of REALTORS (RSAR) jurisdictions have access to standardized forms designed to satisfy wire fraud advisory requirements. Form 547 (Wire Fraud Advisory) is the most widely used document and must be signed by all parties who will be wiring funds during the transaction. The form explicitly warns buyers and sellers about email compromise schemes, directs them to verify wiring instructions by phone using a known number, and confirms they understand the risks.

For agents not affiliated with GLVAR or RSAR, brokerage-specific wire fraud advisories are acceptable provided they meet the minimum disclosure requirements outlined in NRED's 2025 compliance bulletin. The advisory must include, at minimum, a description of common wire fraud schemes, specific instructions for independent verification of wiring details, and a clear statement that the buyer or seller should never rely solely on emailed instructions.

**Form retention matters just as much as form delivery.** Under NAC 645.670, all transaction documents—including the wire fraud advisory—must be retained for a minimum of five years. Agents who deliver the form but fail to keep a signed copy in the file have no defense if a complaint is filed.

| Requirement | Agent Responsibility | Title/Escrow Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Written wire fraud advisory | Deliver Form 547 (or equivalent) to all wiring parties | Provide separate wire verification protocol |
| Verbal callback verification | Direct client to call known number before wiring | Confirm wire details via outbound call to sender |
| Document retention | Retain signed advisory for 5 years per NAC 645.670 | Maintain independent verification records |
| Reporting suspicious activity | Notify broker and title immediately | File SAR if applicable; halt disbursement |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Nevada Wire Fraud Compliance

Knowing the rules is different from executing them under the pressure of a fast-moving transaction. These are the errors that show up repeatedly in NRED disciplinary files and broker audit findings.

**First, agents deliver the wire fraud advisory at the wrong time.** The advisory should go out at or before the contract execution stage—not three days before closing when the buyer is already arranging their wire. By that point, a compromised email thread may have already planted spoofed instructions. Agents who wait until the final walkthrough to hand over Form 547 are technically compliant but practically negligent.

Second, agents assume the title company handles everything. Title and escrow companies run their own verification protocols, but that does not relieve the agent of their independent statutory duty under NRS 645.252. The agent's obligation to provide written notice exists separately from anything the title company does. Agents who rely on the title company's warnings as their own disclosure will not survive a NRED complaint.

Third, agents send wire instructions via email without any disclaimer or callback instruction. Even if an agent receives legitimate wiring details from the escrow officer, forwarding those details in an unencrypted email without directing the client to verify by phone creates liability. The moment that email is intercepted and altered, the agent becomes a link in the fraud chain.

Fourth, agents fail to document verbal conversations about wire safety. If you call your buyer and walk them through verification steps, note the date, time, and substance of that conversation in your transaction file. Undocumented verbal warnings carry no weight in a disciplinary hearing.

Fifth, agents use personal email accounts with weak security. NRED's compliance guidance specifically recommends that licensees use email accounts with multi-factor authentication enabled. Agents running transactions from a Gmail account with no 2FA are an easy target—and their broker is liable for the lapse in supervision.

Understanding [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) often comes down to the same root cause: process failures that compound under time pressure. Wire fraud compliance breaks down for the same reason.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers hold supervisory responsibility under NRS 645.630 and NRS 645.635, which means a sloppy agent creates direct liability for the brokerage. NRED can and does fine brokerages for systemic compliance failures—not just individual agents.

**Every brokerage operating in Nevada should conduct quarterly audits of transaction files to confirm wire fraud advisories are present, signed, and dated.** A spot check of ten percent of closed files per quarter is a defensible audit standard. Brokerages that cannot produce signed Form 547s (or their equivalent) in audited files should treat the gap as urgent.

Brokers must also enforce email security policies. This means requiring all agents to use brokerage-provided email addresses with MFA enabled, prohibiting the use of personal accounts for transaction communication, and documenting these requirements in the office policy manual. Under NRS 645.633(1)(l), a broker can face discipline for failure to exercise reasonable supervision, and email security is now firmly within that scope.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Consequence of Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Wire fraud advisory in file | Quarterly file review | NRED fine, possible license suspension |
| Agent email security (MFA enabled) | Monthly system check | Broker liability for agent's unsecured communications |
| Written office policy on wire procedures | Annual review and agent acknowledgment | Supervisory failure under NRS 645.633(1)(l) |
| Training records for wire fraud awareness | Annual minimum | CE compliance gap, audit deficiency |
| Incident response plan documentation | Annual update | Delayed fraud reporting, increased client losses |

Training is not optional either. Nevada continuing education requirements already include risk management topics, but brokers should mandate at least one annual in-house training session specifically addressing wire fraud protocols. Documenting attendance protects the brokerage if an agent later claims they were never informed of their obligations.

## Penalties and Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

The penalty structure is not abstract. NRED can impose fines up to $10,000 per violation under NRS 645.680 and can suspend or revoke a license under NRS 645.633. In cases where a client suffers actual financial loss due to an agent's failure to provide required wire fraud advisories, civil liability exposure is significant.

Nevada courts have recognized that the duties established under NRS 645.252 create a standard of care enforceable in negligence actions. A buyer who loses $80,000 to a wire fraud scheme and can show their agent never delivered Form 547 has a straightforward negligence claim. The agent's E&O policy may cover this—or it may not, depending on whether the carrier treats the omission as an intentional failure to follow known procedures.

Deals can also fall apart entirely. If fraud is detected mid-transaction and funds are frozen or lost, the purchase agreement may become impossible to perform. The buyer may lose both the property and the earnest money, and the agent may face claims from both sides. The [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) should be treated as a non-negotiable step in every transaction file.

The agents who never face complaints on this issue are the ones who build wire fraud compliance into their standard workflow rather than treating it as an afterthought. Form 547 should be part of your initial document package—delivered alongside the agency disclosure and purchase agreement.

Set a system trigger: the moment a contract is ratified, the wire fraud advisory goes out for signature. Confirm receipt in writing. Follow up verbally within 48 hours of anticipated wire transfer to remind the client of callback verification procedures. Document every touchpoint.
