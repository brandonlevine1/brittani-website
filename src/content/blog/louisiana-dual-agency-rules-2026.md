## Understanding Louisiana Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The rules governing dual representation in Louisiana are tighter than many agents realize, and enforcement has only increased in recent years. Louisiana dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 remain grounded in the Louisiana Real Estate License Law (Louisiana Revised Statutes Title 37, Chapter 16) and the Louisiana Broker Disclosure Act (La. R.S. 9:3891 et seq.), but recent LREC enforcement actions signal that the Commission is auditing disclosure compliance more aggressively than in any prior cycle. Agents who treat agency disclosures as a box-checking exercise are the ones most likely to face disciplinary hearings.

This post breaks down the statutory framework, the forms you must use, the penalties for non-compliance, and the specific mistakes that keep showing up in LREC complaint files.

## How Louisiana Defines Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency

**Louisiana law draws a hard line between dual agency and designated agency, and conflating the two is one of the fastest paths to a violation.** Dual agency occurs when a single licensee represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. Designated agency occurs when a single brokerage firm has two different licensees, each appointed to represent one side of the deal.

Under La. R.S. 9:3891, a licensee must disclose in writing the nature of the agency relationship before a client reveals confidential information. The statute does not allow implied consent—written acknowledgment is the only acceptable form.

| Feature | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---------|------------|-------------------|
| Number of agents involved | One agent represents both parties | Two agents from the same brokerage |
| Fiduciary duty | Limited—agent becomes a neutral facilitator | Full fiduciary duty owed by each designated agent to their client |
| Consent requirement | Written consent from both parties (La. R.S. 9:3891) | Written consent plus broker appointment in writing |
| Confidential information | Agent cannot disclose either party's confidential info to the other | Each agent protects their own client's confidential info |
| Broker's role | Supervising broker oversees the dual agent | Supervising broker appoints and creates an information barrier |

The Louisiana Real Estate Commission ([LREC](https://www.lrec.gov)) has published guidance clarifying that a broker who fails to formally designate agents in writing has effectively allowed unauthorized dual agency, which triggers the full disclosure and consent requirements for dual agency itself.

## Required Forms and When to Present Them

Louisiana's disclosure obligations begin at first substantive contact, not at contract execution. The LREC's mandatory agency disclosure forms must be presented at the earliest of these moments: the first face-to-face meeting, the first telephone conversation with substantive content, or the first showing of property.

The primary forms agents must know are:

The Disclosure of Agency Relationships form (sometimes referenced in brokerage materials as the LREC Agency Disclosure) is required in every transaction. For dual agency situations, the Dual Agency Consent Agreement must be signed by both the buyer and the seller before the agent begins acting in a dual capacity. For designated agency, the broker must document the appointment of each designated agent using an internal designation letter or the brokerage's proprietary form that meets LREC standards.

**Timing is everything—presenting a dual agency consent form at the closing table instead of at first substantive contact is a per-se violation, regardless of whether the parties ultimately agree to dual representation.** The LREC has upheld complaints where agents obtained consent "late" even when no party suffered financial harm, because the statute protects the right to informed decision-making at the outset.

Louisiana also requires use of the Property Disclosure Document for residential transactions, which is a separate obligation but one that interacts with agency duties. If you are functioning as a dual agent, you owe neither party advice on how to complete that form—another reason many brokerages prefer designated agency structures.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance: Fines, License Actions, and Civil Liability

The consequences of botching agency disclosure extend across three separate tracks: administrative, civil, and transactional.

On the administrative side, the LREC can impose fines of up to $2,000 per violation under La. R.S. 37:1455. The Commission can also suspend or revoke a license. In practice, first-time violations involving disclosure timing (rather than outright fraud) often result in mandatory education hours and a fine between $500 and $1,500, but repeat offenders face suspension.

Civil liability is the more financially dangerous track. A buyer or seller who was not given proper agency disclosure can bring suit for rescission of the purchase agreement under Louisiana Civil Code Article 1953 (error induced by fraud or misrepresentation). Courts have also awarded damages where a dual agent favored one party's interests over another without proper consent, treating it as a breach of fiduciary duty.

Transactionally, a contract executed under unauthorized dual agency is voidable at the election of the non-consenting party. Title companies and attorneys involved in Louisiana closings increasingly flag undisclosed dual agency as a title defect, meaning the deal can collapse even at the eleventh hour if documentation is missing from the file.

## Common Mistakes Louisiana Agents Make

After reviewing LREC disciplinary records and title rejection patterns, five errors appear with alarming regularity.

**First, agents assume that verbal disclosure at a showing satisfies the written consent requirement.** It does not. La. R.S. 9:3891 is explicit: written disclosure, written consent. An agent who verbally explains dual agency at a kitchen table and then forgets to follow up with a signed form has violated the statute, full stop.

Second, agents at team-model brokerages confuse intra-team transactions with designated agency. If two agents on the same team within a brokerage represent opposite sides, the broker must still formally appoint each as a designated agent. Team membership does not automatically create a designation—only the supervising broker's written appointment does.

Third, agents fail to re-disclose when circumstances change. A transaction that begins with a buyer's agent and a listing agent from different firms becomes a designated or dual agency situation if the buyer fires their agent and asks the listing agent to write the offer. The moment the relationship changes, a new disclosure and consent must be obtained. This is where many [deadline-related compliance failures](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) originate.

Fourth, some agents use outdated forms. The LREC periodically updates its required disclosure language, and using a form from three years ago—even if substantively similar—can trigger a technical violation during an audit.

Fifth, agents in dual agency transactions continue to advise on price. Under dual agency, you cannot tell the buyer what to offer or tell the seller what to accept. Agents who slip into advisory mode (even casually, like "I'd take the deal if I were you") have breached their neutrality obligation and exposed themselves to disciplinary action and civil claims.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry independent liability for agency violations committed under their supervision. La. R.S. 37:1455 makes the supervising broker responsible for ensuring that all licensees affiliated with the firm comply with disclosure requirements. Ignorance of what your agents are doing is not a defense.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check |
|-------------------|-----------|---------------|
| Signed agency disclosure in every transaction file | Per-transaction | Form present, signed before or at first substantive contact, correct form version |
| Dual agency consent (where applicable) | Per-transaction | Both parties signed, dated before agent began dual role |
| Designated agency appointment letters | Per-transaction (when applicable) | Written appointment by broker, information barrier documented |
| Form version currency | Quarterly | Compare forms in use against current LREC-published versions |
| Agent training records | Annually | Proof that agents completed agency-related CE or internal training |

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-police disclosure compliance are building a ticking liability clock.** A quarterly file audit that takes 15 minutes per transaction can prevent a $2,000 fine and the reputational damage of an LREC complaint. Some brokerages are now automating these compliance checks through transaction management platforms—a trend that reduces human error without adding administrative headcount.

The earlier this checkpoint occurs in your pipeline, the less likely you are to discover a gap at closing.

## Designated Agency: The Preferred Structure for Louisiana Brokerages

Most Louisiana brokerages with more than a handful of agents default to designated agency rather than dual agency, and for good reason. Designated agency preserves full fiduciary duties for each client, reduces the brokerage's liability exposure, and avoids the "neutrality trap" that makes dual agents ineffective advocates for either party.

Under designated agency, the supervising broker occupies a unique position. The broker does not represent either party but instead manages the information barrier between the two designated agents. This means the broker cannot share one client's confidential pricing strategy, motivation, or financial position with the other designated agent.

The practical challenge is physical proximity. In a small office where two designated agents sit ten feet apart, maintaining an information barrier requires discipline and documentation. Brokerages that handle this well use written protocols—sometimes called "Chinese wall" memos—that each designated agent signs at the start of the transaction, acknowledging their obligation not to share confidential information.

For brokerages running [weekly pipeline reviews](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system), flagging designated agency transactions as a standing agenda item ensures that information-barrier compliance stays top of mind rather than getting buried under pricing discussions and inspection timelines.

## How Louisiana Compares to Other States

Louisiana's approach shares similarities with states like [Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026), but key differences exist in form requirements and the role of the supervising broker. Unlike some states that have banned dual agency outright, Louisiana permits it—but loads the front end with consent requirements that effectively discourage it.

The Broker Disclosure Act's emphasis on "first substantive contact" as the trigger point is stricter than states that allow disclosure at contract formation. Louisiana agents working with out-of-state referral clients should be especially careful: a phone call from Texas where the client starts discussing their budget and timeline is substantive contact, even if no Louisiana property has been identified yet.
