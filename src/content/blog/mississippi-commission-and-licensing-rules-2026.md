## Understanding Mississippi Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) governs every aspect of how agents earn, split, and document commissions in this state. If you hold an active license in Mississippi, the **Mississippi commission & licensing rules 2026** framework affects your daily practice more than you might realize—from the moment you sign a listing agreement to the day you deposit your split. Ignorance of the current rules is not a defense the MREC accepts, and violations carry real financial consequences.

Mississippi Code Title 73, Chapter 35 serves as the statutory backbone. MREC administrative rules (particularly Rules 3.1 through 3.5) fill in the operational details that statutes leave open. Together, these authorities dictate trust account handling, commission disbursement timelines, advertising standards, and the supervision duties that brokers owe their affiliated agents.

This post breaks down the specific rules you need to follow in 2026, the forms you must use, the penalties for non-compliance, and the audit trail brokers should maintain to protect their firms.

## Statutory Authority and Regulatory Bodies

The MREC operates under the authority of Mississippi Code Annotated Sections 73-35-1 through 73-35-99. Five commissioners—appointed by the Governor—set policy, approve education providers, and adjudicate disciplinary cases. The Commission's office in Jackson processes roughly 7,500 active licensee renewals each cycle.

**Section 73-35-21 establishes who may receive compensation for real estate brokerage services.** Only licensees affiliated with a licensed broker may receive commission payments. Independent contractor status does not exempt agents from this rule; the payment must flow through the broker's office regardless of the IC agreement in place.

The MREC publishes its [rules and regulations on its official website](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/), and agents should bookmark the administrative code section that governs their license type. Rule changes adopted after January 2026 are posted in the Commission's quarterly newsletter before enforcement begins.

## Commission Payment and Written Agreement Requirements

Mississippi is a state that requires written authorization before a commission claim is enforceable. Section 73-35-33 makes this unambiguous: no licensee may bring a legal action to collect compensation without a signed written agreement specifying the terms. Verbal promises—no matter how clear—will not survive a courtroom challenge.

The standard forms most Mississippi brokerages use include MREC Form 500 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) and MREC Form 501 (Buyer Representation Agreement). Both forms contain commission rate fields, duration clauses, and termination procedures that the MREC expects to see completed in full. Leaving blanks in these forms is a common audit finding that triggers remedial education requirements.

Commission splits between cooperating brokers must also be documented. While the MLS may display an offered split, the closing statement and HUD-equivalent documentation must reflect the actual disbursement. Since the 2024 NAR settlement changes filtered into Mississippi practice, buyer-side compensation now demands even more explicit written confirmation before closing funds are disbursed.

| Form Number | Purpose | Required Signature |
|---|---|---|
| MREC Form 500 | Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement | Seller(s) and listing agent |
| MREC Form 501 | Buyer Representation Agreement | Buyer(s) and buyer's agent |
| MREC Form 502 | Agency Disclosure | All parties at first substantive contact |
| MREC Form 503 | Dual Agency Consent | Both buyer and seller when applicable |
| MREC Form 504 | Property Condition Disclosure | Seller(s) |

## Licensing, Renewal, and Continuing Education

Mississippi issues three license types for individuals: salesperson, broker, and broker-associate. Salespersons must complete 60 hours of pre-license education and pass both state and national exam portions. Brokers need 150 total education hours plus at least two years of active salesperson experience.

**Renewal cycles run on a two-year schedule tied to the licensee's birth month.** The MREC requires 16 hours of continuing education per cycle, including a mandatory 4-hour Mississippi Real Estate Commission Legal Updates course. Failure to complete CE before your renewal date results in automatic inactive status—not a grace period, not a warning letter.

Reinstatement from inactive status requires completing all outstanding CE hours, paying a reinstatement fee (currently $75), and submitting a written application. If the license has been inactive for more than two years, the agent may be required to retake the state exam portion. These are not theoretical penalties; the MREC processed 312 involuntary inactivations in the 2024-2025 cycle.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalty structure under MREC Rule 3.3 gives the Commission broad discretion. Fines range from $100 to $1,000 per violation per occurrence. Repeat offenders face license suspension or revocation after a formal hearing.

Trust account violations carry the harshest penalties. If an agent or broker commingles earnest money with operating funds—even temporarily—the MREC treats it as a presumptive violation of Section 73-35-21(1)(n). A single commingling finding can result in a $1,000 fine, mandatory trust account audit at the licensee's expense, and a public disciplinary entry on the MREC's online database.

Deal cancellation is another real risk. If a buyer or seller discovers that proper agency disclosure (Form 502) was not provided at first substantive contact, they may have grounds to void the contract under Mississippi common law principles of informed consent. Courts have upheld rescission claims where the [failure to disclose agency relationships](/blog/west-virginia-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) deprived a party of material information before signing.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. Section 73-35-35 allows any aggrieved party to file a complaint with the MREC, which may then refer the matter to the Mississippi Attorney General's office for prosecution if criminal conduct is suspected. Unauthorized practice—collecting a commission while unlicensed or inactive—is a misdemeanor under Mississippi law.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make

**First, agents fail to date their forms at the time of execution.** MREC auditors look for execution dates on every Form 500, 501, and 502. A missing date creates ambiguity about when agency began, when commission was earned, and whether disclosure was timely. This single omission accounts for roughly 30 percent of administrative citations in recent MREC enforcement reports.

Second, agents advertise commission rates or rebates without broker approval. MREC Rule 3.2(b) requires that all advertising—including social media posts about commission structures—be reviewed and approved by the responsible broker before publication. Agents who post "I'll rebate 1% of my commission" on Instagram without broker sign-off violate this rule.

Third, agents deposit earnest money into personal accounts or hold checks in desk drawers. Under Rule 3.1, all trust funds must be delivered to the responsible broker within one business day of receipt. The broker then has five business days to deposit. Agents who hold checks "until the inspection is done" are in direct violation.

Fourth, agents practice across state lines without proper licensure. Mississippi does not offer automatic reciprocity with any state. Agents licensed only in Alabama, Tennessee, or Louisiana cannot show property in Mississippi or collect commission on Mississippi transactions without obtaining a Mississippi license through the MREC's application process.

Fifth, agents fail to disclose their licensed status in personal transactions. Section 73-35-21(1)(a) requires disclosure when a licensee buys, sells, or leases property for their own account. Omitting this disclosure—even on a FSBO sale of your own home—can trigger a formal complaint.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Mississippi carry personal liability for the actions of their affiliated agents under Section 73-35-7. This is not a theoretical exposure. The MREC holds brokers directly responsible for supervision failures, even when the agent acted without the broker's knowledge.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly (minimum) | MREC Rule 3.1 |
| Form 502 agency disclosure in every file | Per transaction | Section 73-35-17 |
| CE completion status of all agents | Quarterly check | Section 73-35-15 |
| Advertising review and approval | Before publication | MREC Rule 3.2(b) |
| Commission agreement documentation | Per transaction | Section 73-35-33 |

**Brokers should conduct internal file audits at least quarterly.** Every closed transaction file must contain: the executed listing or buyer representation agreement, the agency disclosure form, the purchase agreement, all addenda, closing statements, and commission disbursement records. Missing any of these documents during an MREC audit triggers a deficiency notice.

Trust account reconciliation deserves special attention. MREC Rule 3.1 requires monthly reconciliation of all trust accounts, with records maintained for a minimum of three years. The reconciliation must identify every deposit, disbursement, and the parties associated with each balance. Brokers who delegate this task to unlicensed staff must still personally review and sign each monthly reconciliation.

Agent supervision extends to teams. Mississippi does not have a separate "team leader" license category. The responsible broker supervises all team members identically, regardless of internal team hierarchies. If a team leader is a salesperson—not a broker—they cannot receive commission directly from a title company or sign closing documents on behalf of the brokerage.

## Staying Compliant Under Mississippi Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The regulatory environment in Mississippi rewards diligence and punishes assumptions. Agents who treat forms as afterthoughts, who delay earnest money delivery, or who assume verbal commission agreements will hold up in court are the agents who end up in MREC disciplinary proceedings. The **Mississippi commission & licensing rules 2026** framework is not dramatically different from prior years, but enforcement intensity has increased as the Commission processes a backlog of post-settlement complaints related to buyer compensation transparency.
