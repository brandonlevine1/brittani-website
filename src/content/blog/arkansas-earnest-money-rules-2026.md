## Understanding Arkansas Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

**Arkansas earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real consequences for agents and brokers who treat deposit handling as an afterthought.** The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) has continued to enforce trust account regulations aggressively, and disciplinary actions published in recent commission meeting minutes confirm that sloppy escrow practices remain the most common reason agents lose their licenses in this state. If you hold an active license, you need to know the specific statutes, form numbers, and deadlines that govern every dollar of earnest money passing through your office.

Earnest money disputes account for a disproportionate number of formal complaints filed with AREC each year. The commission's authority to investigate and discipline licensees for trust account violations is codified in [Ark. Code Ann. Section 17-42-311](https://www.arcounties.org/site/assets/files/1036/arkansas-real-estate-license-law.pdf), and the procedural requirements live in AREC Commission Rules 10.1 through 10.14. Knowing the law is not optional—it is the minimum standard for keeping your license intact.

## Deposit Timing and Trust Account Requirements

AREC Commission Rule 10.10 requires that earnest money received by a licensee be deposited into the designated broker trust account within three business days of receipt. The clock starts when the last party executes the contract, not when the agent physically takes possession of the check. This distinction trips up agents who hold checks in desk drawers waiting for signatures.

**The trust account itself must meet specific criteria.** It must be maintained in an FDIC-insured financial institution located in Arkansas, and the account title must clearly indicate that it holds trust funds. Commission Rule 10.2 mandates that the account be in the name of the principal broker or firm, and the word "trust," "escrow," or "client" must appear in the account name.

Commingling personal funds with trust funds is a violation of Rule 10.4. A broker may keep a small maintenance balance—typically up to $1,000—to cover bank fees, but any amount beyond that invites scrutiny. AREC auditors compare bank statements against transaction records, and discrepancies as small as a few hundred dollars have triggered formal investigations.

## Key Forms Arkansas Agents Must Use

The Arkansas REALTORS Association (ARA) publishes standardized forms that are the backbone of residential transactions in this state. For earnest money matters, the most relevant forms include the Purchase Contract (Form 1), the Earnest Money Release Agreement (Form 15), and the Amendment to Contract (Form 3). Each of these documents references earnest money handling in some capacity.

Form 1, the standard residential purchase contract, includes specific blanks for the earnest money amount, the entity that will hold it, and the deadline for delivery. Agents must complete these fields accurately—leaving them blank or writing "TBD" creates an ambiguity that can delay disbursement and expose the broker to liability. Form 15 becomes critical when a transaction fails, because it requires signatures from both buyer and seller before the escrow holder can release funds.

| Form Number | Form Name | Earnest Money Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Form 1 | Purchase Contract | States amount, holder, and delivery deadline |
| Form 3 | Amendment to Contract | Modifies earnest money terms mid-transaction |
| Form 15 | Earnest Money Release Agreement | Authorizes disbursement after contract termination |
| Form 7 | Buyer's Property Disclosure Response | May trigger contingency affecting deposit return |

Agents who attempt to draft custom earnest money language instead of using the approved forms risk violating the unauthorized practice of law under Ark. Code Ann. 16-22-501. The line between filling in blanks on an approved form and drafting legal provisions is narrow, and AREC has disciplined agents for crossing it.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalties for earnest money violations in Arkansas range from a formal reprimand to outright license revocation. AREC's disciplinary authority under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311 allows the commission to impose fines of up to $2,500 per violation, suspend a license for a defined period, or permanently revoke it. Multiple violations or evidence of intentional mishandling accelerate the severity of sanctions.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer who loses earnest money due to an agent's failure to follow contractual or statutory deposit requirements can sue for damages. Arkansas courts have recognized causes of action for breach of fiduciary duty, negligence, and conversion in earnest money disputes, and the prevailing party may recover attorney's fees depending on the contract language.

**Brokers face vicarious liability for the trust account violations of their affiliated agents.** Under AREC rules, the principal broker is ultimately responsible for every transaction involving their trust account. A single agent's failure to deposit on time or a single unauthorized disbursement can jeopardize the broker's license and the operating status of the entire firm. This reality makes [broker-level auditing](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) of escrow compliance non-negotiable.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Arkansas Earnest Money

Mistake number one: holding the earnest money check until the inspection period passes. Some agents believe there is no reason to deposit a check that might need to be returned. AREC disagrees. The three-business-day rule applies regardless of pending contingencies, and the commission has sanctioned agents who withheld deposits "for the buyer's convenience."

Mistake number two: failing to get both signatures on Form 15 before releasing funds. When a deal collapses and emotions run hot, agents sometimes pressure the escrow holder to release funds based on a single party's instruction. This violates Commission Rule 10.12, which prohibits disbursement without written authorization from all parties or a court order. The broker who releases funds without proper authorization owns the resulting liability.

Mistake number three: misidentifying the escrow holder on Form 1. Agents occasionally list themselves or their team name instead of the principal broker or the actual title company. This creates confusion about who controls the funds and can delay closings or trigger disputes about rightful custody.

Mistake number four: treating wire transfers and electronic deposits differently from physical checks. The three-day deposit rule applies to all forms of earnest money delivery. An agent who receives a wire into a personal account and then transfers it to the trust account has committed commingling, even if the transfer happens within hours. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with these early-stage deposit errors.

Mistake number five: neglecting to document the exact date and time of receipt. Without contemporaneous records, an agent cannot prove compliance with the deposit timeline. AREC auditors expect written logs, and relying on memory or email timestamps is insufficient if those records are incomplete.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Principal brokers in Arkansas carry personal liability for trust account compliance, so passive oversight is not a viable strategy.** AREC Commission Rule 10.8 requires that the principal broker maintain complete records of all trust account transactions, including deposit receipts, disbursement records, and monthly reconciliation statements. The commission can audit these records at any time without prior notice.

Brokers should audit five specific items monthly. First, confirm that every earnest money deposit occurred within three business days of contract execution—compare the executed contract date against the bank deposit date. Second, verify that Form 15 is signed by both parties before any post-termination disbursement clears. Third, review the trust account balance to ensure it matches the sum of all open escrow obligations. Fourth, check that no agent has received earnest money in any form other than into the broker's designated trust account. Fifth, ensure the maintenance balance has not been used to cover operating expenses.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Document to Review | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit timing | Per transaction | Bank statement vs. contract date | Disciplinary action, fine |
| Form 15 signatures | Per terminated deal | Signed release agreement | Unauthorized disbursement claim |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Bank statement vs. ledger | Commingling, shortage |
| Agent receipt verification | Per transaction | Agent receipt log | Commingling violation |
| Maintenance balance | Monthly | Bank statement | AREC audit finding |

Brokers who rely on manual spreadsheets to track these obligations often discover gaps too late. The volume of transactions in a mid-size brokerage makes it nearly impossible to catch every missed deadline without automated tracking. Many firms are turning to transaction coordination platforms to flag deposit deadlines and audit compliance in real time—a shift that directly addresses [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in the first place.

## Escrow Disputes and the Interpleader Process

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the earnest money after a failed transaction, and neither party will sign Form 15, the broker faces a legal standoff. Arkansas law permits the escrow holder to file an interpleader action in circuit court, depositing the disputed funds with the court and asking a judge to determine rightful ownership. This process is governed by Ark. R. Civ. P. Rule 22.

**Filing interpleader is not just a right—it is often the safest course of action for brokers caught between warring parties.** Holding disputed funds indefinitely exposes the broker to claims from both sides, and unilaterally releasing them based on one party's demand violates AREC rules. The interpleader action effectively removes the broker from the dispute and shifts the decision to the court.

The practical cost of interpleader—attorney fees, court costs, and time—means brokers have a strong incentive to ensure contracts contain clear earnest money disposition language. Agents who write vague contingency timelines or fail to specify the conditions for forfeiture versus return are setting their brokers up for expensive legal proceedings that benefit no one.
