# Delaware HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Roughly 40 percent of homes sold in New Castle County sit inside a homeowners association or condominium regime, and the share is climbing in Kent and Sussex counties too. If you list or sell property in one of these communities, your working knowledge of **Delaware hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** directly affects whether the deal closes or collapses. Mishandling the resale certificate, blowing a cancellation deadline, or skipping a required document does more than annoy the other side—it exposes you and your brokerage to statutory liability.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, form numbers, deadlines, and enforcement risks Delaware agents face when a property is governed by a common interest community.

## The Statutory Framework: DUCIOA and the Delaware Condominium Act

Delaware's disclosure obligations for HOA and condo properties flow from two primary statutes. The first is the **Delaware Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act (DUCIOA)**, codified in Title 25, Chapter 81 of the Delaware Code. The second is the older **Delaware Unit Property Act (25 Del. C. Chapter 22)**, which still governs condominiums created before DUCIOA's effective date.

DUCIOA applies to all common interest communities created after its adoption—including planned communities, cooperatives, and condominiums. For agents, the most operationally important sections are 25 Del. C. Section 81-409 (resale of units) and 25 Del. C. Section 81-410 (escrow of deposits). These two sections dictate what the seller must hand over, when the buyer can cancel, and how earnest money is protected during the review window.

The [Delaware Real Estate Commission](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/) does not publish a state-specific HOA disclosure form the way some states do. Instead, the resale certificate content requirements are spelled out directly in Section 81-409, and agents must confirm each line item is addressed.

## What the Resale Certificate Must Contain

**Section 81-409 mandates a resale certificate prepared by the association, not the seller or the listing agent.** The seller requests it, the association prepares it, and the buyer receives it before or at the time of contract execution—or the cancellation clock never starts.

The certificate must include, at minimum, the following items: the monthly common expense assessment and any special assessments already approved, the association's current operating budget and most recent audited or reviewed financial statement, the amount of any reserves, the nature and status of any pending litigation involving the association, and any capital expenditures anticipated within the current or next fiscal year. It must also disclose whether the association's insurance covers the unit interior or only common elements.

Agents should also confirm the certificate addresses whether the unit is in compliance with association rules and whether any fees or violations are outstanding. An incomplete certificate can restart or void the buyer's review period, a risk that shows up far too often in Delaware transactions.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Deadlines

The cancellation window is the single most deal-sensitive deadline in an HOA or condo resale. Under Section 81-409, a buyer who receives the resale certificate and governing documents before executing the contract has no separate statutory cancellation right—the idea being that the buyer entered the agreement with full knowledge. However, if the documents are delivered after contract execution, the buyer receives a **15-day right of cancellation** from the date of actual receipt.

| Scenario | Cancellation Period | Deposit Return |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate delivered before contract execution | No statutory cancellation right | Per contract terms |
| Resale certificate delivered after contract execution | 15 calendar days from receipt | Full refund required |
| Resale certificate never delivered | Buyer may cancel anytime before closing | Full refund required |
| Certificate delivered but materially incomplete | Period may not begin; treated as undelivered | Full refund if canceled |

This table is worth memorizing. The most dangerous row is the last one. If an association delivers a certificate that omits required items—say, the pending-litigation disclosure or the reserve balance—a buyer's attorney can argue the cancellation window never started. That argument has teeth in Delaware courts.

If your buyers are purchasing in a community governed by the older Unit Property Act rather than DUCIOA, the cancellation mechanics may differ slightly. Always confirm which statute applies based on the community's date of creation and declaration.

## Delaware-Specific Forms and Document Workflow

Delaware does not mandate a single numbered state form for HOA and condo disclosures the way it does for the Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition (Form SDRC). Instead, the obligations are embedded in the standard contract forms published by the **Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR)**. The DAR Standard Form of Agreement of Sale includes addendum language and checkboxes addressing common interest community status.

Agents should be familiar with the following documents in every HOA or condo transaction:

The **DAR Agreement of Sale** itself contains a common interest community rider section where the parties acknowledge the community type and the delivery (or waiver) of the resale certificate. The **Resale Certificate** is prepared by the association or its management company. The **Governing Documents Package** typically includes the declaration, bylaws, rules and regulations, and current budget. Agents should also secure the **Association's Certificate of Insurance** to confirm coverage type and limits for the lender.

**Failing to check the correct boxes on the DAR Agreement of Sale is one of the fastest ways to create an unenforceable cancellation deadline.** If the contract does not properly reflect the common interest community status, the buyer's attorney will flag it, and you will be revising documents mid-transaction.

## Liability, Fines, and Deal Cancellation: What Agents Risk

Delaware does not impose a standalone statutory fine on licensees who fail to deliver HOA disclosures. The consequences come from three directions, and each one is severe enough on its own.

First, the buyer can cancel the contract and demand a full deposit refund under Section 81-409. There is no cure period. If the resale certificate was not delivered or was materially deficient, the buyer walks, the deposit goes back, and the seller has no claim for breach.

Second, the Delaware Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 for incompetence, negligence, or failure to disclose material facts. A finding can result in license suspension, mandatory continuing education, or revocation. Third, the agent and brokerage face civil liability. If a buyer closes without receiving required disclosures and later discovers undisclosed special assessments or litigation, the listing agent who failed to ensure proper delivery becomes a co-defendant in the malpractice suit.

These risks intensify when agents work across state lines. If you also handle transactions in neighboring states, understanding how [different jurisdictions treat disclosure deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) keeps your compliance posture consistent.

## Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make

Five errors show up repeatedly in audits and post-closing disputes involving HOA and condo transactions.

**Mistake one: assuming the seller will handle the resale certificate without follow-up.** Many sellers have never heard of a resale certificate. If you do not explain the requirement, request it on their behalf, and track delivery, it will not happen. The listing agent owns this workflow operationally, even if the legal obligation rests on the seller.

Mistake two: treating the resale certificate as optional when the buyer "waives" it verbally. A verbal waiver means nothing. Any waiver of the cancellation right must be documented in writing within the contract or an addendum. Without it, the buyer retains full statutory cancellation rights up to closing.

Mistake three: confusing the resale certificate with the seller's property condition disclosure. These are entirely separate documents governed by different statutes. The Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition (SDRC form) addresses physical defects. The resale certificate addresses the association's financial health, rules, and legal status. Both are required; neither substitutes for the other.

Mistake four: failing to confirm which statute governs the community. Older condominiums formed under the Unit Property Act have different procedural requirements than communities formed under DUCIOA. Agents who apply the wrong statute's rules may miscalculate cancellation windows or omit required items.

Mistake five: not verifying the certificate's date. Associations sometimes recycle old certificates or take weeks to produce a new one. A stale certificate—one that predates a recently approved special assessment, for example—can be treated as materially incomplete, reopening the buyer's cancellation window.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Delaware law. If an agent in your office botches an HOA disclosure, the Commission can hold you responsible under 24 Del. C. Section 2913. That means your compliance infrastructure must catch these errors before closing, not after.

| Audit Checkpoint | What the Broker Should Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Contract form accuracy | Common interest community box checked, correct addendum attached | Every HOA/condo transaction |
| Resale certificate on file | Certificate dated, complete, and delivered to buyer with documented receipt | Before cancellation period expires |
| Cancellation period tracking | 15-day window calculated from actual delivery date, not contract date | Every transaction where delivery is post-execution |
| Governing documents package | Declaration, bylaws, budget, and insurance certificate in the file | Before closing |
| Waiver documentation | Written waiver signed by buyer if cancellation right is relinquished | As applicable |

Brokers should also audit whether agents are using current-year DAR forms. Outdated contract templates may reference superseded statute sections or omit updated disclosure triggers. A quarterly form review takes less than an hour and eliminates one of the most common sources of compliance gaps.
