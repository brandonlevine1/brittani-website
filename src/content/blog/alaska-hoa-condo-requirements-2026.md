## Understanding Alaska HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Alaska's disclosure framework for homeowners associations and condominium sales is governed primarily by the Alaska Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act, codified in Alaska Statutes Title 34, Chapter 08 (AS 34.08). If you are handling any transaction involving a common interest community in this state, the **Alaska HOA & condo disclosure rules 2026** should be at the top of your compliance checklist. Unlike some states that leave HOA disclosures as a loose contractual matter, Alaska has a statutory scheme that dictates exactly what must be delivered, when it must be delivered, and what rights the buyer holds if it is not.

The Alaska Real Estate Commission, operating under the Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing, enforces agent conduct standards that intersect directly with these disclosure obligations. Failing to understand the interplay between the statute and your duties as a licensee creates real liability exposure. This post breaks down the specific requirements, the forms involved, the penalties for noncompliance, and the operational mistakes that trip up even experienced agents.

## The Statutory Framework: AS 34.08 and What It Requires

Alaska's Common Interest Ownership Act applies to condominiums, cooperatives, and planned communities created after January 1, 1986. For properties created before that date, older provisions under AS 34.07 (the prior Horizontal Property Regimes Act) may still apply, though most active HOAs have transitioned to the newer framework. Agents must verify which statute governs the specific community, because the disclosure obligations differ.

**Under AS 34.08.510 through AS 34.08.590, the seller of a unit in a common interest community must deliver two categories of documents to the buyer.** The first is the public offering statement, required for initial sales by a developer under AS 34.08.515. The second is the resale certificate, required for resale transactions under AS 34.08.590.

The resale certificate must include the current monthly assessment amount, any special assessments approved or pending, the association's current budget, information about pending litigation involving the HOA, and the status of the seller's account. These are not optional disclosures. They are statutory mandates triggered the moment a unit in a qualifying community goes under contract.

## Resale Certificate Requirements and Form Numbers

Alaska does not prescribe a single state-issued form for the resale certificate itself. The content requirements are embedded in AS 34.08.590, and the HOA or its management company typically generates the document. However, the Alaska Association of Realtors provides standardized transaction forms that agents should use to frame and memorialize the disclosure obligations between the parties.

The relevant AAR forms include the Residential Real Property Transfer Disclosure Statement and the Condition of Property Addendum. Agents working in Anchorage and the Matanuska-Susitna Borough will also encounter the Anchorage Board of Realtors Condominium/HOA Addendum, which specifically addresses the resale certificate delivery timeline and buyer cancellation rights. The Alaska Association of Realtors Purchase and Sale Agreement contains a section on common interest community disclosures that references AS 34.08 directly.

| Document | Source | Who Pays | Statutory Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resale Certificate | HOA or management company | Seller (unless contract states otherwise) | AS 34.08.590 |
| Public Offering Statement | Developer | Developer | AS 34.08.515 |
| HOA Governing Documents (CC&Rs, bylaws) | HOA or management company | Typically included with resale certificate | AS 34.08.590(a) |
| Financial Statements / Budget | HOA | Included in resale certificate | AS 34.08.590(a)(4) |
| Special Assessment Disclosures | HOA | Included in resale certificate | AS 34.08.590(a)(7) |

**The resale certificate must be delivered before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed, or the buyer's cancellation window does not begin to run.** This is the single most misunderstood timing issue in Alaska HOA transactions.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Timing

AS 34.08.590(d) grants the buyer of a resale unit the right to cancel the purchase agreement within 15 days after receiving the resale certificate. This is not a contingency that must be written into the contract. It exists by operation of law. Even if the purchase agreement is silent on the topic, the buyer can still exercise this right.

The cancellation must be delivered in writing, and upon cancellation, all deposits must be returned promptly. The 15-day clock does not start until the buyer actually receives a complete and accurate resale certificate. If the certificate is never delivered or is materially incomplete, the cancellation right extends indefinitely until delivery or closing, whichever occurs first.

For developer sales under AS 34.08.515, the buyer receives a public offering statement instead. The cancellation period for developer sales is also 15 days, but the public offering statement carries significantly more detailed requirements, including projected budgets, descriptions of common elements, and copies of the declaration and bylaws. ## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Noncompliance with Alaska's HOA and condo disclosure rules carries consequences at three levels: the transaction, the licensee, and the brokerage.

At the transaction level, the most immediate risk is buyer cancellation. A buyer who never received a resale certificate can cancel at any point before closing. Post-closing, the buyer may have a cause of action for damages under AS 34.08.595, which provides that a seller who fails to deliver the required documents is liable for actual damages and, in some circumstances, attorney's fees. The Alaska Supreme Court has interpreted the Common Interest Ownership Act's disclosure provisions strictly, siding with buyers who were not given a meaningful opportunity to review HOA documents before committing.

At the licensee level, the Alaska Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action under AS 08.88.071 for failure to disclose material facts or for negligent handling of a transaction. Sanctions range from a letter of advisement to license suspension. The Commission has historically treated disclosure violations as evidence of incompetence or untrustworthiness, both of which are grounds for disciplinary action under 12 AAC 64.130.

At the brokerage level, supervising brokers may face vicarious liability if an agent's failure to deliver disclosures results in buyer damages. Alaska follows standard agency liability principles, meaning the broker is responsible for the acts of their agents performed within the scope of the agency relationship. This is why broker-level compliance audits matter, a topic addressed further below.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Alaska HOA Transactions

**Mistake one: assuming the title company will handle the resale certificate.** In Alaska, the seller and listing agent bear the primary responsibility for obtaining and delivering the resale certificate. Title companies may assist, but they are not statutorily obligated to order or review it. Agents who assume someone else is handling this step often discover the gap too late, typically days before closing.

Mistake two: delivering an outdated resale certificate. AS 34.08.590 does not specify an explicit expiration period for the certificate, but the information must be current as of the date of delivery. An agent who recycles a certificate from a prior failed transaction or relies on one that is several months old risks delivering materially inaccurate financial or assessment data. If the HOA approved a special assessment between the certificate date and closing, the buyer has grounds for a claim.

Mistake three: failing to document delivery and receipt. The 15-day cancellation clock runs from the date the buyer receives the certificate. Without a signed acknowledgment or documented email delivery with confirmation, agents have no way to prove when the clock started. This creates disputes at closing and, in worst cases, post-closing litigation.

Mistake four: conflating the property condition disclosure with the HOA disclosure. The Residential Real Property Transfer Disclosure Statement under AS 34.70 covers the physical condition of the property. The resale certificate under AS 34.08.590 covers the financial and governance status of the HOA. These are separate obligations. One does not satisfy the other. Agents who check the HOA box on the property disclosure form and skip the resale certificate have missed the statutory requirement entirely. Mistake five: not verifying the property's community type. Not every neighborhood with a homeowners association falls under AS 34.08. Some older subdivisions operate under deed restrictions without a formal common interest community structure. Agents must verify whether the community was created under the Common Interest Ownership Act or exists as an informal association, because the disclosure obligations differ substantially.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Alaska have an affirmative duty under AS 08.88.391 to supervise the activities of their licensees. For HOA and condo transactions, this means building compliance checks into the transaction review process. The following table outlines what brokers should verify at each stage.

| Transaction Stage | Broker Audit Item | What to Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake | Community classification | Confirm whether AS 34.08 applies; verify with HOA management |
| Contract execution | Resale certificate delivery | Signed buyer acknowledgment with date; certificate completeness |
| During escrow | Special assessment updates | Any new assessments approved after certificate date |
| Pre-closing review | 15-day cancellation period compliance | Confirm period has expired before scheduling closing |
| File archiving | Document retention | Full resale certificate and acknowledgment in transaction file |

**Brokers who do not audit these items are exposing themselves to liability that could have been prevented with a simple file review.** The Alaska Real Estate Commission's investigation process often starts with a complaint from a buyer, and the first thing the investigator requests is the transaction file. If the resale certificate and delivery acknowledgment are missing, the burden shifts to the broker to explain why.

Brokerages handling volume across multiple Alaska markets, particularly Anchorage, Fairbanks, and Juneau, should consider standardizing their HOA disclosure workflow.
