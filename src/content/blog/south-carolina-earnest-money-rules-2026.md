## Understanding South Carolina Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

South Carolina earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who handle trust funds carelessly. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) enforces these rules through S.C. Code Title 40, Chapter 57, and violations can cost you your license, your reputation, or both. Every dollar of earnest money you touch belongs to someone else until closing or lawful disbursement—treat it that way.

The rules governing escrow accounts, deposit timelines, and dispute resolution are not suggestions buried in a training manual. They are codified obligations that the Commission audits, and the consequences of noncompliance scale from fines to license revocation. This post breaks down exactly what you must do, what your broker must enforce, and where agents routinely stumble.

## Statutory Framework: The Laws That Govern Your Trust Account

**S.C. Code Section 40-57-135 is the backbone of earnest money handling in the state.** It mandates that every broker-in-charge maintain a separate trust or escrow account in a federally insured depository located in South Carolina. Commingling personal funds with trust funds is a per se violation—no intent required.

Section 40-57-137 addresses disputed funds and interpleader rights. When buyer and seller cannot agree on who keeps the earnest money, the broker-in-charge has a defined path: hold the money, notify both parties in writing, and if no resolution arrives within 30 days, file an interpleader action with the court. The statute protects brokers who follow these steps, but it punishes those who release funds unilaterally to either party without authorization.

The SCREC's [Regulation 105-7](https://llr.sc.gov/re/) further details recordkeeping requirements and the 48-hour deposit rule. Every licensee in the state should have read this regulation at least once in the past 12 months—it is the document the Commission uses when it shows up for a trust account audit.

## Key Forms Agents Must Know

South Carolina agents work primarily with forms published by the South Carolina Association of Realtors (SCR). The ones that directly touch earnest money are not optional paperwork—they are your paper trail if anything goes sideways.

| Form Number | Form Name | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| SCR Form 310 | Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate | Primary contract; specifies earnest money amount, holder, and deposit deadline |
| SCR Form 313 | Release of Earnest Money/Trust Money | Authorizes disbursement when both parties sign |
| SCR Form 320 | Addendum to Contract | Modifies earnest money terms post-execution |
| SCR Form 395 | Notice to Buyer/Seller | Used for time-sensitive notifications including default and cure |

**Form 313 is the single most important document when a deal falls apart.** Without both signatures on this form (or a court order), the broker-in-charge has no lawful authority to release disputed funds. Agents who pressure one party to sign it—or worse, fabricate consent—are exposing themselves to Commission discipline and civil liability.

Form 310 includes a blank where the parties designate who holds the earnest money. If your contract names your brokerage, your broker-in-charge owns the compliance burden from that moment forward. If an attorney is named instead, the deposit obligation still falls on the licensee to deliver the funds within the contract timeline.

## Deposit Deadlines and the 48-Hour Rule

The deposit window is not negotiable. Once the broker-in-charge or their designated agent physically receives the earnest money check or wire, they have 48 banking hours to deposit it into the trust account. Weekends and bank holidays do not count. A check received at 3 PM on Friday must be deposited by 3 PM on Tuesday if Monday is a regular banking day.

Electronic transfers have shortened the float time, but they have not changed the compliance clock. If a buyer wires funds to the brokerage trust account, the 48-hour rule still applies to the brokerage's obligation to verify receipt and record the deposit. Agents who [miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in transactions often cite confusion about when the clock starts—it starts at receipt, not at contract ratification.

One wrinkle that trips up newer agents: a post-dated check does not satisfy the deposit requirement. If a buyer hands you a check dated five days in the future, you must still deposit it within 48 hours. If the bank rejects it, you have a contract compliance problem, not an escrow law problem—but both create liability.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Earnest Money in South Carolina

Five errors recur with enough frequency that the SCREC has addressed them in disciplinary bulletins and continuing education requirements.

**First, agents hold checks in their desk drawer.** This violates the deposit rule and creates an uninsured gap where the funds are neither in the buyer's account nor in a trust account. The Commission treats this as a custody failure, and it can trigger a full trust account audit of the brokerage.

Second, agents release funds without Form 313 or a court order. When a deal collapses, the temptation to "just give the money back to the buyer" is strong—especially when the seller does not contest it verbally. Verbal consent is not written authorization. The statute demands a signed release or judicial action, full stop.

Third, agents fail to document which party provided the earnest money when multiple buyers are involved. In joint purchases, the contract should specify each party's contribution. Without this documentation, the disbursement upon cancellation becomes a guessing game that can expose the broker-in-charge to claims from both sides.

Fourth, agents commingle earnest money with commission advances or brokerage operating funds. Even brief commingling—depositing a trust check into the wrong account for "just one day"—constitutes a violation. SCREC does not require intent; the act itself is the offense.

Fifth, agents neglect to provide written receipts. SC License Law requires that the holder of earnest money provide a receipt to the depositor. Failure to issue one does not invalidate the deposit, but it creates an evidentiary hole that makes defense in a dispute far more difficult.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Broker-in-charge responsibility under South Carolina law is not delegable. You can assign tasks to agents, office managers, and transaction coordinators, but Section 40-57-135(B) places the compliance obligation squarely on the BIC. If your agent violates the trust account rules, the Commission comes after your license first.

| Audit Area | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly (minimum) | Bank balance matches ledger balance; no negative sub-account balances |
| Deposit timing | Per transaction | Confirm deposit within 48 hours; match receipt date to deposit date |
| Form 313 execution | Per cancellation | Both signatures present before any disbursement |
| Commingling review | Quarterly | Verify no operating funds deposited into trust; no trust funds in operating |
| Recordkeeping | Ongoing | Three-year retention of all trust account records per Regulation 105-7 |

**Brokers who rely on memory instead of a documented audit process are gambling with their license.** The Commission can initiate a trust account investigation based on a single complaint from a buyer, seller, or opposing agent. When that happens, the BIC must produce every ledger entry, bank statement, and contract excerpt within the timeframe the investigator specifies—usually 10 business days.

If your brokerage handles more than a handful of transactions per month, manual tracking becomes unsustainable. This is precisely where [checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) and where structured systems prevent the kind of oversight that leads to Commission citations. ## Liability, Fines, and License Actions for Noncompliance

The SCREC enforcement toolkit includes informal warnings, formal reprimands, fines up to $10,000 per violation, mandatory continuing education, license suspension, and permanent revocation. Trust account violations almost never receive a warning—they proceed directly to investigation and potential hearing.

Civil liability runs parallel to Commission discipline. A buyer who loses earnest money due to an agent's unauthorized release can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and negligence. Attorney fees in South Carolina are recoverable under certain statutory claims, which means the agent's exposure can exceed the original earnest money amount by a factor of ten.

Deal cancellation is another real-world consequence. When an agent deposits late or fails to deposit at all, the non-breaching party may argue that the contract terms were not met. Courts have allowed buyers to walk away from otherwise binding contracts where the seller's agent failed to deposit timely, reasoning that material breach of an escrow term excuses further performance.

## Disputed Funds: The Interpleader Path

When both parties claim the earnest money and refuse to sign Form 313, the broker-in-charge enters a legally constrained holding pattern. Under Section 40-57-137, the BIC must send written notice to both parties stating that the funds are disputed and that the brokerage will hold them pending resolution.

If 30 days pass with no signed release and no agreement, the BIC may file an interpleader action in the county court of common pleas. This action deposits the funds with the court and removes the brokerage from the dispute. The court then adjudicates ownership of the funds based on the contract terms and equitable principles.

Filing interpleader is not free—there are court costs and typically attorney fees for the brokerage. However, it is almost always cheaper than the alternative: unilateral release followed by a Commission complaint, a civil suit, or both. Brokers should build interpleader costs into their risk planning for every transaction they oversee.
