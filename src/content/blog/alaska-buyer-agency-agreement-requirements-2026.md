The rules around buyer representation in Alaska changed faster than most agents expected, and the consequences for getting them wrong are already showing up in commission disputes and licensing complaints. Understanding Alaska buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 is no longer optional knowledge — it is the baseline for legally representing any buyer in the state. The Alaska Real Estate Commission, operating under the Alaska Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing, has enforcement authority that extends from formal reprimand to license revocation.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, required forms, penalty exposure, and operational mistakes that Alaska agents and brokers should be tracking right now.

## The Legal Foundation: Alaska Statutes and the NAR Settlement

Alaska's agency law sits primarily in AS 08.88.396, which mandates that all real estate licensees disclose their agency relationship in writing before performing any act as an agent. That statute has been on the books for years, but the 2024 NAR settlement injected new operational requirements that Alaska brokerages must now layer on top of existing state law. The result is a two-tier compliance framework: state statute plus MLS participation rules.

**The NAR settlement's most consequential change for Alaska agents is the prohibition on offers of buyer-agent compensation on MLS listings, which makes the buyer agency agreement the sole contractual vehicle for establishing how — and how much — a buyer's agent gets paid.** Before August 2024, many Alaska agents treated buyer agency agreements as a formality or skipped them entirely when the listing already offered co-op compensation. That shortcut is gone.

Alaska's Real Estate Commission has not yet enacted separate administrative regulations specifically codifying the NAR settlement terms. However, the [Alaska Association of Realtors](https://www.alaskarealtors.com) updated its standard forms in late 2024 and again in early 2026 to align with both AS 08.88 requirements and the settlement's buyer-agreement mandates. Agents who are members of a Realtor-affiliated MLS are bound by those participation rules as a condition of their MLS access.

## Required Forms and What Each One Covers

Alaska does not have a single state-mandated buyer agency agreement form issued by the Real Estate Commission itself. Instead, the Commission requires written disclosure under AS 08.88.396, and most agents satisfy that requirement by using forms published by the Alaska Association of Realtors. The two primary forms are AAR Form 200 (Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement) and AAR Form 201 (Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement).

Here is a side-by-side comparison of what each form covers and when to use it:

| Element | AAR Form 200 (Exclusive) | AAR Form 201 (Non-Exclusive) |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer loyalty required | Yes — buyer works with one agent only | No — buyer may engage multiple agents |
| Agent compensation clause | Must specify amount or method | Must specify amount or method |
| Definite expiration date | Required | Required |
| Scope of property types | Defined in agreement | Defined in agreement |
| Termination provisions | Written notice per agreement terms | Either party may terminate with notice |
| MLS settlement compliant | Yes (2026 revision) | Yes (2026 revision) |

Both forms require the agent to fill in compensation terms explicitly. A blank or vague compensation field will not satisfy the NAR settlement requirements and could expose the agent to an MLS compliance complaint. Agents should also be aware of AAR Form 100 (Consent to Dual Agency), which becomes relevant when an agent's brokerage also represents the seller, a common scenario in smaller Alaska markets like Juneau, Fairbanks, and the Kenai Peninsula.

## Timing: When Must the Agreement Be Signed?

**The agreement must be signed before the agent provides any substantive brokerage services to the buyer, including touring properties.** The NAR settlement rules explicitly prohibit agents from showing homes to a buyer without a signed agreement in place. Alaska's AS 08.88.396 similarly requires that agency disclosure happen "before performing any act as an agent of a party."

The practical question agents keep asking is whether a phone conversation or initial consultation counts as "performing an act as an agent." The Alaska Real Estate Commission has not published formal guidance drawing that exact line, but the safe practice — and the one most brokerages have adopted — is to present the buyer agency agreement at the first substantive meeting, whether in person or virtual, and to have it signed before any property-specific activity begins. Waiting until an offer is on the table is too late and creates a window of uncompensated, unprotected exposure for both the agent and the buyer.

## Alaska Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: Penalties for Non-Compliance

Failure to comply with buyer agency agreement requirements in Alaska triggers consequences on multiple fronts. Agents often think of commission loss as the worst-case scenario, but the exposure extends much further.

Under AS 08.88.071, the Alaska Real Estate Commission has authority to impose disciplinary sanctions against any licensee who violates the provisions of AS 08.88, including the written agency disclosure requirement. Sanctions can include formal censure, suspension of license, revocation of license, and civil penalties up to $25,000 per violation. The Commission also has the power to require continuing education as a condition of reinstating a suspended license.

Beyond the Commission's enforcement, an unsigned or defective buyer agency agreement opens the door to civil liability. If a buyer later claims they did not understand the agency relationship or did not agree to the compensation terms, the agent's brokerage may have no contractual basis to collect a fee. Alaska courts have consistently held that real estate agency relationships are governed by written agreements, and oral representations alone are insufficient to establish an enforceable compensation claim.

| Non-Compliance Scenario | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|
| No signed agreement before touring | MLS participation violation, potential fine from MLS |
| Blank compensation field on form | Commission dispute, possible arbitration loss |
| No definite expiration date | Agreement may be deemed unenforceable |
| Failure to disclose dual agency | AS 08.88.396 violation, Commission discipline |
| Forged or backdated signature | Criminal exposure, license revocation |

The MLS itself can also impose fines for participation rule violations. Alaska's MLSs, including the Anchorage/Mat-Su MLS and the Kenai Peninsula MLS, have adopted policies consistent with the NAR settlement, and their compliance committees are actively reviewing complaints.

## Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make With Buyer Agreements

After reviewing complaint data and talking to managing brokers across the state, five recurring errors stand out.

**First, agents present the agreement but fail to fill in the compensation section completely.** The NAR settlement requires that compensation be specific and not open-ended. Writing "per MLS" or "TBD" in the compensation field is a violation. The amount or percentage must be stated clearly, even if it is later renegotiated on a transaction-by-transaction basis.

Second, agents use outdated form versions. AAR updated Forms 200 and 201 in early 2026, and older versions do not include the settlement-required language about compensation negotiability and buyer's right to request the seller cover some or all agent fees. Using a pre-2026 form creates a compliance gap that neither the agent nor the brokerage may notice until a dispute arises.

Third, agents fail to provide the buyer with a signed copy. AS 08.88.396 and general contract law both require that the buyer receive a copy of the signed agreement. Filing the original with the brokerage and assuming the buyer has their own copy is not sufficient. Electronic delivery with a read-receipt or a transaction management system audit trail is the cleanest way to prove delivery.

Fourth, agents neglect to address the expiration date or set an unreasonably long term. An agreement without an expiration date may be voidable. Setting a 12-month term with no termination clause will draw scrutiny from the Commission and is unlikely to survive an arbitration challenge. Most Alaska brokerages recommend terms of 90 to 180 days.

Fifth, agents operating in dual agency situations fail to obtain separate written consent using AAR Form 100 before proceeding. Alaska permits dual agency under AS 08.88.396(c), but only with informed written consent from both parties. Missing this step is one of the most commonly cited violations in Commission disciplinary proceedings.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry supervisory liability under AS 08.88.081, which means a buyer agency violation committed by an agent can result in discipline against the broker's own license. Broker oversight is not optional — it is a statutory obligation.

**Brokers should build a quarterly audit process that checks every active buyer file for a signed buyer agency agreement dated before the first showing or substantive service.** The audit should verify that compensation terms are filled in, the expiration date is present, the correct form version is used, and a signed copy was delivered to the buyer. Any file missing these elements should be flagged immediately and the responsible agent should receive written remediation instructions.

Brokerages with teams face an additional layer of complexity. Team leads who are not themselves managing brokers cannot sign buyer agency agreements on behalf of the brokerage unless the managing broker has granted that authority in writing. This is a frequent audit failure point, particularly in larger Anchorage and Wasilla teams where showing agents rotate among buyers. The [rules around team supervision in other states](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) highlight similar risks.

Broker training records should also document that every agent in the office received instruction on the current buyer agency agreement requirements, including the NAR settlement changes. If the Commission investigates a complaint, one of the first things they request is evidence that the brokerage had a compliance training program in place. A [well-maintained checklist system](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) can make the difference between a defensible file and a costly gap.

## How Compensation Negotiations Actually Work Now

The mechanics of buyer-agent compensation in Alaska have shifted from an MLS-driven model to a negotiation-driven model. Sellers may still offer to pay the buyer's agent, but that offer cannot appear on the MLS. Instead, it must be communicated through other channels — listing remarks, direct broker-to-broker communication, or the purchase agreement itself.

This means the buyer agency agreement is the starting point for every compensation conversation. The agreement establishes what the buyer has agreed to pay their agent. If the seller offers to cover part or all of that amount through the purchase agreement, the buyer's obligation is reduced accordingly. If the seller offers nothing, the buyer is responsible for the amount stated in their agreement.

Agents who are uncomfortable with this conversation — and many still are — should practice the discussion with their broker before sitting down with a buyer. The buyer agency agreement is a negotiation document. The buyer has the right to request changes to the compensation terms, and the agent has the right to decline to work for a fee they consider insufficient. Treating the agreement as a take-it-or-leave-it form undermines trust and misrepresents the buyer's rights under the NAR settlement framework.
