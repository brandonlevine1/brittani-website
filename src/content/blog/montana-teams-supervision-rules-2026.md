## Why Montana Teams & Supervision Rules 2026 Matter Right Now

The phrase "Montana teams & supervision rules 2026" is showing up in more disciplinary actions than agents would like to admit. The Montana Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Department of Labor and Industry, has sharpened its enforcement posture following a wave of consumer complaints about unclear team structures and inadequate broker oversight. If you run a team—or belong to one—the rules that govern your day-to-day advertising, transaction management, and supervisory chain are not optional suggestions.

Montana's real estate licensing framework sits primarily within Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Title 37, Chapter 51, and the Administrative Rules of Montana (ARM) Title 24, Chapter 210. These statutes define who can supervise whom, how teams must present themselves to the public, and what documentation must flow through the designated broker. Failing to track these requirements exposes agents to fines, license action, and deal-level liability that no errors-and-omissions policy wants to cover.

## Team Registration and Naming Requirements

**Every team operating in Montana must use a name that includes the brokerage's registered name or a clearly identifiable reference to it.** ARM 24.210.641 requires that all advertising—including yard signs, social media profiles, and email signatures—display the brokerage name in a manner no less prominent than the team name. The Board does not allow a team name to stand alone without brokerage identification.

Registration of a team name requires written approval from the designated broker, followed by submission to the Board. The Board reviews the proposed name against existing registrations and checks for misleading language—terms like "Realty," "Brokerage," or "Company" in a team name will trigger rejection unless the team is itself a licensed brokerage. This is codified under MCA 37-51-321, which restricts the use of terms that imply independent brokerage status.

Montana does not require a separate form number for team registration. Instead, agents submit the request through the Board's online licensing portal under the brokerage amendment category, referencing the designated broker's license number. The broker must confirm the addition, creating a documented chain of authorization the Board can audit at any time.

## Supervision Structure Under Montana Law

The designated broker holds absolute supervisory authority over every licensee affiliated with the brokerage, including those organized into teams. MCA 37-51-313 makes this unambiguous: the broker is responsible for all real estate activities conducted by affiliated licensees. A team leader who holds only a salesperson license cannot serve as a statutory supervisor—they can delegate tasks, but they cannot absorb the broker's legal obligations.

| Role | Can Supervise Transactions? | Can Sign Listing Agreements? | Advertising Authority |
|------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|-----------------------|
| Designated Broker | Yes — full statutory authority | Yes | Full, subject to Board rules |
| Associate Broker (Team Leader) | Yes — with written broker delegation | Yes — with written delegation | Must include brokerage name |
| Salesperson (Team Leader) | No — coordination only, not supervision | No — broker must sign or delegate to associate broker | Must include brokerage and team name |
| Salesperson (Team Member) | No | No | Must include brokerage and team name |

Associate brokers who serve as team leaders occupy a distinct middle ground. Under ARM 24.210.625, an associate broker may receive written delegation of supervisory duties from the designated broker. That delegation must be documented in writing, maintained at the brokerage, and available for Board inspection. Without it, the associate broker carries no more supervisory authority than a salesperson.

The distinction matters during disputes. When a transaction goes sideways and the Board investigates, they pull the delegation file first. If that document does not exist or is vague about scope, the designated broker cannot argue they transferred responsibility—and the team leader cannot claim authority they never legally held. ## Advertising and Disclosure Obligations for Teams

Montana's advertising rules for teams have caught more licensees off guard than almost any other compliance area. ARM 24.210.641 mandates that all team advertising include the licensed brokerage name in equal or greater prominence compared to the team name. "Equal or greater" is assessed visually—font size, color contrast, and placement all factor into Board evaluations.

**Social media profiles present a particular risk area because platform character limits tempt agents to drop the brokerage name.** The Board has issued guidance letters confirming that Instagram bios, Facebook page names, and even TikTok handles fall under advertising rules. An agent who lists "The Smith Team" without "XYZ Realty" is in violation regardless of platform.

Disclosure obligations extend beyond advertising into transaction documents. Montana's Seller Disclosure Statement and the Agency Disclosure (Form RER-1) must identify the licensee's brokerage. When a team member presents Form RER-1 to a buyer or seller, the brokerage must be clearly listed—not just the team name. The Board considers a form that identifies only the team, without the brokerage, to be materially incomplete.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Montana Teams

The five errors below appear repeatedly in Board disciplinary summaries and audit findings. Each one is avoidable with proper systems.

**First, team leaders operating as unlicensed supervisors.** A salesperson team leader who reviews contracts, approves disclosure forms, and "signs off" on files is performing supervisory acts without legal authority. The Board views this as an unauthorized practice issue under MCA 37-51-321. The fix is straightforward: either the team leader upgrades to an associate broker license, or the designated broker must perform the review function directly.

Second, missing or outdated delegation agreements. Brokers who verbally authorize an associate broker team leader to supervise transactions—without putting it in writing—are violating ARM 24.210.625. Verbal agreements do not satisfy the Board's documentation requirements, and they evaporate the moment a complaint is filed. The delegation must specify which activities are delegated, must be dated, and must be renewed whenever the scope changes.

Third, using team email addresses that obscure the brokerage identity. An email address like info@montanaeliteteam.com violates advertising rules if the brokerage name does not appear in the signature block or the body of every client communication. The Board has disciplined agents for this exact issue.

Fourth, failing to disclose team structure changes mid-transaction. If a buyer's primary team contact changes—say the original agent goes on leave and another team member steps in—the client must be notified and a new agency disclosure may be required. Montana's agency disclosure statute (MCA 37-51-313) ties the relationship to the individual licensee, not the team as a collective.

Fifth, commingling team operating accounts with trust funds. Some teams maintain a shared account for marketing expenses and inadvertently deposit earnest money into it. [Trust account violations](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026) carry some of the most severe penalties in any state, and Montana is no exception—suspension or revocation is on the table.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers who oversee teams carry a heavier compliance burden than those managing only individual agents. The [Board of Realty Regulation](https://boards.bsd.dli.mt.gov/realty-regulation) expects designated brokers to maintain active oversight programs, not passive file reviews conducted after closing.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Documentation Required | Penalty for Non-Compliance |
|------------|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Team advertising (all platforms) | Quarterly minimum | Screenshots with dates, broker sign-off | Fine up to $1,500/violation |
| Delegation agreements | Annual renewal or upon scope change | Signed written delegation in broker file | Supervisory liability reverts to broker |
| Agency disclosure completeness (Form RER-1) | Every transaction | Copy in transaction file with brokerage identified | Transaction voidability, Board complaint |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Bank statements, ledger comparison | License suspension or revocation |
| Team roster accuracy | Upon any personnel change | Updated Board records within 10 business days | Administrative fine, public record notation |

**Brokers must also verify that team members are not holding themselves out as independent entities.** This means reviewing business cards, website bios, social media, and even voicemail greetings. A single overlooked Instagram story that omits the brokerage name can trigger a complaint if a consumer reports confusion about who they are actually working with.

The recordkeeping obligation under ARM 24.210.805 requires brokers to maintain transaction files for seven years. For teams, this includes internal communications about task assignments, showing schedules, and who handled which client interaction. If a Board investigation opens three years post-closing, the broker must produce these records or face an adverse inference.

## Penalties and Liability When Rules Are Broken

Montana's penalty structure escalates quickly. A first advertising violation may draw a warning letter, but repeated offenses or violations involving consumer harm trigger formal disciplinary proceedings. Under MCA 37-51-321, the Board can impose fines up to $1,500 per violation, mandate additional continuing education, issue probationary licenses, or suspend and revoke outright.

Civil liability compounds the regulatory risk. A buyer who discovers their "team leader" had no supervisory authority—and that the broker never reviewed the contract—has grounds for a negligence claim. Montana courts have recognized that consumers rely on the apparent authority of team leaders, and a brokerage that creates that appearance without proper delegation may be liable under agency principles even beyond the licensing statute.

Deal cancellation is also a real consequence. If a court or mediator determines that the agency disclosure was deficient because it listed only the team name, the underlying contract may be voidable at the injured party's election. This is not theoretical—it has occurred in Montana arbitration proceedings. Agents who want to keep deals intact must treat [disclosure and deadline management](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) as non-negotiable workflow steps.
