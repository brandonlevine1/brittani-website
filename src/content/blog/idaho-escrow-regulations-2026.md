## Understanding Idaho Escrow Regulations 2026 and Why They Matter Now

Idaho escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who fail to track deposits, disbursements, and trust account management. The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) has continued tightening enforcement actions in recent years, and the penalties hit where it hurts most—your license and your wallet. If you handle residential transactions in this state, the statutory framework governing escrow is not optional reading.

The primary governing statutes sit within [Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title54/T54CH20/), which establishes the Idaho Real Estate Brokerage Representation Act. Supplemental administrative rules under IDAPA 24.33.01 fill in the procedural details that agents encounter daily—deposit timelines, trust account record-keeping, and dispute resolution protocols.

**Idaho is one of the states where the broker, not the agent, bears ultimate liability for escrow compliance.** That distinction matters because an agent's mistake flows uphill. Brokers who fail to supervise trust account activity face the same license jeopardy as the agent who physically mishandled the deposit.

## Who Can Hold Escrow Funds in Idaho

Not everyone involved in an Idaho transaction has legal authority to hold escrow. Under Idaho Code Section 54-2045, only three categories of parties may receive and hold earnest money deposits: a licensed broker maintaining a trust account, a licensed title and escrow company operating under Idaho Code Title 30, Chapter 22, or an attorney acting as escrow agent.

Individual agents cannot hold earnest money in personal accounts. This sounds obvious, but IREC complaint records show it remains a recurring violation—particularly among newer licensees who accept a buyer's check and hold it in a drawer "until Monday." That three-day window under IDAPA 24.33.01.104 starts ticking when the agent physically receives the funds, not when the broker becomes aware.

The distinction between who physically receives the check and who must deposit it creates a chain-of-custody problem that brokers need to address through written office policy. If your brokerage lacks a documented earnest money handling procedure, you are operating on borrowed time.

## Deposit Timelines and Trust Account Requirements

**The three-business-day deposit rule is the single most frequently violated escrow regulation in Idaho.** IDAPA 24.33.01.104 states that earnest money must reach the broker's trust account within three business days of acceptance of the offer, unless the purchase and sale agreement specifies a different timeline. The RE-21 Purchase and Sale Agreement form (published by Idaho REALTORS) includes a blank line for parties to designate an alternative deposit deadline, but most transactions default to the three-day standard.

Trust accounts themselves must meet specific criteria. Idaho Code 54-2046 requires brokers to maintain trust accounts at an FDIC-insured financial institution located in Idaho. The account must be clearly labeled as a trust account, and broker operating funds cannot be commingled—except for a minimal amount needed to keep the account open, which IREC guidance caps at $1,000.

Record-keeping requirements under IDAPA 24.33.01.105 demand that brokers maintain a ledger showing the date of receipt, source of funds, property address, and disbursement date for every escrow deposit. These records must be retained for a minimum of five years after the transaction closes or terminates.

| Requirement | Idaho Standard | Key Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit deadline | 3 business days after acceptance | IDAPA 24.33.01.104 |
| Account location | FDIC-insured, located in Idaho | Idaho Code 54-2046 |
| Commingling limit | Up to $1,000 for account maintenance | IREC administrative guidance |
| Record retention | 5 years minimum | IDAPA 24.33.01.105 |
| Dispute resolution | Interpleader or mutual release | Idaho Code 54-2048 |

## Disbursement Rules and Earnest Money Disputes

Disbursement triggers a different set of compliance risks. Idaho Code 54-2047 provides that a broker shall disburse trust funds only in accordance with the terms of the purchase and sale agreement or upon written agreement of all parties. Unilateral disbursement by the broker—even when one party appears clearly at fault—exposes the broker to civil liability.

When a transaction falls apart and parties dispute who gets the earnest money, the broker's obligations are spelled out under Idaho Code 54-2048. The broker must provide written notice to both parties requesting they sign a mutual release (typically using the Idaho REALTORS RE-19 Release of Earnest Money form). If neither party agrees within a reasonable time—or if conflicting demands persist—the broker must either continue holding the funds or file an interpleader action in court.

The interpleader option is expensive and time-consuming, which is why many agents work hard to get RE-19 signatures before positions harden. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) and addressing earnest money release language early can prevent weeks of post-termination disputes.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make With Escrow

Five specific errors appear repeatedly in IREC disciplinary actions and complaint files. Each one is preventable with basic procedural awareness.

**First, holding the check too long.** Agents receive earnest money at a Saturday showing and forget to deliver it to their broker until the following Wednesday or Thursday. By then, the three-business-day clock has expired. The fix is simple: deliver the check to your brokerage's designated person or lockbox immediately, even on weekends.

Second, using the wrong escrow holder designation on the RE-21. Section 4 of the RE-21 Purchase and Sale Agreement requires agents to identify who will hold the earnest money. Leaving it blank or writing in a party that lacks legal authority to hold funds—such as the selling agent personally—creates a compliance defect that can void the contract's enforceability.

Third, failing to get written authorization before releasing funds after a contract termination. Agents sometimes assume that because an inspection contingency was invoked, they can tell the title company to return the deposit. Without signed authorization from both parties or a fully executed RE-19, that direction is premature and potentially actionable.

Fourth, depositing earnest money into the wrong trust account when working under a team structure. Idaho's team supervision rules require that all escrow deposits flow through the supervising broker's designated trust account, not a team-specific or satellite account that lacks IREC registration.

Fifth, neglecting to confirm that wire transfers actually arrived. Agents who accept wire transfer as the earnest money delivery method sometimes mark the file as complete without verifying receipt. Given the rise in wire fraud schemes, this verification step is both a compliance issue and a security imperative—something we explored in depth regarding [wire instructions verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process).

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

IREC expects brokers to maintain active oversight of escrow activity, not just sign off on files after closing. IDAPA 24.33.01.106 places affirmative supervisory duties on designated brokers, including regular trust account reconciliation and review of agent-held documentation.

| Audit Area | Frequency Recommended | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Ledger matches bank statement; no unexplained balances |
| Deposit timing compliance | Per transaction | Date on receipt vs. date of deposit |
| RE-19 execution on terminated deals | Per termination | Both signatures present; funds disbursed accordingly |
| Agent delivery logs | Weekly | Chain-of-custody documentation for physical checks |
| Commingling review | Quarterly | Only authorized minimal balance from operating funds |

Brokers should also conduct random file audits at least quarterly. The goal is to verify that the RE-21 escrow holder designation matches the actual party holding funds, that deposit timelines were met, and that terminated transactions have proper RE-19 documentation or interpleader filings.

Failure to supervise carries its own penalty track. Under Idaho Code 54-2059, IREC can impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation against the broker individually. License suspension or revocation is on the table for patterns of neglect, even when the broker did not personally mishandle funds.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance Under Idaho Code

The enforcement framework gives IREC several tools. Idaho Code 54-2059 authorizes civil penalties up to $5,000 for each violation. License suspension or revocation proceedings fall under Idaho Code 54-2060, which requires a hearing before the Commission unless the licensee agrees to a stipulated settlement.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil exposure. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue for breach of fiduciary duty and seek actual damages, statutory damages, and attorney fees under Idaho Code 12-120(3) (commercial transaction fee-shifting). That civil exposure exists independent of any IREC action, meaning an agent could simultaneously lose their license and face a five-figure judgment.

Deal cancellation is another practical consequence. If an earnest money deposit is not properly handled—late deposit, wrong account, unauthorized disbursement—the non-offending party may have grounds to terminate the contract and pursue damages. The RE-21 contains default language making timely deposit a material term, which means breach of the deposit timeline could constitute a material breach of the entire agreement.

## How Idaho Escrow Regulations 2026 Differ From Neighboring States

Idaho's approach to escrow sits between the more permissive frameworks in Montana and Wyoming and the attorney-involvement norms in states further east. The three-day deposit rule is stricter than Wyoming's five-day standard, but Idaho does not require attorney supervision of closings as some eastern states mandate.

| Feature | Idaho | Wyoming | Wisconsin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit deadline | 3 business days | 5 business days | Per offer terms (typically 2 days) |
| Who may hold escrow | Broker, title co., attorney | Broker, title co., attorney | Broker, title co., attorney |
| Interpleader requirement | Yes, if dispute unresolved | Yes, after 15 days | Yes, varies by contract |
| Broker trust account audit | IREC may audit at any time | WREC may audit at any time | DSPS may audit at any time |
| Maximum civil fine per violation | $5,000 | $1,000 | $1,000 |

Idaho's $5,000 per-violation ceiling is notably higher than most neighboring states, reflecting the legislature's intent to make non-compliance genuinely painful. Agents licensed across multiple states—particularly those working Idaho-Washington or Idaho-Montana border markets—should not assume that compliance habits from one state satisfy another.
