## Understanding South Carolina Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

South Carolina's transfer tax structure confuses agents more often than it should. The South Carolina transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by state statute rather than county discretion, which means every agent operating in the state faces a single set of rules — but the details matter. Getting the calculation wrong by even a dollar results in rejected documents at the Register of Deeds (ROD) office.

The fee that South Carolina imposes on real property transfers is technically called the "deed recording fee," not a transfer tax, though the industry uses both terms interchangeably. It is codified under [S.C. Code Ann. Section 12-24-10 et seq.](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t12c024.php), administered by the South Carolina Department of Revenue (SCDOR), and collected at the county level by the Register of Deeds. Every agent who walks into a listing appointment or drafts a net sheet needs to know this number cold.

The rate has not changed for 2026: $1.85 per $500 of the property's fair market value, equivalent to $3.70 per $1,000. There is no additional county or municipal transfer tax in South Carolina, which simplifies the math relative to states with layered local surcharges.

## The Fee Schedule Broken Down

**The calculation itself is straightforward, but the rounding rules trip up even experienced agents.** You divide the sales price (or assessed fair market value, whichever is greater) by $500, round up to the nearest whole number, and multiply by $1.85. On a $350,000 sale, that means 700 increments multiplied by $1.85, yielding a deed recording fee of $1,295.

Recording fees for the deed itself — separate from the transfer tax — are set by the county ROD and based on page count. Most counties charge between $10 and $15 for the first page and $2 to $3 for each additional page. Mortgage recording follows a different schedule.

| Fee Type | Rate | Paid By (Standard) | Governing Authority |
|----------|------|---------------------|---------------------|
| State Deed Recording Fee (Transfer Tax) | $1.85 per $500 of value | Seller | SCDOR / S.C. Code 12-24-10 |
| County Document Recording | $10-$15 first page, $2-$3 each additional | Buyer (deed), Seller (satisfaction) | County ROD |
| Mortgage Recording | $10-$15 first page, $2-$3 each additional | Buyer | County ROD |
| PT-100 Filing | No separate fee | N/A | SCDOR |

These fees appear on the Closing Disclosure (CD) in Sections E and H. Agents who prepare estimated net sheets before a closing attorney gets involved must use the correct per-thousand rate or risk client frustration when the final numbers shift.

## Required Forms and Where They Go

Every transfer of real property in South Carolina requires a completed **SC DOR Form PT-100** — the Affidavit of Taxable or Exempt Transfers of Real Property. This form accompanies the deed when it is presented to the county Register of Deeds. Without it, the deed will not be recorded, period.

The PT-100 requires the grantor's name, grantee's name, property TMS (Tax Map System) number, the consideration paid, and the calculated deed recording fee. If the transfer qualifies for an exemption under S.C. Code Section 12-24-40 (such as transfers between spouses, transfers to trusts, or foreclosure deeds), the specific exemption code must be entered on the form. Leaving the exemption field blank on an otherwise exempt transfer means the full fee gets charged.

Agents do not personally file the PT-100 in most transactions — that responsibility falls to the closing attorney or their paralegal. However, agents who handle FSBO transactions or work with out-of-state attorneys unfamiliar with South Carolina procedures may need to flag the requirement early. Knowing the form number and its purpose keeps you from [missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that stall closings.

## Exemptions That Agents Must Recognize

Not every conveyance triggers the deed recording fee. S.C. Code Section 12-24-40 lists more than a dozen exemptions, and agents should recognize at least the most common ones because they directly affect net sheet accuracy.

Transfers between spouses incident to divorce carry no deed recording fee. Transfers to revocable trusts where the grantor is the sole beneficiary are also exempt. Deeds in lieu of foreclosure, tax sale deeds, and conveyances by governmental entities fall into the exempt category as well. Each exemption has a corresponding code that must appear on Form PT-100.

**A common source of liability arises when agents assume a transaction is exempt without verifying the specific statutory basis.** For instance, a transfer from a parent to a child is not automatically exempt in South Carolina. If the closing attorney relies on the agent's incorrect characterization of the transaction to prepare a PT-100 claiming an exemption, and the SCDOR later audits and assesses the fee, the question of who bears the cost becomes a contractual dispute. Build a habit of deferring exemption determinations to the closing attorney while flagging potential exemptions in your transaction notes.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

Five errors appear with frustrating regularity across South Carolina transactions. Each one either delays closing or creates post-closing liability.

**First, agents miscalculate the deed recording fee on net sheets by using $3.70 per $1,000 without rounding up to the nearest $500 increment.** A property selling for $347,500 requires 695 increments (not 695.0 — you must round up to the next whole increment if there is any remainder), meaning the fee is 695 times $1.85, or $1,285.75. Using flat division without the rounding step understates the fee.

Second, agents forget that the deed recording fee is calculated on fair market value, not necessarily the contract price. If a property sells below market in a related-party transaction, the SCDOR can assess the fee based on the higher appraised value. Agents who prepare closing cost estimates based solely on a below-market contract price set up their clients for a surprise assessment.

Third, listing agents occasionally mark the wrong party as responsible for the deed recording fee on the contract. The South Carolina Association of Realtors (SCR) Form 310 — the standard Residential Contract of Sale — includes a closing costs section where this allocation is specified. Leaving it blank or contradicting the standard practice without the seller's informed consent creates post-ratification disputes.

Fourth, agents working transactions involving new construction sometimes overlook that a separate deed recording fee applies to the lot transfer if it happens in a separate conveyance from the improvement sale. Two deeds means two fees and two PT-100 forms.

Fifth, agents fail to confirm that the TMS number on the PT-100 matches the legal description on the deed. A mismatch causes rejection at the ROD, and the correction cycle can push recording past the contract's required closing date.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under S.C. Code Section 40-57-137 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. While the closing attorney bears primary responsibility for recording, the broker's exposure arises from pre-closing representations made on net sheets, marketing materials, and buyer cost estimates.

|-------------------|----------------|-----------|
| Net sheet fee accuracy | Incorrect estimates trigger SCREC complaints | Every transaction |

**Brokers should require every transaction file to contain a copy of the recorded deed with the ROD stamp or electronic confirmation within five business days of closing.** This simple audit step catches recording failures before they metastasize into title disputes. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) has disciplined brokers who allowed agents to operate without adequate closing file documentation — the defense of "the attorney handled it" does not fully insulate the brokerage.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

The SCDOR can assess unpaid deed recording fees plus penalties and interest against the grantor. Under S.C. Code Section 12-24-90, the penalty for failure to pay is 25 percent of the fee due, plus interest at the rate established under S.C. Code Section 12-54-25. These amounts become a lien against the property until paid.

From the agent's perspective, the risk is reputational and regulatory rather than directly financial. An agent who consistently provides incorrect fee estimates may face a SCREC complaint under the "competency" standard of S.C. Code Section 40-57-145(A)(16), which covers conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness. Repeated errors can lead to mandatory additional education, fines, or license suspension.

Deal cancellation is also a realistic outcome. If a recording is rejected because of a PT-100 error or fee miscalculation, and the closing date passes without a valid extension, the buyer may invoke the contract's time-is-of-the-essence clause. The transaction dies, the agent loses a commission, and the seller re-enters the market having already made relocation plans. ## Practical Workflow for Getting It Right

Start every listing with a net sheet that uses the statutory formula, not a round estimate. Pull the TMS number from the county GIS portal and verify it against the deed on file. Note on your transaction checklist whether the transfer might qualify for an exemption, and flag it for the closing attorney with the specific code reference from Section 12-24-40.

At contract drafting, confirm the fee allocation language on SCR Form 310 reflects the parties' agreement. If the buyer is assuming the deed recording fee as a negotiation concession, spell it out explicitly rather than relying on custom.

Before closing, review the draft Closing Disclosure against your original net sheet. Confirm the deed recording fee line matches your calculation. If it does not, call the closing attorney's office before the CD goes final. Post-closing, confirm recording and retain a copy of the stamped deed in your file.
