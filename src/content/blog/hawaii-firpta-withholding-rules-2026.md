## Understanding Hawaii FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

If you list or sell property in Hawaii involving a non-US person, the Hawaii firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 apply to your transaction whether you represent the buyer or the seller. Missing a single step exposes your client—and potentially you—to five-figure liability. The stakes are higher in Hawaii than most states because the Islands layer a state withholding requirement (HARPTA) on top of the federal FIRPTA obligation, creating a dual-withholding scenario that catches even experienced agents off guard.

Hawaii's foreign buyer and seller population remains outsized relative to other states. Japanese, Canadian, and Korean nationals own significant residential inventory on Oahu, Maui, and the Big Island. That means you will encounter these rules repeatedly, not as a once-in-a-career edge case.

## The Federal Layer: FIRPTA Basics That Still Trip Up Agents

The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (IRC Section 1445) requires the buyer to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when a transferor is a "foreign person." A foreign person includes any non-resident alien individual, foreign corporation, foreign partnership, foreign trust, or foreign estate. The withholding must be remitted to the IRS using Form 8288 and Form 8288-A within 20 days of closing.

**Agents often assume the escrow company "handles it," but FIRPTA liability attaches to the buyer by statute—not to the escrow officer.** If escrow fails to remit, the IRS pursues the buyer first. The buyer's agent who knew or should have known the seller was foreign and failed to flag the issue may face claims of negligence from their own client.

Reduced withholding is available if the property sells for under $300,000 and the buyer intends to use it as a residence (0% withholding), or between $300,001 and $1,000,000 for a buyer-residence purchase (10% withholding). Sellers can also apply for a [withholding certificate](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/firpta-withholding) using Form 8288-B before or at closing to reduce the amount based on expected gain.

## HARPTA: Hawaii's State-Level Withholding Requirement

Hawaii Revised Statutes Section 235-68 imposes the Hawaii Real Property Tax Act (HARPTA) withholding of 7.25% of the gross sales price on dispositions by non-resident sellers. "Non-resident" here means anyone who does not have a Hawaii tax domicile—not just foreign nationals. A seller who lives in California, Texas, or any other state and sells their Maui vacation condo triggers HARPTA.

The critical form is Form N-288, "Hawaii Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Nonresident Persons of Hawaii Real Property Interests." The withholding agent (buyer) must file Form N-288 and remit the tax to the Hawaii Department of Taxation within 20 days after the transfer date. The seller receives Form N-288A as a receipt showing the amount withheld.

**When both FIRPTA and HARPTA apply simultaneously, the combined withholding can reach 22.25% of gross sales price.** On a $2 million Kailua beachfront sale, that means $445,000 held from the seller's proceeds. If you have not discussed this with your foreign seller at listing, expect a furious call the week before closing.

| Requirement | FIRPTA (Federal) | HARPTA (State) |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding Rate | 15% of gross price | 7.25% of gross price |
| Governing Law | IRC Section 1445 | HRS Section 235-68 |
| Filing Form | Form 8288 / 8288-A | Form N-288 / N-288A |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after closing | 20 days after closing |
| Reduced Rate Application | Form 8288-B to IRS | Form N-288C to HI Dept of Tax |
| Liability Falls On | Buyer | Buyer |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Hawaii FIRPTA/HARPTA Transactions

**Mistake 1: Confusing "foreign national" with "non-resident."** FIRPTA applies to foreign persons (non-US citizens and non-resident aliens). HARPTA applies to anyone not domiciled in Hawaii. A US citizen living in Oregon triggers HARPTA but not FIRPTA. A Japanese national living in Tokyo triggers both. Conflating the two leads to either over-withholding or dangerous under-withholding.

**Mistake 2: Relying on verbal seller representations without documentation.** The IRS and Hawaii Department of Taxation do not accept an agent's word that the seller "told me they were a US citizen." You need the written FIRPTA affidavit (Transferor's Certification of Non-Foreign Status) signed under penalty of perjury. Without it, the buyer must withhold regardless of what the seller claims verbally.

**Mistake 3: Missing the 20-day remittance window.** Both FIRPTA and HARPTA require filing and payment within 20 days of the transfer date. Agents who assume escrow will handle it on their timeline—or who close on a Friday before a holiday week—risk blowing the deadline. Late filing triggers penalties and interest that the buyer owes.

**Mistake 4: Failing to coordinate reduced withholding applications early enough.** Form 8288-B (federal) and Form N-288C (state) take time to process. If you wait until two weeks before closing to inform your seller they can apply for a reduction, it is too late. Best practice is to initiate these applications 60 to 90 days before the anticipated close date.

**Mistake 5: Listing agents ignoring the issue because "it's the buyer's problem."** Technically, the buyer is the withholding agent. Practically, a listing agent who fails to flag a seller's non-resident status creates a closing disaster. The deal stalls, the seller is blindsided by a six-figure withholding, and the listing agent faces an ethics complaint or malpractice claim for failing to advise their client.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The direct statutory liability falls on the buyer. Under IRC Section 1445(b) and HRS 235-68, if the required withholding is not collected and remitted, the buyer is personally liable for the full amount plus interest and penalties. The IRS penalty for failure to withhold is typically the amount that should have been withheld, plus interest from the date of closing.

For agents, the exposure is indirect but real. A buyer's agent who knew or should have known the seller was foreign—and failed to advise the buyer to withhold—can be sued by the buyer for negligence. The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HREC) can also take disciplinary action under HRS Chapter 467 if an agent's failure to disclose material facts about withholding requirements constitutes a violation of their fiduciary duty.

Deal cancellations happen when withholding is discovered late. A foreign seller who expected full proceeds may refuse to close once they learn 22.25% will be held. If the listing agent never mentioned this possibility, the [deal breaks down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the final days—destroying timelines, earnest money, and client relationships.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge have an obligation under HRS 467-1.6 to supervise their agents' transactions. When it comes to FIRPTA and HARPTA compliance, that supervision must be proactive rather than reactive. Waiting until an escrow officer calls with a withholding question is too late.

**Every brokerage operating in Hawaii should have a written policy requiring agents to identify seller residency status at listing intake.** This is not optional diligence—it is risk management. The policy should require agents to document residency status in the transaction file, flag any non-resident or foreign seller to the transaction coordinator, and confirm that reduced withholding applications have been discussed with the seller and their tax advisor.

Brokers should audit for three specific items in every file involving a non-resident seller. First, confirm the FIRPTA affidavit or withholding documentation is present. Second, verify that escrow has been instructed in writing to withhold and remit. Third, check that the [timeline of deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) accounts for the 20-day remittance window and any pending withholding certificate applications.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Seller residency status | Signed affidavit or foreign status disclosure in file | No documentation of residency in listing file |
| Escrow withholding instructions | Written confirmation from escrow company | Verbal-only instruction or no instruction |
| Remittance deadline tracking | Calendar entry for 20-day post-close deadline | No system for tracking post-close obligations |
| Reduced withholding application | Copy of Form 8288-B or N-288C if applicable | Application filed less than 30 days before close |
| Tax advisor coordination | Evidence seller was advised to consult CPA/attorney | Agent providing tax advice directly |

Training matters here. The HREC does not test FIRPTA/HARPTA in depth on the licensing exam, which means new agents often encounter these rules for the first time in a live transaction. Brokers who invest in annual compliance training—or who use [checklist-driven systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to catch withholding issues before they become crises—protect both their agents and their E&O policies.

## Exemptions and Reduced Rates Worth Knowing

Not every foreign or non-resident sale triggers the full withholding. Under FIRPTA, the buyer-residence exemption eliminates withholding entirely if the price is $300,000 or less and the buyer signs an affidavit confirming they will use the property as a residence. For prices between $300,001 and $1,000,000 with a buyer-residence use, the rate drops to 10%.

Under HARPTA, the seller can request a reduced withholding or full exemption by filing Form N-288C with the Hawaii Department of Taxation. The Department will issue a determination based on the seller's expected Hawaii tax liability on the gain. If the seller has a loss or minimal gain, the withholding can be reduced to zero.

> "The amount required to be withheld under this section shall not exceed the transferor's maximum tax liability with respect to the disposition." — HRS Section 235-68(c)

Agents should advise sellers at listing that applying for reduced withholding takes time. The IRS has historically taken 90 days or more to process Form 8288-B applications. Hawaii's Form N-288C processing time is shorter but still unpredictable. Building this timeline into the transaction from day one prevents closing delays.

## Integrating Compliance Into Your Transaction Workflow

The most reliable way to prevent FIRPTA/HARPTA errors is to build residency verification into your listing intake process—not as an afterthought during escrow, but as a mandatory field on your initial seller questionnaire. Ask directly: "Are you a US citizen or resident alien?" and "Is Hawaii your state of tax domicile?" Document the answers in writing.

Transaction coordinators who manage Hawaii files need specific training on dual-withholding scenarios.
