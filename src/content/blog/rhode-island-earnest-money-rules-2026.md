## Rhode Island Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026: The Statutory Framework

Rhode Island earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry specific obligations that can trip up even experienced licensees. The state regulates these deposits primarily through Rhode Island General Laws Title 5, Chapter 20.5 — the Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons Act — and the rules promulgated by the Rhode Island Department of Business Regulation (DBR). Failing to follow these rules exposes agents, brokers, and their clients to financial liability and disciplinary action.

The DBR has enforcement authority over all licensed real estate activity in the state. Its Real Estate Section issues guidance, investigates complaints, and adjudicates violations related to escrow handling. Every agent operating in Rhode Island should treat R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-11 as their escrow playbook.

Unlike states where title companies routinely hold deposits from day one, Rhode Island practice often places earnest money directly in the listing broker's escrow account. This means the broker assumes fiduciary responsibility the moment funds are received. That responsibility does not end until closing or a lawful release occurs.

## Deposit Timelines and Account Requirements

**R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-11 mandates that any deposit received by a licensee must be placed in an escrow account within two business days of receiving a fully executed purchase and sales agreement.** The clock starts when both parties have signed — not when the check is physically handed over at the showing or open house. Agents who pocket a check "until the sellers sign" are already skating on thin ice if they hold it beyond that statutory window.

The escrow account must be a separate, identifiable account in a federally insured financial institution located in Rhode Island. Brokers cannot use out-of-state banks for escrow unless specifically authorized. The account must carry the designation "escrow" or "trust" in its title, making it clearly distinguishable from the broker's operating funds.

Salespersons are prohibited from maintaining their own escrow accounts. All deposits must be turned over to the broker or the broker's designated escrow holder immediately upon receipt. This is not a suggestion — it is a licensure requirement.

## Key Forms and Documents Rhode Island Agents Use

The Rhode Island Association of Realtors (RIAR) publishes standardized forms that govern most residential transactions. The most relevant forms for earnest money include:

| Form | Purpose | Key Escrow Provision |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| RIAR Purchase and Sales Agreement | Primary contract for residential sales | Specifies deposit amount, escrow holder, and timeline |
| RIAR Escrow Agreement Addendum | Clarifies escrow terms when non-standard arrangements apply | Defines conditions for release or forfeiture |
| RIAR Release of Deposit Form | Authorizes disbursement of escrowed funds | Requires signatures from both buyer and seller |
| RIAR Contingency Addendum(s) | Inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies | Triggers for deposit return if contingencies fail |

Agents should confirm they are using the most current RIAR forms for 2026. Outdated forms can create ambiguity around escrow terms, leading to disputes that the DBR may investigate. The Purchase and Sales Agreement includes a section where the escrow holder and deposit amount are explicitly identified — leaving this blank or incomplete is a compliance failure.

The Release of Deposit Form deserves special attention. Rhode Island does not allow unilateral release of escrow funds. Both buyer and seller must execute the release, or the escrow holder must retain the funds pending a court order or mutual agreement. Agents who release funds based on a verbal okay are violating the statute.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of escrow violations in Rhode Island range from formal reprimand to license revocation. The DBR can impose fines, require additional education, suspend a license, or permanently revoke it depending on severity and intent. Here is how penalties typically break down:

| Violation Type | Potential Consequence |
|---------------|---------------------|
| Late deposit (beyond 2 business days) | Written warning to suspension |
| Commingling escrow with personal or operating funds | Suspension or revocation |
| Unauthorized release of deposit | Civil liability plus disciplinary action |
| Failure to maintain escrow records | Fine plus mandatory audit |
| Conversion of escrow funds | Criminal prosecution plus revocation |

**Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face direct civil liability.** A buyer whose deposit is mishandled can sue the agent and broker for breach of fiduciary duty. Courts in Rhode Island have awarded damages exceeding the deposit amount when agents acted negligently or in bad faith. The broker's errors and omissions insurance may not cover intentional escrow violations.

Deal cancellation is another real consequence. If a buyer or seller discovers that escrow protocols were not followed, they may have grounds to void the contract entirely. This is particularly dangerous in competitive markets where losing a deal means losing months of work.

## Common Mistakes Rhode Island Agents Make

**The first and most frequent error is treating the two-business-day deposit deadline as optional.** Agents sometimes hold checks over a weekend, wait for "confirmation" from the other side, or simply forget. The statute is unambiguous: two business days from fully executed agreement. No exceptions.

The second mistake is failing to document the escrow holder in the Purchase and Sales Agreement. When the escrow holder field is left blank or contains vague language like "to be determined," disputes become nearly impossible to resolve quickly. The RIAR form provides a specific line for this — fill it in every time, without exception.

Third, agents frequently mishandle disputed deposits. When a transaction falls apart and both sides claim the earnest money, some agents panic and release to whichever party pressures them hardest. Rhode Island law requires the escrow holder to retain the funds until receiving mutual written authorization or a court order. Releasing under pressure is a licensure violation and potential tort.

Fourth, some agents fail to provide written receipts for earnest money. R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-11 requires documentation of all funds received. A verbal acknowledgment does not satisfy this requirement. Every deposit must be receipted in writing with the amount, date, payor, and escrow account identification.

Fifth, newer agents sometimes deposit earnest money into their personal bank account before transferring it to the broker. This constitutes commingling regardless of how quickly the transfer occurs. The funds must go directly to the broker's escrow account — no intermediary stops.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry ultimate responsibility for escrow compliance within their office. The DBR holds the designated broker accountable even when a supervised salesperson commits the violation. This makes internal auditing not just best practice, but a self-preservation mechanism.

**Every broker should conduct monthly reconciliation of their escrow account.** This means comparing the account balance against the sum of all individual escrow deposits that should be held at that point in time. Discrepancies — even small ones — must be investigated immediately. The DBR can request escrow records at any time during a routine audit or complaint investigation.

Brokers should maintain a transaction-level escrow ledger that tracks each deposit's receipt date, deposit date, amount, source, and eventual disposition. Rhode Island regulations require these records to be kept for a minimum of six years after the transaction closes or terminates. Digital record-keeping is acceptable, but brokers should ensure backups exist and that records can be produced quickly if the DBR requests them.

Training is another broker obligation. When onboarding new agents, brokers must verify that each licensee understands the two-business-day rule, the prohibition on personal escrow accounts, and the mutual-release requirement. Documenting this training protects the broker if an agent later violates protocol. The [reasons agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are well-documented, and escrow timelines are consistently among them.

Brokers should also audit their agents' use of current RIAR forms. Outdated forms missing the 2026 escrow provisions create compliance gaps. A quarterly review of form versions in use takes minutes but can prevent months of regulatory headaches. Understanding [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often reveals that escrow mishandling is a root cause.

## Disputed Deposits and Interpleader Actions

When a transaction collapses and both parties claim entitlement to the earnest money, Rhode Island law gives the escrow holder limited options. The holder can release the funds upon receiving written mutual consent, or the holder can file an interpleader action in Rhode Island Superior Court, depositing the funds with the court and asking a judge to determine rightful ownership.

> R.I. Gen. Laws 5-20.5-11 provides that a broker holding disputed funds shall not be liable for maintaining those funds pending resolution, provided the broker does not disburse without proper authorization.

Filing an interpleader protects the broker from liability to either party. However, it involves court costs and attorney fees. Some brokers include language in their escrow agreements that allows recovery of these costs from the disputed funds. Agents should be aware of this provision and disclose it to clients at the time of contract execution.

The RIAR Release of Deposit Form streamlines non-disputed releases. When both parties agree — such as after a failed inspection contingency — executing this form promptly clears the broker's escrow obligation and returns funds to the buyer. Delays in processing agreed-upon releases, while not technically a statutory violation, can generate complaints to the DBR and damage the broker's reputation.
