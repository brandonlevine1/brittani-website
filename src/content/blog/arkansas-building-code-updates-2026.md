## Arkansas Building Code Updates 2026 and Why Agents Cannot Afford to Ignore Them

The Arkansas building code updates 2026 represent the most significant shift in residential construction standards the state has seen in nearly a decade. Effective January 1, 2026, the Arkansas Fire Prevention Commission formally adopted the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) and the 2021 International Building Code (IBC), replacing the 2012 editions that had governed construction since their phased adoption years ago. For licensed agents, these updates change what you need to ask about, disclose, and document on every transaction involving new construction or renovated properties.

This is not just a builder's problem. When a buyer asks whether the home they are purchasing meets current code, or when an appraiser flags non-conforming features, the agent who lacks basic fluency in the updated standards faces both reputational damage and regulatory exposure. The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) has made clear through its disciplinary rulings that ignorance of material property conditions—including code compliance—does not shield licensees from liability under [Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/laws-rules/).

## What Actually Changed in the 2026 Code Adoption

The jump from the 2012 IRC/IBC to the 2021 editions introduces dozens of changes relevant to residential transactions. Three areas stand out for agents handling existing home sales or new construction closings.

**Energy efficiency requirements under the 2021 IRC Chapter 11 are substantially more stringent, requiring higher insulation R-values, improved window U-factors, and mandatory air sealing inspections.** Homes built under the old code that undergo significant renovation may now trigger compliance upgrades. This matters when a seller has added square footage or converted a garage without pulling permits under the new standards.

Structural provisions now include updated wind load maps for Arkansas, particularly affecting properties in the northeast and central portions of the state. Deck attachment requirements have also been revised, with prescriptive ledger board connections now specified in far greater detail than the 2012 edition allowed.

Electrical panel clearances, AFCI circuit requirements expanded to additional rooms, and updated egress window sizing for bedrooms round out the changes most likely to surface during home inspections.

| Category | 2012 Code (Previous) | 2021 Code (Current - 2026) |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| Ceiling Insulation (Zone 3) | R-30 | R-38 |
| Wall Insulation (Zone 3) | R-13 or R-20 | R-20 or R-13+5ci |
| AFCI Protection | Bedrooms only | Kitchens, laundry, bedrooms, living areas |
| Deck Ledger Attachment | General requirement | Prescriptive bolt spacing tables |
| Egress Window Min. Opening | 5.7 sq ft | 5.7 sq ft (no change, but sill height clarified) |

## How This Affects the Property Disclosure Statement

Arkansas requires sellers to complete the Property Disclosure Statement under Ark. Code Ann. 18-44-101 through 18-44-115. The form asks sellers to disclose known defects in structural components, electrical systems, plumbing, and other material conditions. When a property was built or renovated under older codes, sellers are not required to bring the home up to 2026 standards—but they must disclose known non-conforming conditions.

Here is where agents get into trouble. The AREC Property Disclosure Form does not have a specific line item for "building code compliance." Instead, it relies on broader categories like "Are you aware of any structural defects?" and "Are you aware of any additions or improvements made without required permits?" An agent who learns during a listing appointment that the seller added a bathroom without permits has a duty to ensure that information appears on the disclosure.

AREC Rule 10.11 states that licensees shall not conceal material facts or fail to disclose information that might affect the buyer's decision. If you know the 2018 addition lacks a permit and therefore was never inspected against any code edition—let alone the 2026 standards—that fact must surface in the transaction file.

For agents working with buyers on new construction, request a copy of the Certificate of Occupancy and verify it was issued under the 2021 IRC/IBC. Builders who pulled permits before January 1, 2026, may have completed construction under the old code, which is permissible. But if work was completed without final inspection, the property may lack CO documentation entirely, creating a financing obstacle with most lenders.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Building Code Issues

Five errors appear repeatedly in AREC disciplinary files and transaction disputes related to code compliance.

**First, agents assume that older homes are "grandfathered" and therefore immune from any code discussion.** While existing structures are generally not required to meet new codes retroactively, renovations and additions performed after January 2026 must comply with the 2021 IRC/IBC. An agent who tells a buyer "the code doesn't apply because the house was built in 1995" is giving dangerously incomplete advice when the seller added a deck in 2026 without a permit.

Second, agents confuse municipal code enforcement with state code adoption. Arkansas is a home-rule state where municipalities may adopt codes more restrictive than the state minimum. Little Rock, Fayetteville, and Bentonville each have local amendments. An agent working across jurisdictions who assumes uniform code application is making a material mistake that could surface during inspection negotiations.

Third, listing agents fail to ask sellers directly about unpermitted work. The standard listing intake conversation should include a question about any work done without permits. Many sellers do not consider a finished basement or converted attic to be "additions" unless specifically asked. The AREC Seller's Property Disclosure Form (SPDF) question about improvements is only as good as the conversation that accompanies it.

Fourth, buyer's agents dismiss inspection findings related to code violations as "cosmetic" or "not a safety issue." When an inspector notes that GFCI protection is missing in a bathroom or that the electrical panel lacks proper clearance, those are code violations under both the 2012 and 2021 editions. Dismissing them verbally, especially in writing, creates a discoverable record if something goes wrong after closing.

Fifth, agents fail to document conversations about code issues in the transaction file. If a seller verbally acknowledges unpermitted work but the disclosure form says otherwise, the agent who does not follow up in writing has created a gap that AREC investigators will notice during a complaint review. [Keeping deadline-driven documentation habits](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) is not optional when code compliance is at stake.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-305, which requires them to exercise reasonable supervision over all licensees operating under their license. When it comes to building code issues in transactions, brokers should implement three specific audit measures.

| Broker Audit Area | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Disclosure Form Completion | All SPDF fields completed; permits question answered | Every listing file |
| New Construction CO Verification | Certificate of Occupancy on file before closing | Every new construction file |
| Inspection Response Documentation | Code-related findings addressed in amendment or addendum | Random audit quarterly |
| Agent Training Records | CE or internal training on 2026 code adoption | Annual review |

**Brokers should require that every listing file include a signed disclosure form with the permits question answered affirmatively or negatively—never left blank.** A blank field on the SPDF is an invitation to a complaint. AREC disciplinary actions show that the Commission views blank disclosures more harshly than incorrect ones, because a blank field suggests the question was never asked.

For new construction transactions, brokers should mandate that a CO be uploaded to the transaction file before the closing date is confirmed. Lenders typically require this anyway, but cash transactions or portfolio loans may close without one—creating post-closing liability when the buyer discovers the property never passed final inspection.

Quarterly file audits should specifically examine how agents handled inspection findings related to electrical, structural, and energy code issues. Did the agent advise the buyer to seek further evaluation? Was the seller's response documented? Were repair agreements specific enough to confirm code-compliant repairs? These questions protect the brokerage from vicarious liability claims.

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

AREC has authority under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311 to impose sanctions ranging from reprimand to license revocation. Fines of up to $2,500 per violation are authorized. In cases involving knowing concealment of material defects—which includes undisclosed code violations an agent was aware of—the Commission has historically imposed license suspensions of 30 to 180 days.

Beyond regulatory sanctions, agents face civil liability. Arkansas courts have held that real estate licensees owe a duty of honesty and disclosure to all parties in a transaction, not just their client. A buyer who discovers post-closing that the listing agent knew about unpermitted electrical work and failed to ensure disclosure can pursue a negligence claim. The [standard property condition disclosure framework](/blog/wisconsin-property-condition-disclosures-2026) used in other states provides useful comparison for how seriously regulators treat these obligations.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk during the transaction. Buyers who discover code violations during the inspection period may terminate under the inspection contingency in the AREC standard Purchase Agreement. More problematically, if violations surface after the inspection period but before closing—through an appraisal or lender-required inspection—the buyer may lack a contractual exit, creating pressure on both agents to either resolve the issue quickly or face a breach-of-contract dispute.

Title insurance policies typically exclude losses arising from code violations, meaning neither party has a backstop if the issue surfaces post-closing. This makes front-end disclosure and documentation the only reliable risk management tool available to agents.

## Practical Steps for Agents in 2026

Start by familiarizing yourself with which edition of the IRC/IBC governs the properties in your market area. The Arkansas Fire Prevention Commission publishes the adopted codes and state-specific amendments on its website. Knowing whether your municipality has adopted additional amendments beyond the state baseline is equally important.

Add a specific question to your listing intake process: "Has any work been done on this property without a building permit since you purchased it?" Document the answer regardless of what it is. If the seller says yes, ensure the SPDF reflects that information and recommend the seller consult with the local building department about retroactive permitting options.

For buyer's agents, treat inspection findings related to code compliance as material facts that require written follow-up. If your buyer waives an inspection contingency, document their awareness of the decision's implications—including the risk that undiscovered code violations will become their responsibility at closing.
