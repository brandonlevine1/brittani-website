# Hawaii Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026: What Every Agent Must Know

The Hawaii commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have fundamentally changed how agents on every island handle buyer representation and compensation. If you are still operating under pre-settlement assumptions—posting commission splits in the MLS, skipping written buyer agreements, or treating compensation as an afterthought during negotiations—you are exposed to real liability. The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HREC) has made clear that compliance is not optional, and the enforcement posture has tightened considerably since the settlement's practice changes took full effect.

This article breaks down what Hawaii-licensed agents and their brokers must do differently, which forms to use, and what penalties look like when someone gets it wrong.

## How the NAR Settlement Reshaped Hawaii Commission Practices

The national settlement, finalized in 2024, prohibited MLS-based offers of buyer-agent compensation and required written buyer representation agreements before any property tours. Hawaii's local MLS systems—including the HiCentral MLS operated by the Honolulu Board of Realtors—removed all commission-offer fields by August 2024. But the downstream effects on daily practice are still catching agents off guard.

**Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467, which governs real estate licensees, already required written agency disclosures, but the settlement layered new federal-level requirements on top of state law.** HREC has issued guidance confirming that agents who fail to secure proper written agreements before showing property risk violating both NAR's practice mandates and HRS 467-14, which outlines grounds for disciplinary action including license revocation.

The practical result: every compensation arrangement must be negotiated, documented, and signed before a buyer steps foot in a listed property. No exceptions for open houses where you subsequently begin representing an attendee. No exceptions for "quick showings" to gauge interest.

## Written Buyer Agreements: Which Hawaii Forms to Use

The Hawaii Association of Realtors (HAR) updated its standard forms in late 2024 and again in early 2026 to address settlement compliance. The primary forms agents should know include:

| Form Number | Form Name | Required When |
|---|---|---|
| RR-204 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Contract | Before showing any property to an exclusive buyer client |
| RR-205 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Contract | Before showing property to a non-exclusive buyer client |
| RR-301 | Seller's Disclosure of Compensation to Buyer's Broker | When a seller agrees to pay buyer-agent compensation outside the MLS |
| RR-103 | Purchase Contract (revised 2026) | At offer, with compensation terms clearly stated in Section 18 |

Form RR-204 now includes a mandatory field where the buyer-agent's compensation must be stated as a specific dollar amount or percentage. Leaving this field blank renders the agreement non-compliant. The 2026 revision also added language requiring the buyer to acknowledge that compensation is negotiable and that no industry standard exists.

**Agents who rely on older versions of these forms—anything dated before the January 2026 revision—are using documents that do not satisfy current HREC guidance.** Your brokerage should have purged old inventory, but if you are printing from personal templates, verify the revision date in the form footer.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance operate on three levels in Hawaii: MLS penalties, HREC disciplinary action, and civil liability.

At the MLS level, HiCentral MLS can impose fines starting at $500 for first-time violations of the prohibition on advertising buyer-agent compensation within listing data. Repeated violations can result in MLS suspension. These are administrative penalties—they do not require a formal complaint from another party.

HREC operates under [HRS 467-14](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/hrscurrent/Vol10_Ch0436-0474/HRS0467/HRS_0467-0014.htm), which grants authority to suspend, revoke, or refuse to renew a license for conduct including "failure to comply with the requirements of a real estate transaction." An agent who shows property without a signed buyer agreement, then attempts to collect a commission, could face a formal complaint from the buyer, the listing broker, or even their own brokerage. Sanctions under HRS 467-14 include fines up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory education, probation, or outright revocation.

Civil exposure is the third layer. A buyer who signed no compensation agreement—or signed one that lacks the specificity required under the settlement rules—can argue that any commission charged was unauthorized. Brokers have already seen post-closing disputes where buyers demand refund of fees their agent collected without a properly executed RR-204 or RR-205.

**Deal cancellation is also on the table.** If a seller discovers that a buyer agent was operating without a compliant written agreement, the seller's attorney may argue that any commission concession negotiated into the purchase contract was procured improperly, creating grounds to void or renegotiate the deal.

## Common Mistakes Hawaii Agents Are Making Right Now

After tracking compliance issues statewide, several patterns emerge repeatedly. These are not hypotheticals—they are errors HREC investigators and managing brokers are seeing in active files.

The first mistake is using verbal agreements to "hold the place" of a written buyer contract. Agents tell buyers they will sign paperwork later, then show three or four properties before circling back. Under the settlement rules, this is non-compliant from the moment the first showing begins. Hawaii law does not recognize verbal agreements as satisfying the written-agreement requirement.

The second mistake involves open house conversions. An agent hosts an open house, meets an unrepresented buyer, and begins a showing tour of other properties the same day without executing RR-204 or RR-205. The casual nature of open houses makes this feel low-risk, but it is the exact scenario the settlement targeted.

Third, agents are failing to update compensation terms when deal terms change. If a buyer agreement specifies compensation at 2.5% and the seller subsequently offers 2% through a separate agreement, the agent must address the gap with their buyer in writing before closing. Silently absorbing the difference or billing the buyer without written authorization violates the disclosure requirements.

Fourth, some agents are still embedding compensation language in MLS "agent remarks" or "showing instructions" fields—attempting to communicate commission offers without using the now-prohibited compensation field. HiCentral MLS treats this as a circumvention violation and has flagged listings for removal.

Fifth, teams are assigning showing agents to cover tours without verifying that the buyer's written agreement names the correct licensee or team. If the agreement is with Agent A but Agent B conducts the showings, the compensation authorization may not extend to the work being performed. This is a problem that [better systems for weekly pipeline reviews](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) can catch before it becomes an audit finding.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in Hawaii carry direct supervisory liability under HRS 467-1.6. If an agent in your office violates commission structure rules, HREC can and does hold the principal broker responsible for inadequate supervision. This is not theoretical—HREC's enforcement history includes sanctions against brokers whose agents operated without proper documentation.

Brokers should audit five specific elements in every active transaction file:

| Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agreement execution date | Must predate first showing | Agreement signed same day or after first showing |
| Compensation specificity | Dollar amount or percentage stated, not "TBD" | Blank fields or vague language like "customary commission" |
| Form version | RR-204/RR-205 dated January 2026 or later | Older revision dates in form footer |
| Compensation source documentation | Written seller concession or buyer payment acknowledgment | No paper trail for who is paying agent |
| Team assignment alignment | Name on agreement matches showing agent or includes team clause | Showing agent not named anywhere in agreement |

**Quarterly file audits are the minimum defensible standard for a Hawaii brokerage in 2026.** Monthly audits are better. If your office processes more than 20 transactions per quarter, consider a systematic approach—either a dedicated compliance officer or automated file review that flags missing documents before closing.

## Negotiating Compensation Outside the MLS

The settlement did not ban seller-paid buyer-agent compensation. It banned advertising that compensation in the MLS. This distinction matters enormously in Hawaii's market, where sellers frequently offer buyer-agent compensation to attract financed buyers who cannot afford to pay their agent separately.

Sellers who want to offer compensation now do so through form RR-301, a standalone disclosure document. This form is transmitted directly to buyer agents—typically through the listing broker—rather than broadcast through MLS data fields. The offer can also be communicated through listing agent websites, property flyers, or direct broker-to-broker communication.

Buyer agents must still disclose to their clients any compensation they will receive from the seller. The revised RR-103 Purchase Contract (2026 edition) includes Section 18, which requires explicit identification of compensation source and amount. Failing to complete this section is a material omission.

For agents worried about how to handle these conversations with clients, the key is transparency at the outset. Your buyer agreement (RR-204 or RR-205) sets the compensation expectation. If the seller's offer via RR-301 meets or exceeds that amount, the buyer pays nothing additional. If it falls short, the buyer covers the gap—and that must be disclosed before the buyer submits an offer.

## Staying Compliant as Hawaii's Rules Continue Evolving

HREC has signaled that additional rulemaking may follow in late 2026 or early 2027, particularly around team structures and compensation splitting disclosures. The Real Estate Branch of the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA) is the regulatory body to watch for updates. Agents should monitor DCCA bulletins and HAR's legal hotline advisories.

The firms that avoid enforcement trouble will be those who treat compliance as a systems problem, not a willpower problem.
