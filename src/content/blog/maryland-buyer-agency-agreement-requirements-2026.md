## Understanding Maryland Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The Maryland buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 represent a regulatory framework that every licensed agent in the state must execute correctly or face real financial and legal consequences. Following the NAR settlement that took effect in August 2024, Maryland's existing statutory disclosure rules under the Maryland Real Property Article Title 17, Subtitle 5 now intersect with new MLS participation mandates that demand written buyer agreements before an agent provides substantive services. This is not optional guidance from your brokerage—it is the law.

Maryland was already ahead of many states on agency disclosure. The Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form has been a requirement since 1997, and the state's statutory framework under Section 17-530 explicitly requires that the nature and scope of agency be disclosed in writing. What changed in 2025 and carries into 2026 is the enforcement mechanism: Bright MLS now conditions showing access on documentation that a written buyer representation agreement exists, and the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) has increased its audit activity accordingly.

**Agents who treated the 2024 settlement as a distant national event are now receiving MREC inquiry letters.** The Commission's 2025 annual report noted a 34 percent increase in complaints related to agency disclosure and compensation transparency, and that trend shows no sign of slowing.

## The Statutory Foundation: Maryland Real Property Article Title 17

Maryland's agency law lives in Title 17, Subtitle 5 of the Real Property Article. Section 17-530 requires licensees to provide written disclosure of their agency relationship before providing real estate brokerage services. Section 17-532 outlines the duties owed by a buyer's agent, including confidentiality, obedience, loyalty, disclosure, accounting, and reasonable care.

The statute does not prescribe a specific form, but it does mandate that the agreement include the scope of services, the duration, and the compensation arrangement. MREC has interpreted this to require a definite termination date and a clear description of how the agent will be paid, including whether the agent may receive compensation from the listing broker, the buyer, or both.

For agents working under a team structure, the agreement must identify the supervising broker and the brokerage entity. Individual team members cannot enter into buyer agency agreements in their own name unless independently licensed as brokers. This is a frequent audit finding that [team supervision rules in other states](/blog/washington-teams-supervision-rules-2026) also flag, and Maryland is no different.

## Required Forms and Where to Find Them

Maryland REALTORS (the state association) publishes the standard forms most brokerages adopt. The primary documents for buyer representation in 2026 are:

| Form Number | Title | Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| Form 1101 | Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Standard exclusive engagement with a single buyer's agent |
| Form 1101-NE | Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement | Buyer retains right to work with other agents simultaneously |
| Form 1102 | Limited-Service Buyer Representation Agreement | Reduced-scope engagements with enumerated services |
| Form 1001 | Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent | Mandatory first-contact disclosure of agency options |

Form 1001 is not the buyer agency agreement itself—it is the preliminary disclosure form that must be presented at first substantive contact. Agents who confuse these two documents risk a compliance gap: presenting Form 1001 without following up with Form 1101 (or an equivalent written agreement) before showings begin leaves the agent without a valid representation contract.

**The distinction matters because Form 1001 discloses possible relationships, while Form 1101 creates the actual contractual obligation.** Both must be completed, in sequence, and both must be retained in the transaction file for a minimum of five years per COMAR 09.11.02.03.

## Compensation Transparency Rules Post-Settlement

The NAR settlement's compensation transparency requirements layer on top of Maryland's existing law. Under the revised Bright MLS rules effective August 2024, offers of buyer-agent compensation may no longer appear in MLS listings. This means the buyer agency agreement must clearly state how the buyer's agent will be compensated and the maximum amount or percentage the buyer may owe.

Maryland permits several compensation structures within the written agreement. The agent may be paid by the seller through a separate agreement, by the buyer directly, through a concession negotiated in the purchase contract, or through a combination. What Maryland does not permit is ambiguity: MREC's position, articulated in its [2024 guidance bulletin](https://www.dllr.state.md.us/license/mrec/), states that the agreement must specify a maximum compensation amount or rate rather than an open-ended formula.

Agents accustomed to writing "compensation per MLS" in their buyer agreements must stop immediately. That language references a system that no longer publishes the data point. Replace it with a specific dollar amount or percentage and a clear statement of what happens if the seller offers less than that amount. This parallels issues agents face in [Washington's commission structure changes](/blog/washington-commission-structure-changes-2026), where similar post-settlement adjustments have caused compliance missteps.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance with Maryland's buyer agency agreement requirements triggers consequences across three channels: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

On the regulatory side, MREC can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under Real Property Article Section 17-322. Repeated violations can result in license suspension or revocation. The Commission's disciplinary actions are public record and appear in licensee lookup searches, which means a single violation can damage an agent's reputation with future clients who search their name.

Civil liability arises when a buyer or seller challenges the validity of the agency relationship. Without a properly executed written agreement, an agent may be unable to enforce a commission claim in court. Maryland courts have consistently held that oral buyer agency agreements are unenforceable for purposes of commission recovery, citing the Statute of Frauds as applied to brokerage agreements exceeding a one-year performance window.

**At the transactional level, a missing or defective buyer agency agreement can delay or kill a deal.** Title companies in Maryland increasingly require evidence of a valid buyer representation agreement before disbursing commission at settlement. If the agreement is missing, the settlement agent may hold the buyer-agent commission in escrow pending resolution, forcing the agent to litigate or negotiate after closing.

## Five Common Mistakes Maryland Agents Make

The first and most frequent error is presenting Form 1001 at first contact but never following up with a signed Form 1101 before beginning substantive services. The agent believes they have satisfied the disclosure requirement, but they have not created a binding representation agreement. MREC treats this as a violation of Section 17-530.

The second mistake involves duration. Agents leave the termination date blank or insert language like "until buyer purchases a home." Maryland law requires a definite expiration date. An agreement without one is arguably void for indefiniteness, which means the agent has no enforceable right to compensation if the buyer walks away or purchases through another agent.

Third, agents fail to initial or update the compensation section when circumstances change mid-engagement. If the buyer and agent agree to modify the commission structure—say, from 2.5 percent to a flat fee—that modification must be documented in writing and signed by both parties. Verbal modifications are unenforceable.

Fourth, dual agency consent is handled improperly. When an agent's brokerage also lists a property the buyer wants to purchase, the agent must obtain written consent to dual agency on a separate disclosure before writing the offer. Form 1101 contains a pre-consent provision, but MREC has stated in advisory opinions that transaction-specific consent is still required at the time dual agency actually arises.

Fifth, agents operating across state lines—common in the D.C. metro market—apply Virginia or D.C. practices to Maryland transactions. Maryland's requirements differ materially from both jurisdictions. An agent licensed in Maryland who shows Maryland property to a buyer must use Maryland forms and follow Maryland timelines, regardless of where the buyer resides or where the agent's primary office is located.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Maryland Real Property Article Section 17-322.1. If an agent under their supervision fails to obtain a proper buyer agency agreement, the broker faces the same disciplinary exposure as the agent. This is not theoretical—MREC has sanctioned brokers for systemic compliance failures in their offices.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Signed Form 1001 in file | Every transaction | Missing or unsigned disclosure form |
| Signed Form 1101/1101-NE/1102 in file | Every buyer transaction | Agreement dated after first showing |
| Definite termination date present | Every agreement | Blank field or indefinite language |
| Compensation amount specified | Every agreement | "Per MLS" or blank compensation field |
| Dual agency consent (if applicable) | When brokerage lists the property | Generic pre-consent without transaction-specific acknowledgment |

Brokers should run these audits monthly at minimum. Quarterly is too infrequent given MREC's current enforcement posture. A practical approach is to require agents to upload their signed buyer agreements to the transaction management system before any showing confirmation is issued. This creates a timestamp trail that proves compliance if a complaint surfaces later.

**Firms that automate this checkpoint eliminate the most common failure point in their compliance chain.** Manual reminders and checklist emails get ignored when agents are busy, which is exactly when mistakes happen. ## Duration, Termination, and Renewal Pitfalls

Maryland does not impose a statutory maximum on buyer agency agreement duration, but MREC has stated through informal guidance that agreements should be "reasonable in duration." Post-settlement best practices from NAR recommend terms that align with the expected transaction timeline, typically 90 to 180 days for residential purchases.

Renewal clauses deserve scrutiny. An automatic renewal provision that extends the agreement indefinitely unless the buyer sends written notice is likely enforceable under Maryland contract law but will draw MREC attention if a consumer complains. The better practice is to include a defined end date with an optional written extension addendum that requires affirmative signatures from both parties.

Early termination rights must be spelled out. Form 1101 includes a cancellation provision, but many agents modify the form to restrict the buyer's ability to terminate. While contractually permissible, overly restrictive termination clauses expose the agent to Consumer Protection Act claims if the buyer feels trapped. MREC's 2025 bulletin specifically warned against "punitive termination fees" that exceed the agent's actual costs incurred during the representation period.
