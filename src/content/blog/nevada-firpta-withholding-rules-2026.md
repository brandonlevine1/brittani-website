# Nevada FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers: What Agents Must Know in 2026

Understanding the **Nevada firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026** is not optional for agents working in a market that attracts heavy international investment. Las Vegas alone saw over $2.3 billion in foreign-buyer transactions in the 2024-2025 cycle according to NAR's International Transactions report, and Clark County remains one of the top five metro areas nationwide for non-resident alien property ownership. When those buyers eventually sell, agents on both sides of the transaction face federal withholding obligations that carry serious financial penalties for non-compliance.

FIRPTA—the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act—requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold a percentage of the gross sales price and remit it to the IRS. Nevada has no state income tax, which simplifies one layer of this equation, but agents who assume that eliminates all withholding issues are making a dangerous mistake.

## How FIRPTA Withholding Works in Nevada Transactions

The federal withholding rate under IRC Section 1445 remains at 15% of the gross sales price for most residential transactions in 2026. This is not a tax on profit—it applies to the full sale price regardless of the seller's actual gain or loss. The buyer, not the seller, bears legal responsibility for remitting the withheld amount to the IRS using Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons).

**Nevada's absence of a state income tax means there is no parallel state withholding form to file.** This distinguishes Nevada from states like California (which requires a separate 3.33% state withholding via Form 593) or Hawaii (which imposes its own HARPTA withholding at 7.25%). Agents moving to Nevada from those states sometimes assume a simpler compliance environment, but the federal FIRPTA obligations remain unchanged and carry the same penalties.

The 20-day filing deadline after closing is where transactions most commonly break down. Forms 8288 and 8288-A must reach the IRS within 20 days of the transfer date. Most Nevada title companies and escrow officers handle this mechanically as part of their closing workflow, but the legal liability still rests with the transferee (buyer).

## Rate Tiers and Exemptions Every Nevada Agent Should Memorize

Not every sale triggers the full 15% withholding. The rate structure depends on both the sales price and the buyer's intended use of the property.

| Sales Price | Buyer Intends Personal Residence Use | Withholding Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Yes (with affidavit) | 0% |
| $300,001 – $1,000,000 | Yes (with affidavit) | 10% |
| Over $1,000,000 | Yes or No | 15% |
| Any amount | No (investment/rental) | 15% |

The personal residence exemption requires the buyer to sign an affidavit at closing stating they have definite plans to reside in the property for at least 50% of the number of days it is used during each of the first two 12-month periods following the transfer. This is not a casual checkbox—it is a sworn statement with legal consequences if false.

Foreign sellers can also apply for a withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B, which requests a reduced or eliminated withholding based on the seller's expected actual tax liability on the gain. The IRS typically processes these within 90 days, and Nevada escrow companies will often hold the withheld funds in escrow pending the determination rather than delaying closing entirely.

## Identifying Foreign Sellers: The Agent's Affirmative Duty

**Under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-2(b), the buyer's agent has a duty to inform the buyer of FIRPTA obligations if the agent has actual knowledge that the seller is a foreign person.** This is not a passive role. If the listing agent knows the seller is a non-resident alien or foreign corporation and discloses that fact during the transaction, the buyer's agent cannot claim ignorance.

In Nevada, the standard Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA) used through the Greater Las Vegas Association of Realtors and the Reno/Sparks Association of Realtors includes a FIRPTA advisory clause. The clause puts both parties on notice that withholding may apply, but it does not relieve the agents of their knowledge-based duties under federal regulations.

The seller can provide a Non-Foreign Affidavit (also called a FIRPTA Affidavit or Certificate of Non-Foreign Status) under IRC 1445(b)(2) to certify they are not a foreign person. This affidavit must include the seller's name, U.S. taxpayer identification number, and home address. If the seller provides this affidavit and the buyer has no reason to believe it is false, the buyer is generally relieved of withholding obligations.

Here is where Nevada agents get tripped up: an LLC or trust that owns the property may itself be a domestic entity, but if its members or beneficiaries are foreign persons, the entity-level affidavit may still be valid for FIRPTA purposes. The test is whether the entity itself is classified as a foreign entity, not whether its owners are foreign. However, if the LLC is disregarded for tax purposes and the single member is a foreign person, FIRPTA withholding applies.

## Common Mistakes Nevada Agents Make With FIRPTA

The first error agents make is assuming the title company handles everything without any agent involvement. Title companies in Nevada do process the mechanical filing, but if the agent fails to identify a foreign seller or fails to communicate that status to the title company, the withholding simply does not happen—and the buyer inherits the liability.

The second mistake is accepting a Non-Foreign Affidavit without reviewing it for completeness. An affidavit missing a TIN, containing a foreign address, or signed by someone other than the actual transferor creates a false sense of security. Agents are not expected to be tax attorneys, but a two-minute review of the document for obvious red flags is a reasonable standard of care.

Third, agents confuse residency status with citizenship. A seller can be a U.S. green card holder (lawful permanent resident) and still be classified as a non-foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. Conversely, a person who lives in Las Vegas on a work visa but has not met the substantial presence test may still be classified as foreign. The [IRS FIRPTA guidance page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/firpta-withholding) provides the definitive test.

Fourth, listing agents fail to advise foreign sellers early enough in the process to apply for a withholding certificate. Form 8288-B takes approximately 90 days to process. If the seller does not file until after entering escrow on a 30-day close, the options narrow to either delaying closing or withholding the full 15% and seeking a refund later.

Fifth, agents working in Nevada's luxury market above $1 million forget that the personal residence exemption does not reduce the rate below 15% at that tier. A foreign national selling a $3 million home in Summerlin faces a $450,000 withholding regardless of whether the buyer intends to live there.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokerage-level compliance starts with training but must extend to file audits.** The Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED), which operates under the Nevada Department of Business and Industry, holds brokers responsible for supervising their agents' transactions under NRS 645.630 and NAC 645.610. While NRED does not directly enforce FIRPTA (that falls to the IRS), a pattern of agent negligence on federal withholding issues could constitute a failure of supervision under state law.

Brokers should verify that every transaction file involving a seller who is not clearly a U.S. citizen contains either a completed Non-Foreign Affidavit or documentation that FIRPTA withholding was processed at closing. This is a binary audit point—one or the other must be present.

| Audit Checkpoint | Document Required | Who Provides It |
|---|---|---|
| Seller is U.S. citizen/resident | Non-Foreign Affidavit (Certificate of Non-Foreign Status) | Seller |
| Seller is foreign person, full withholding | IRS Form 8288 and 8288-A confirmation | Title/escrow company |
| Seller applied for reduced withholding | IRS Form 8288-B filing confirmation | Seller's tax counsel |
| Buyer claims residence exemption for reduced rate | Buyer's affidavit of intended use | Buyer |
| Entity seller (LLC, corp, trust) | Entity classification documentation | Seller or seller's counsel |

Brokers managing teams with international clientele—particularly in Clark County and Washoe County—should consider adding a FIRPTA compliance step to their [transaction coordination checklist](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory). Missing this step does not just risk IRS penalties; it risks E&O claims from buyers who discover post-closing that they owe the IRS $75,000 they never budgeted for.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

The IRS does not treat FIRPTA non-compliance as a paperwork technicality. Under IRC Section 1445(d), the buyer who fails to withhold becomes personally liable for the tax that should have been withheld, plus interest and potential penalties. The penalty for failure to collect can reach 100% of the amount that should have been withheld.

For agents, the exposure comes through IRC 1445(d) and Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(e), which extend potential liability to the "transferee's agent" if that agent had actual knowledge that the transferor was a foreign person and failed to notify the transferee. The penalty for the agent can be up to the full amount of the required withholding—meaning on a $2 million sale, an agent could face personal liability of $300,000.

Deal cancellation is another practical risk. When FIRPTA issues surface mid-escrow because the listing agent failed to disclose the seller's foreign status early, buyers sometimes exercise contingencies and walk. The listing agent loses the deal, the seller loses the buyer, and the brokerage loses revenue—all because of a conversation that should have happened during the listing appointment.

Nevada agents should also consider that [missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on the 20-day Form 8288 filing window create compounding penalties even when the withholding amount is correct. Late filing penalties accrue monthly under IRC 6651.

## Practical Workflow for Nevada Agents Representing Foreign Sellers

At the listing appointment, ask directly whether the seller is a U.S. citizen or permanent resident. If they are not, discuss the withholding implications immediately and recommend they consult a tax professional about filing Form 8288-B for a potential reduced withholding certificate. Document this conversation in writing.

During escrow, confirm with the title company that FIRPTA withholding has been calculated correctly and that Forms 8288 and 8288-A will be filed within 20 days of closing. If the seller has a pending withholding certificate application, coordinate with the escrow officer on holding funds pending the IRS determination.

For buyer's agents, confirm that your client understands their legal obligation as the transferee. If the seller provides a Non-Foreign Affidavit, review it for the required elements—name, TIN, address, and signature under penalty of perjury. If anything appears inconsistent, raise it with the title company before closing, not after.
