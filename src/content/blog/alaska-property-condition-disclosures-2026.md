# Alaska Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026: What Every Agent Must Get Right

Getting Alaska property condition & environmental disclosures 2026 compliance wrong does not just kill deals — it creates the kind of liability that follows agents for years. Alaska's disclosure framework is deceptively straightforward on paper, but the overlap of state statutes, federal environmental mandates, and the sheer variety of property conditions unique to the state makes this an area where mistakes compound fast. If you are listing residential property anywhere from Juneau to Fairbanks, here is exactly what the law demands and where your peers keep stumbling.

## The Statutory Framework: AS 34.70 and What It Actually Requires

Alaska's residential real property transfer disclosure statute lives in [Alaska Statutes Title 34, Chapter 70](https://www.akleg.gov/basis/statutes.asp#34.70). AS 34.70.010 requires that every seller of residential real property containing one to four dwelling units deliver a completed written disclosure statement to the buyer before the buyer executes a purchase agreement. The statute specifies that this disclosure must be made "in good faith" and cover known material defects affecting the property.

The Alaska Real Estate Commission, operating under the Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing within the Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development, oversees licensee compliance. The Commission does not publish its own mandatory disclosure form, but the Alaska Association of Realtors provides the standard Residential Real Property Transfer Disclosure Statement that has become the industry default. Agents working outside of the association's ecosystem need to ensure that whatever form they use meets every element outlined in AS 34.70.020.

**There are narrow exemptions under AS 34.70.110, but they are narrower than most agents assume.** Transfers by foreclosure, transfers between co-owners, and transfers by a fiduciary in the administration of a decedent's estate are exempt. Court-ordered transfers and transfers by a governmental entity are also carved out. A standard arm's-length sale between a willing buyer and a willing seller almost never qualifies for an exemption, period.

## Specific Forms and the Environmental Add-Ons

The core form agents should know is the Residential Real Property Transfer Disclosure Statement, commonly referenced by its Alaska Association of Realtors form number. This document covers structural systems, plumbing, electrical, heating, water and sewer, roof condition, boundary disputes, title defects, and environmental hazards. Sellers are directed to disclose known defects across each category or mark them as unknown.

Beyond the state form, federal law adds a second mandatory layer for certain properties. The EPA's lead-based paint disclosure requirement under 42 U.S.C. 4852d and the HUD/EPA rule at 24 CFR Part 35 applies to every residential property built before 1978. Agents must ensure delivery of the EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home," obtain the seller's completed lead-based paint disclosure form, and include the required lead paint warning language in the purchase agreement. This is not optional, and it applies everywhere in Alaska.

|---|---|---|---|
| Residential Property Transfer Disclosure | Sale of 1-4 unit residential property | AS 34.70 | AAR Residential Disclosure Statement |
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure | Pre-1978 residential property | 42 U.S.C. 4852d / 24 CFR 35 | EPA Lead Paint Disclosure Form |
| Flood Zone Disclosure | Property in FEMA-designated flood zone | NFIP / AS 34.70.020 | Addressed within state disclosure form |

Alaska's unique geography introduces environmental concerns that agents in the Lower 48 rarely encounter. Permafrost conditions, prior contamination from fuel storage tanks (especially common in rural areas), well water contamination, and seismic hazard history all fall within the "known material defects" a seller must disclose. The Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation (ADEC) maintains contaminated sites databases that listing agents should be checking when they suspect environmental issues.

## What Happens When You Fail to Comply

**The consequences for noncompliance range from deal cancellation to personal monetary liability, and in serious cases, license discipline.** Under AS 34.70.080, if the seller fails to deliver the required disclosure before the buyer signs the purchase agreement, the buyer has the right to rescind within three days after delivery — or at any point before closing if delivery never occurs. That rescission right is statutory, not negotiable.

From a licensee standpoint, AS 08.88.391 imposes an independent duty on agents to disclose material facts they know about. If you know the crawlspace floods every spring and the seller checked "no known defects" on the water intrusion line, your silence does not protect you. The Alaska Real Estate Commission can open a disciplinary investigation under AS 08.88.071 and 12 AAC 64, which can result in license suspension, revocation, fines, or mandatory education. Civil lawsuits from buyers alleging fraudulent concealment add another layer of financial exposure.

## Five Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Make

The first mistake is treating the disclosure form as the seller's problem alone. Agents regularly hand the blank form to the seller, collect the completed version, and pass it through without reviewing a single answer. Alaska law does not require the agent to verify seller statements, but it absolutely requires agents to disclose material facts independently known. If you walked the property and noticed foundation cracks, water staining, or fuel oil odors, you cannot pretend you did not.

The second mistake is missing the lead-based paint disclosure on pre-1978 homes. This is federal, not state, and violations carry penalties of up to $19,507 per violation under the Toxic Substances Control Act as adjusted for inflation. Agents sometimes skip this form because the property "looks renovated," but renovation does not eliminate the obligation unless the home was built in 1978 or later.

**The third mistake is ignoring environmental red flags specific to Alaska.** Underground heating oil storage tanks are ubiquitous in rural and semi-rural Alaska properties. If the seller knows or should know about an abandoned or leaking tank, that fact must appear on the disclosure. Agents who skip a cursory check of the ADEC contaminated sites database before listing a property are rolling the dice.

The fourth mistake involves timing. AS 34.70.030 requires delivery before the buyer executes the purchase agreement. Agents who send the disclosure concurrently with the purchase agreement or — worse — after mutual execution create an automatic rescission window that can blow up a deal at the worst possible moment.

The fifth mistake is failing to retain copies. Under the [general best practices for documentation in real estate transactions](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), every disclosure form, every delivery confirmation, and every amendment should be filed in the transaction record. The Alaska Real Estate Commission can request documentation years after a transaction closes.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory responsibility under AS 08.88.081 and the Commission's regulations at 12 AAC 64. That responsibility extends to ensuring every agent under their supervision is delivering disclosures on time, retaining documentation, and flagging known material facts. A broker who allows systemic noncompliance risks their own license in addition to the agent's.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure delivery timing | Confirm disclosure delivered before purchase agreement execution | Every transaction |
| Lead paint form for pre-1978 properties | Verify form completed, pamphlet delivered, language in purchase agreement | Every applicable transaction |
| Agent independent disclosure notes | Review agent's file notes for material facts observed during property visits | Quarterly file audit |
| Environmental red flag documentation | Check for ADEC database review or tank disclosure where applicable | Every listing with oil heat or rural parcels |
| Signature and date verification | Ensure all disclosure forms have complete buyer and seller signatures and dates | Every transaction |

Brokers should build a standardized compliance review into their [transaction coordination workflows](/blog/where-deals-break-down). The review does not need to be elaborate, but it must be consistent. A single missed lead-paint form in a post-closing complaint can trigger a Commission investigation that audits the entire brokerage's files.

**Quarterly file audits are the minimum defensible standard.** During those audits, brokers should verify that disclosure forms match the property characteristics — a pre-1978 home with no lead paint form in the file is an instant red flag. Environmental disclosures referencing underground storage tanks or contamination history should be cross-referenced against the ADEC contaminated sites program records when available.

## Alaska-Specific Environmental Issues That Demand Extra Attention

Permafrost degradation is not an abstract climate discussion for Alaska agents — it is a disclosure issue. If a seller knows the property sits on permafrost-affected soil and has experienced settling, heaving, or structural movement, that knowledge triggers disclosure obligations under AS 34.70.020. Buyers purchasing in Fairbanks, the Mat-Su Valley, or other Interior regions should receive honest answers about foundation performance.

Flood risk adds another layer. Properties within FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Areas require disclosure, and sellers who have filed flood insurance claims are generally required to report that history. Agents listing properties along the Matanuska River, Kenai River, or in coastal communities subject to storm surge should verify whether the property carries a flood insurance history.

Water quality disclosures are also critical in areas served by private wells rather than municipal systems. If a seller has tested the well and knows about contamination from prior tests, that information must be disclosed. The ADEC Drinking Water Program and local borough health departments maintain records that agents can reference when questions arise.
