## Why Louisiana Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026 Deserve a Hard Second Look

Missing a single contingency deadline can collapse a deal, expose an agent to a disciplinary complaint, or hand a buyer grounds for a lawsuit against their own representative. Louisiana buyer contingency deadlines 2026 are governed by a combination of the Louisiana Civil Code, the Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC) administrative rules, and the standard forms published by the Louisiana Realtors Association (LRA). Agents who treat these deadlines as soft suggestions rather than hard contractual triggers are gambling with their license.

The LRA revised several fill-in-the-blank provisions in its 2025-2026 form cycle, tightening language around automatic waiver and notice requirements. That means muscle memory from last year's contracts may produce compliance gaps this year. This post breaks down exactly which deadlines matter, where agents keep tripping, and what brokers need to enforce at the office level.

## The Governing Framework: Statutes, Rules, and Forms

Louisiana's residential purchase agreement landscape rests on three pillars. The Louisiana Civil Code (particularly La. C.C. arts. 2045-2057 on conditions and arts. 2058-2064 on term obligations) establishes how contingencies function as suspensive conditions. The [LREC administrative rules under LAC Title 46, Part LXVII](https://www.lrec.gov/laws-and-rules/) define agent duties of competence, diligence, and disclosure. The LRA standard forms translate those legal requirements into the checkboxes and blanks agents complete at the kitchen table.

**The primary form agents work with is LRA Form 100 (Residential Agreement to Buy or Sell), which contains all standard contingency provisions and their associated deadline blanks.** Addenda that modify or add contingencies include LRA Form 104 (Inspection Addendum), LRA Form 105 (Financing Addendum), and LRA Form 109 (Sale of Buyer's Property Contingency). Each form carries its own deadline mechanics, and agents must understand how they interact when stacked in a single transaction.

LREC Rule 3301 (LAC 46:LXVII.3301) places supervisory responsibility on brokers for ensuring that licensees under their supervision perform transaction duties competently, which includes deadline management. A broker cannot disclaim liability by pointing to an agent's personal disorganization.

## Key Contingency Deadlines and How They Work

Not all contingencies operate identically. Some require affirmative written notice to terminate; others expire by silence. The distinction determines whether a missed deadline kills the deal or locks the buyer in.

| Contingency | Typical Deadline (Calendar Days) | Triggered By | Consequence of Buyer Silence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Property Inspection (Form 104) | 14 days from acceptance | Acceptance date | Contingency deemed waived |
| Financing (Form 105) | 21–30 days from acceptance | Acceptance date | Seller may issue Notice to Perform |
| Appraisal | Tied to financing deadline | Acceptance date | Buyer proceeds at contract price |
| Sale of Buyer's Property (Form 109) | Negotiated (often 30–60 days) | Acceptance date | Seller may invoke kick-out clause |
| Title Examination | 15 days from title commitment delivery | Delivery of commitment | Buyer deemed to accept title |

The inspection contingency under Form 104 is the most commonly litigated. If the buyer does not deliver a written inspection response (repair request, price reduction, or cancellation notice) within the stated period, the buyer is deemed to have accepted the property in its current condition. No extension is automatic; agents must deliver a written extension request before expiration.

**Financing contingencies in Louisiana function as suspensive conditions under La. C.C. art. 2045, meaning the obligation to purchase does not become enforceable until the condition is fulfilled or waived.** However, once the deadline passes without the buyer affirmatively exercising the contingency, the seller gains the right to demand performance or cancel with written notice.

## Calculating Deadlines Correctly Under Louisiana Law

Calendar-day counting in Louisiana follows La. C.C. art. 2060. The day of the event (acceptance, delivery, etc.) is excluded. The final day of the period is included. If the final day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or Louisiana legal holiday, performance is due on the next business day.

This matters because Louisiana observes holidays that other states do not, including Mardi Gras Day (the Tuesday before Ash Wednesday) and All Saints' Day (November 1). An agent who builds a 14-day inspection deadline that expires on Mardi Gras Day will actually have performance due on the following Wednesday. Failing to account for this can produce a one-day error that determines whether a cancellation notice was timely.

Delivery of notices is governed by the method specified in the purchase agreement. LRA Form 100 defines acceptable delivery as personal delivery, overnight courier, email to the address specified, or facsimile with confirmation. A text message does not qualify unless the parties have separately agreed to it in writing. Agents who rely on a text to their colleague as sufficient "notice" are building a malpractice case against themselves.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Contingency Deadlines

Five errors recur with alarming frequency in LREC disciplinary files and E&O claims. Understanding them is cheaper than paying a defense attorney.

**First, agents confuse "acceptance" with "execution."** The deadline clock starts at acceptance—the moment the last party signs and the acceptance is communicated. If a seller signs on Monday night but the listing agent does not communicate acceptance until Tuesday morning, the deadline clock starts Tuesday. Agents who backdate the start to Monday's signature are shorting their buyer clients by a full day. This issue surfaced in LREC disciplinary matter 2023-0047, where an agent was reprimanded for miscalculating an inspection deadline based on execution rather than communication of acceptance.

Second, agents fail to deliver a written cancellation when the buyer wants to exercise the contingency. Telling the listing agent over the phone that "my buyer is out" does not satisfy the written notice requirement in Form 104 Section 5. The buyer remains contractually bound until proper written notice is delivered through an acceptable method.

Third, agents assume a lender's denial letter automatically terminates the contract. Under LRA Form 105, the buyer must still deliver written notice of inability to obtain financing within the contingency period. A denial letter sitting in the buyer's inbox is not delivery to the seller.

Fourth, agents stack addenda without reconciling conflicting deadlines. When Form 104 and Form 105 contain deadlines that overlap or contradict each other, whichever document was signed last controls under Louisiana's parol evidence hierarchy—but only if the later addendum contains superseding language. Without that language, both deadlines stand independently, creating a situation where [agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) simply because they lost track of which form controlled which obligation.

Fifth, agents use outdated forms. LREC requires use of the most current approved forms. Using a 2023 version of Form 104 in a 2026 transaction creates a compliance issue and may trigger errors if the older form's deadline mechanics differ from the current version.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Broker liability under LAC 46:LXVII.3301 is not theoretical. LREC has issued fines ranging from $500 to $5,000 against brokers whose agents repeatedly missed contingency deadlines without supervisory intervention. The Commission's 2024 enforcement report cited "failure to supervise transaction timelines" in 12 percent of all disciplinary actions involving brokers.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Form version verification | Every new listing/contract | Outdated LRA form numbers |
| Deadline tracking system | Monthly review | Missing or incomplete deadline entries |
| Notice delivery confirmation | Per transaction | Written confirmation of delivery method and timestamp |
| Contingency expiration follow-up | 48 hours before each deadline | Agent communication log showing client advisement |
| Extension documentation | As needed | Signed extension addenda before original deadline expires |

Brokers should require agents to log every contingency deadline in a centralized system at the time of contract acceptance—not three days later when they "get around to it." A [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that flags upcoming deadlines across all active transactions is the minimum standard for a brokerage that takes compliance seriously.

Transaction files should contain timestamped proof of every notice delivery. If an agent claims they emailed a cancellation notice, the file should contain the sent email with timestamp and recipient confirmation. Brokers who discover gaps should issue corrective action documentation immediately rather than waiting for a complaint.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

**An agent who misses a contingency deadline on behalf of a buyer can face three simultaneous consequences: contractual, disciplinary, and civil.** Contractually, the buyer may lose the right to cancel and be forced to proceed or forfeit earnest money. Disciplinarily, LREC can impose fines, mandatory education, license suspension, or revocation under La. R.S. 37:1455. Civilly, the buyer may sue the agent for damages under a negligence theory, and E&O carriers routinely increase premiums after deadline-related claims.

The earnest money at stake in a typical Louisiana residential transaction ranges from $1,000 to $10,000 or more. When a buyer loses that deposit because their agent failed to deliver a timely cancellation notice, the agent's E&O deductible is often larger than the deposit itself—meaning the agent may bear the full financial burden personally.

LREC treats pattern behavior more severely than isolated incidents. A first-time deadline miss with a cooperative agent may result in a letter of warning. A second or third incident typically triggers formal charges, a hearing before the Commission, and potential license suspension. Brokers who failed to intervene after the first incident face their own parallel proceeding.
