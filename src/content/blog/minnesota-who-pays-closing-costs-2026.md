## Understanding Minnesota Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Minnesota who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by a single statute. Unlike some states where law mandates specific fee allocation, Minnesota leaves nearly all closing cost splits to the purchase agreement negotiated between the parties. That flexibility creates opportunity for skilled agents — and real liability for those who leave contract language vague.

Minnesota Statute Chapter 507 governs conveyancing requirements, while Chapter 287 establishes the deed tax and mortgage registry tax obligations. The Minnesota Department of Commerce oversees licensee conduct, and agents who misadvise clients on cost allocation can face disciplinary action under Minnesota Statute 82.81. Knowing which costs are customary versus which are legally mandated separates competent practitioners from those inviting complaints.

This post breaks down the specific fees each party typically bears, the forms that memorialize those allocations, and the mistakes that get agents into trouble.

## Mandatory Costs Imposed by Minnesota Law

**Only a handful of closing costs in Minnesota are assigned by statute rather than contract.** The state deed tax under Minnesota Statute 287.21 falls on the grantor (seller) at a rate of $3.30 per $1,000 of net consideration. Hennepin and Ramsey counties add an additional $1.65 per $1,000 under the Environmental Response Fund surcharge.

The mortgage registry tax — Minnesota Statute 287.05 — is the buyer's obligation when recording a new mortgage, assessed at $2.30 per $1,000 of the debt secured. Conservation fees under Minnesota Statute 40A.152 attach a $5 per-transaction fee to all recorded documents. These statutory costs are non-negotiable in their assignment.

Everything else — title insurance premiums, closing agent fees, survey costs, recording fees beyond the statutory minimum — is subject to negotiation. Agents who tell clients "the seller always pays for the title search" are confusing custom with law.

The table below reflects prevailing custom in the Twin Cities metro market as of 2026, though rural outstate markets may differ. Custom is not binding; it merely fills gaps when the purchase agreement is silent.

The well disclosure certificate requirement under Minnesota Statute 103I.235 is particularly notable because failure to provide it can void the transaction. The seller bears the inspection cost by custom because the statute places the disclosure obligation on them.

## The Purchase Agreement Forms That Govern Allocation

Minnesota agents overwhelmingly use forms published by Minnesota Realtors (MNR). The primary residential purchase agreement — currently MNR Form 5 (2026 revision) — contains lines specifically designating which party pays title insurance, closing fees, and special assessments. Paragraph 10 of the form addresses closing costs and prorations, and agents must fill it out completely rather than relying on assumptions.

**Leaving any allocation line blank in MNR Form 5 is the single most common source of closing-table disputes in Minnesota.** When that happens, the title company or closing agent must either contact both parties for clarification — delaying closing — or apply local custom, which may not match either party's expectations.

For buyer-side representation, the MNR Buyer Representation Agreement (Form 101) now requires disclosure of any compensation arrangement affecting how closing costs interact with agent fees. Post-NAR settlement practice changes mean agents should also confirm that concession requests in MNR Form 5 do not inadvertently create prohibited tying arrangements under the Minnesota Department of Commerce's advertising and compensation rules.

Agents working with new construction will encounter MNR Form 20, which allocates costs differently because builders frequently bundle title company selection and absorb the owner's policy cost. Even there, the allocation must be explicit on the form — oral agreements about who pays what are unenforceable under [Minnesota's Statute of Frauds (Minn. Stat. 513.05)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/513.05).

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Cost Allocation

**First, agents routinely quote seller net sheets using metro-area customs in outstate markets.** In parts of Greater Minnesota — particularly the Iron Range and southern border counties — the buyer historically pays for the owner's title policy. Agents relocating from the metro who assume seller-pay custom can create unrealistic seller expectations and blow up deals late in the process.

Second, agents miscalculate seller credits by ignoring lender caps. A buyer's lender may permit only a 3% seller concession on a conventional loan with less than 10% down. When an agent writes a $12,000 seller credit on a $300,000 home financed at 5% down, the lender will reject the excess — and the deal stalls. Agents must verify lender guidelines before drafting concession language.

Third, agents confuse special assessments with property taxes. Under MNR Form 5, Paragraph 10, the treatment of pending and levied special assessments requires explicit election. A pending assessment for a street reconstruction in Brooklyn Park could run $15,000 or more. Agents who check the wrong box — or leave the section incomplete — expose their clients to surprise five-figure charges at closing.

Fourth, some agents verbally promise buyers that "the seller will cover your closing costs" during showings without confirming this in writing. If the purchase agreement omits the credit, the buyer may have a misrepresentation claim against their own agent. The Minnesota Department of Commerce takes these complaints seriously under its authority in Minnesota Statute 82.81(a)(3), which covers misleading conduct.

Fifth, dual agents sometimes allocate costs in a way that favors one party without adequate written informed consent from both. Under Minnesota Statute 82.67, a dual agent owes loyalty to both parties. An unbalanced allocation that benefits the seller on costs — without clear buyer consent — can trigger license discipline and civil liability.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear vicarious liability for their agents' contract errors under Minnesota Statute 82.81(b). A supervising broker who fails to catch an incomplete cost-allocation section in a purchase agreement may face a complaint alongside the agent. Regular file audits should flag any MNR Form 5 where Paragraph 10 fields are left blank or contradicted by an addendum.

**Brokers should also audit seller net sheets and buyer estimates produced by their agents to ensure they match the signed purchase agreement.** Discrepancies between marketing-stage estimates and contract terms generate the majority of closing-table disputes reported to the Minnesota Department of Commerce's enforcement division.

Here is a quick-reference table brokers can use in their audit process:

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Look For | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|
| MNR Form 5, Paragraph 10 completeness | All cost lines filled; special assessment election marked | Closing delay; client complaint |
| Seller credit vs. lender cap alignment | Credit amount within loan-program limits | Loan denial; renegotiation |
| Dual-agency cost allocation | Written consent from both parties | License discipline under 82.67 |
| Well/septic disclosure (Form 103I) | Certificate completed before closing | Transaction voidable under 103I.235 |
| Net sheet accuracy | Matches signed contract terms | Misrepresentation claim under 82.81 |

Brokers who discover non-compliance mid-transaction should document the correction in writing and ensure an amended purchase agreement is signed before closing. Oral corrections do not satisfy the Statute of Frauds and will not protect the brokerage in litigation.

## Liability Consequences for Non-Compliance

An agent who fails to properly memorialize closing cost allocation faces three tiers of risk. The Minnesota Department of Commerce can impose fines up to $10,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, and require corrective education under Minnesota Statute 82.81. Civil liability exposes the agent and brokerage to damage claims from either party — commonly the difference between what the client expected to pay and what they actually owed at closing.

Deal cancellation is the third risk. If a buyer's lender rejects a transaction because seller credits exceed program limits — or because the purchase agreement contradicts the loan estimate — either party may invoke a financing contingency to walk away. The listing agent's seller then loses time on market and potentially a lower re-sale price. These cascading losses trace back to a single form error.

Agents sometimes assume errors and omissions insurance will cover these claims. E&O policies frequently exclude coverage for incomplete contract drafting or verbal promises not reduced to writing. The defense costs alone can run $20,000 or more even when coverage applies. Preventing the mistake is cheaper than litigating it.
