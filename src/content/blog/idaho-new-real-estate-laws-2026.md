## Idaho New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Changes That Matter

The Idaho legislature passed several bills during the 2025 session that reshape how residential transactions work starting in 2026. If you hold an active license in the state, the phrase **Idaho new real estate laws 2026** should be front-of-mind at your next team meeting. Two bills in particular — HB 312 and SB 1089 — amend portions of Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20 and Title 55, Chapter 25, directly affecting disclosure obligations, buyer representation, and commission transparency.

The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) has already issued [guidance bulletins through its official site](https://irec.idaho.gov/) outlining compliance timelines. Agents who assume they can figure it out later are making a bet that rarely pays off once a transaction goes sideways.

This article breaks down the specific changes, the forms involved, and the real consequences of non-compliance. Every section here maps to something you will encounter on your next deal.

## Buyer Representation Agreements Are No Longer Optional

**HB 312 amends Idaho Code 54-2083 to require a signed, written buyer representation agreement before an agent provides brokerage services to a buyer.** This mirrors the national shift post-NAR settlement, but Idaho's version includes state-specific language requirements that differ from the generic national templates.

The approved form is the RE-44 Buyer Representation Agreement, published by the Idaho Association of Realtors in coordination with IREC. Agents must present and execute this form prior to showing property — not after, not at the offer stage, and not as a retroactive signature at closing.

The statute defines "brokerage services" broadly. Sending a buyer a listing link with commentary, scheduling a showing, or advising on pricing all qualify. If you do any of these without the RE-44 in place after March 1, 2026, you are operating outside your authorized scope.

| Requirement | Before 2026 | After March 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Written buyer agreement | Recommended, not required | Mandatory under IC 54-2083 |
| Form required | None specified | RE-44 (or equivalent IREC-approved) |
| Timing of execution | Flexible | Before brokerage services begin |
| Commission disclosure | Verbal acceptable | Must be in writing within the RE-44 |
| Termination clause | Optional | Required with specific duration cap |

The duration cap is significant. SB 1089 limits initial buyer representation agreements to six months unless the buyer initiates a written extension. This prevents indefinite lockups that have drawn consumer complaints in other states.

## Updated Seller Disclosure Requirements Under Title 55

Idaho Code 55-2501 through 55-2515 already mandated seller disclosure, but the 2026 amendments expand the scope of what sellers must reveal. The revised RE-25 Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Form now includes sections on prior insurance claims, water rights adjudication status, and known wildfire risk designations.

**Listing agents must deliver the updated RE-25 form — not the pre-2026 version — for any listing entered into MLS after January 1, 2026.** Using the outdated form does not satisfy the statutory obligation, even if the content of the disclosure is identical.

The new wildfire risk section is particularly relevant for properties in the Boise foothills, McCall corridor, and any WUI (Wildland-Urban Interface) zone. Sellers must disclose whether the property has received a wildfire risk assessment and whether any mitigation was recommended but not completed. This tracks with Idaho Department of Lands mapping data that became publicly accessible in 2025.

Failure to provide the updated RE-25 triggers buyer rescission rights under 55-2508. The buyer may cancel the contract within three business days of receiving a late or non-conforming disclosure — or at any point before closing if no disclosure was provided at all. Agents who facilitate a closing without proper disclosure expose themselves and their brokerage to civil liability claims for damages the buyer discovers post-closing.

## Commission Transparency and the New RE-47 Form

SB 1089 introduces a standalone commission disclosure document — the RE-47 Commission Disclosure Statement — that must be presented to all parties at or before the time an offer is submitted. This is separate from whatever commission terms exist in the listing agreement or buyer representation agreement.

The RE-47 requires plain-language disclosure of who pays the buyer's agent, the exact dollar amount or percentage, and whether any portion of the commission is shared with a referral entity. It must be signed by both the buyer and seller (or their respective agents on their behalf under written authorization).

This form exists because Idaho's version of commission reform goes further than the NAR settlement baseline. The legislature wanted a single document — visible to both sides — that eliminates any ambiguity about money flow. Agents who bury commission terms exclusively in the listing agreement or rely on MLS fields alone will not satisfy 54-2087(3) as amended.

For context on how other states are handling similar disclosure shifts, the [Wisconsin approach to commission structure changes](/blog/wisconsin-commission-structure-changes-2026) provides an instructive comparison. Idaho's version is more prescriptive about form usage but less restrictive on allowable structures.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

Experience with similar legislative changes in other states suggests a predictable set of compliance failures. Here are the errors most likely to generate IREC complaints and deal blowups in 2026.

**First: using the old RE-25 form after January 1.** Many agents have PDF templates saved locally or in their transaction management systems. If your system auto-populates an outdated form, you are non-compliant from day one. Audit every template in every system you use — CRM, DocuSign templates, TC platforms, and brokerage file systems.

Second: executing the RE-44 after services have already been provided. The statute is clear about timing, and IREC's guidance bulletin specifically states that retroactive execution does not cure the violation. If you showed a buyer three homes last week without the RE-44, signing it today does not erase the exposure.

Third: omitting the termination date or exceeding the six-month cap on buyer agreements. IREC has signaled this will be an audit priority. Agreements without a specified end date are voidable, meaning the buyer can walk at any time and you have no recourse for commission recovery.

Fourth: failing to present the RE-47 to both parties. Some agents will present it only to their own client. The statute requires both sides to receive and acknowledge the form. If the other agent is not cooperating, document your attempts in writing — but the obligation remains yours.

Fifth: treating the wildfire disclosure section as optional or marking it "unknown" without investigation. The standard is not what the seller happens to know off the top of their head. If public records from Idaho Department of Lands indicate a WUI designation, the seller — and by extension the listing agent who counsels them — cannot claim ignorance. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry heightened responsibility under Idaho Code 54-2038, which requires supervision of all licensees within the brokerage. The 2026 changes add specific audit obligations that IREC has indicated will factor into brokerage-level compliance reviews.

**Brokers must verify that every active transaction file contains the correct version of the RE-25, RE-44 (where applicable), and RE-47 before closing.** This is not a best practice — it is now a condition of maintaining brokerage licensure under the amended supervision requirements.

| Audit Item | Frequency Required | Consequence of Failure |
|---|---|---|
| RE-44 execution date vs. first service date | Per transaction | Brokerage fine up to $5,000 per violation |
| RE-25 form version verification | Per listing | Buyer rescission, agent discipline |
| RE-47 dual-party acknowledgment | Per transaction | IREC complaint, deal cancellation risk |
| Buyer agreement duration compliance | Quarterly review | Voided agreements, commission loss |
| Wildfire disclosure completeness | Per WUI-zone listing | Civil liability, E&O claims |

Brokers should also update their policies and procedures manual to reflect the new statutory requirements. IREC's 2026 audit protocol, published in draft form in Q4 2025, specifically asks for written brokerage policies addressing each of the new forms. A brokerage without updated written policies is a brokerage asking for an adverse finding.

Transaction coordinators handling Idaho files need updated checklists immediately. If your TC is still working off a 2024 compliance framework, every file is at risk. For teams handling volume, [understanding why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is the difference between one missed form and a systemic compliance failure across dozens of files.

## Penalties and Liability: What Actually Happens When You Get It Wrong

IREC's disciplinary authority under Idaho Code 54-2060 includes license suspension, revocation, fines up to $5,000 per violation, and mandatory continuing education requirements. The 2026 amendments add a new provision: automatic fine escalation for repeat violations within a 24-month window. A first offense on RE-47 non-compliance might result in a $1,000 fine and a corrective action plan. A second offense within two years triggers $5,000 and a mandatory 90-day supervision period.

Civil liability runs in parallel. Buyers who discover undisclosed wildfire risk or who were not informed of commission structures have private causes of action under both the disclosure statutes and Idaho's Consumer Protection Act (Idaho Code 48-601 et seq.). Damages can include rescission, repair costs, and attorney fees.

**E&O insurance carriers are already issuing bulletins about the 2026 changes.** Multiple carriers have indicated that claims arising from use of outdated forms may not be covered if the insurer determines the agent knew or should have known about the statutory update. This is not theoretical — carriers denied similar claims in Oregon after that state's 2024 disclosure overhaul.

> "Licensees are expected to know and comply with current statutory requirements. Ignorance of a legislative change effective on a published date is not a defense to a complaint." — IREC Guidance Bulletin 2025-04
