## Understanding Wisconsin Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Wisconsin earnest money & escrow laws 2026 continue to trip up even experienced agents, and the consequences have grown sharper as the Department of Safety and Professional Services (DSPS) steps up enforcement audits. If you hold a Wisconsin real estate license, you are bound by Chapter 452 of the Wisconsin Statutes and Wisconsin Administrative Code Chapter REEB 18, both of which dictate how trust funds—including earnest money—must be handled. Getting this wrong doesn't just kill a deal; it puts your license at risk.

The regulatory framework hasn't changed dramatically from prior years, but DSPS has issued updated guidance on electronic trust account records and shortened its review timeline for complaints. Agents who treated escrow compliance as a "broker problem" are finding themselves named individually in disciplinary proceedings. This post breaks down the statutes, forms, deposit timelines, disbursement rules, common mistakes, and broker-level audit responsibilities you need to master right now.

## The Statutory Framework: Chapter 452 and REEB 18

**Wisconsin Statutes Section 452.13 establishes the foundational trust account requirements for every broker operating in the state.** It mandates that all funds received by a broker on behalf of another person in a real estate transaction must be deposited into a trust account maintained at a Wisconsin financial institution. The statute does not allow commingling personal funds with trust funds except for a nominal amount to keep the account open—typically no more than $200.

Wisconsin Administrative Code [REEB 18](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/code/admin_code/reeb/18) provides the operational details. It specifies recordkeeping standards, reconciliation frequency, and the circumstances under which a broker may disburse trust funds. REEB 18.09 specifically addresses earnest money disputes, requiring brokers to retain the funds until they receive written mutual release from all parties or a court order directing disbursement.

The distinction matters because agents frequently confuse their obligations under the statutes versus the administrative code. Chapter 452 gives DSPS the authority to discipline; REEB 18 tells you precisely what conduct triggers that authority.

## Key Forms and Deposit Deadlines

Wisconsin agents conduct most residential transactions using the WB-11 Residential Offer to Purchase, published by the Wisconsin Department of Administration. This form contains a specific section—Line 2 of the Earnest Money paragraph—where parties designate the deposit amount, the form of payment, and the entity that will hold the funds. Agents also encounter the WB-14 (Residential Condominium Offer to Purchase) and WB-13 (Vacant Land Offer), each containing similar earnest money provisions.

**The WB-11 requires the buyer to deliver earnest money within the time frame stated in the offer.** If no specific time frame is written, the default expectation is delivery at the time the offer is made. Once the listing broker receives the earnest money, DSPS guidance directs deposit into the trust account by the end of the next business day following acceptance of the offer unless the offer specifies otherwise. Agents who hold checks in desk drawers "until the inspection passes" are violating this requirement.

| Scenario | Deposit Deadline | Governing Authority |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Earnest money delivered with offer | Next business day after acceptance | REEB 18.04 |
| Earnest money promised within X days | Per offer terms, then next business day after receipt | WB-11, REEB 18.04 |
| Wire transfer earnest money | Confirm receipt, document within 24 hours | DSPS guidance memo |
| Earnest money held by title company | Title company's own trust account rules apply | Contractual agreement |

One area that catches agents off guard: if the offer is rejected or withdrawn before acceptance, the earnest money must be returned immediately to the buyer. There is no discretion here. Holding rejected-offer earnest money for even a few days can generate a DSPS complaint.

## Disbursement Rules and Dispute Resolution

Disbursing earnest money is where most liability lives. Under REEB 18.09, when a transaction closes, the broker disburses trust funds according to the closing statement. That part is straightforward. The complexity arises when deals fall apart.

If both parties agree on who should receive the earnest money after a failed transaction, the broker needs that agreement in writing before releasing funds. The WB-44 (Amendment to Offer) or a standalone release form—commonly a broker-drafted mutual release—serves this purpose. Without mutual written consent, the broker must continue holding the funds.

**When the parties cannot agree, the broker faces a legal constraint: REEB 18.09 prohibits unilateral disbursement.** The broker cannot simply decide the buyer defaulted and hand funds to the seller. Instead, the broker must hold the funds until receiving either a signed mutual release, a court order, or expiration of the statutory interpleader timeline. Many brokers choose to interplead the funds—depositing them with the court and letting a judge decide—rather than risk liability for an improper disbursement.

Agents should also be aware that Wisconsin Statutes Section 452.13(3) authorizes DSPS to order a broker to interplead trust funds if a complaint is filed. The agency doesn't need to wait for a court action to force the issue.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Earnest Money

Knowing the rules isn't enough if you're repeating the same errors your colleagues make every quarter. Here are the specific mistakes that generate the most DSPS complaints and E&O claims in Wisconsin.

**First, agents fail to specify the deposit deadline in the WB-11.** Leaving the earnest money delivery timeframe blank creates ambiguity that benefits no one. Buyers assume they have until inspection; sellers assume the money is already deposited. When the deal collapses and the earnest money was never actually delivered, the agent faces questions from both sides—and from DSPS if a complaint follows.

Second, agents accept earnest money made payable to themselves rather than the broker's trust account. Wisconsin law is clear: trust funds belong in the broker's trust account. A check made out to "Jane Smith, Realtor" creates an immediate compliance problem even if the agent endorses it over to the brokerage. Train yourself to catch this at the offer stage.

Third, agents verbally promise earnest money refunds without written authorization. A buyer calls, says they're canceling under a contingency, and the agent tells them "your earnest money will be back in three days." But the listing agent disputes the contingency deadline. Now the broker is stuck between a verbal promise and a written dispute. Every disbursement commitment must be documented in writing first—no exceptions.

Fourth, agents misunderstand the [wire fraud risks](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) involved with electronic earnest money transfers. Fraudsters target real estate transactions specifically because of the large sums moving through email-coordinated wires. Wisconsin agents must verify wiring instructions through a secondary channel before directing any party to send funds.

Fifth, agents treat post-closing trust account reconciliation as optional. REEB 18.06 requires monthly reconciliation. If your brokerage handles its own escrow rather than using a title company, every agent generating deposits into that trust account shares responsibility for accurate recordkeeping.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry primary liability for trust account compliance under Section 452.13. This means every supervising broker in Wisconsin should maintain a systematic audit process—not just annual reviews, but monthly checkpoints that catch problems before DSPS does.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Bank balance matches ledger balance, outstanding deposits identified |
| Deposit timing | Per transaction | Earnest money deposited within deadline stated in offer |
| Disbursement authorization | Per transaction | Written mutual release or court order on file before any release |
| Stale funds review | Quarterly | Any funds held more than 90 days without activity—investigate |
| Agent-named checks | Ongoing | No checks made payable to individual agents deposited into trust |

Brokers who delegate trust account management to a bookkeeper or office manager remain personally responsible for compliance. DSPS does not recognize delegation as a defense. If your office handles escrow directly rather than routing to a title company, you need documented procedures, dual signatures on disbursements over a threshold amount, and immediate investigation of any discrepancies.

The enforcement consequences are real. DSPS can impose formal reprimands, require additional education, levy fines, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely. Under Wis. Stat. 452.17, a broker who mishandles trust funds may also face civil liability for actual damages plus attorney fees. Agents operating under that broker can have their own licenses disciplined if DSPS determines they participated in or facilitated the violation.
