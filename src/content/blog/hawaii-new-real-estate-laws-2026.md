## Hawaii New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Regulatory Shift Is Already Here

The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC) published its final rule amendments in late 2025, and the statutory changes under Hawaii new real estate laws 2026 took effect at the start of the year. If you have not updated your transaction files, disclosure packets, and office policies, you are already operating at risk. These are not minor tweaks—the legislature amended HRS Chapter 508D, adjusted commission licensing fees under HRS Chapter 467, and expanded climate-related disclosure obligations that directly affect every residential transaction closed on the islands.

This article breaks down each change, identifies the forms you need to swap out, and flags the penalties HIREC can impose when agents fall short. Brokers will also find a specific audit checklist later in the post.

## Revised Seller Disclosure Requirements Under HRS 508D

**The most consequential change for 2026 is the expanded Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement (SRPD), which now includes mandatory climate risk and sea-level rise sections.** Hawaii Act 178 (2025 Session Laws) amended HRS 508D-4 to require sellers to disclose known information regarding flood zone status, coastal erosion exposure, and any prior damage from hurricane, tsunami, or volcanic activity.

The new SRPD form—designated as SRPD-2026 by HIREC—replaces the prior version entirely. Agents who continue using the older form risk buyer rescission rights under HRS 508D-11, which grants buyers a 15-calendar-day right to rescind after receiving a compliant disclosure. Using an outdated form does not start that clock, meaning the buyer's rescission window remains open indefinitely until a conforming disclosure is delivered.

The form must be completed and delivered to the buyer before or at the time the buyer signs a binding purchase contract. Delivery after execution does not excuse the obligation—it merely extends the rescission period.

## Agency Disclosure Form Updates and Timing Rules

Hawaii's mandatory agency disclosure framework under HRS 467-14.5 also received a procedural overhaul. HIREC now requires agents to present the Agency Disclosure form (Form AD-2026) at first substantive contact rather than at the previously accepted "earliest practicable time." The distinction matters because HIREC's enforcement guidance defines first substantive contact as any discussion of property specifics, pricing, or client motivation—including phone calls and text messages.

**If an agent discusses a property's listing price or a buyer's budget before delivering the AD-2026 form, that agent has violated HRS 467-14.5.** The penalty structure under HRS 467-14(20) allows HIREC to impose fines up to $2,500 per violation, suspend a license, or refer the matter for revocation proceedings.

Dual agency consent also received clarification. Under the revised rules, both parties must sign separate acknowledgment sections on the AD-2026 form—a single combined signature block is no longer accepted. Agents facilitating dual agency must obtain informed written consent from each party on distinct pages of the form, and brokers must countersign within 48 hours.

## Licensing Fee Increases and Continuing Education Adjustments

The biennial license renewal fee for salespersons and brokers increased from $314 to $364 under amendments to Hawaii Administrative Rules Title 16, Chapter 99. The Real Estate Recovery Fund contribution also rose by $20 per renewal cycle. These increases took effect for all renewals processed after January 1, 2026.

| Fee Category | Previous Amount | 2026 Amount | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salesperson Biennial Renewal | $314 | $364 | +$50 |
| Broker Biennial Renewal | $314 | $364 | +$50 |
| Recovery Fund Contribution | $16 | $36 | +$20 |
| Late Renewal Penalty | Equal to fee | Equal to fee | No change |
| Original License Application | $100 | $125 | +$25 |

Continuing education requirements remain at 20 hours per biennium, but HIREC added a mandatory 3-hour module on climate risk disclosure and coastal hazard identification. This module must be completed through an approved provider and cannot be substituted with general elective credits. Agents who completed their CE cycle before the module became available in March 2026 must complete it as a supplemental requirement before their next renewal.

## Buyer Representation Agreement Mandates Post-NAR Settlement

Following the National Association of Realtors settlement implementation, Hawaii codified buyer representation agreement requirements through Act 203 (2025 Session Laws), adding HRS 467-14.6. Every agent working with a buyer must execute a written buyer representation agreement before showing properties, touring open houses on the buyer's behalf, or submitting offers.

The agreement must specify the compensation structure, duration of the agency relationship, and the scope of services the agent will provide. HIREC published a template form (BRA-2026) that satisfies statutory minimums, though brokerages may use custom agreements provided they contain all required elements listed in HRS 467-14.6(b).

**Failure to secure a signed BRA-2026 before substantive buyer services creates an uncompensated services risk and exposes the agent to HIREC discipline.** If a buyer later disputes the agent's claim to compensation, the absence of a written agreement eliminates the agent's contractual basis entirely.

Agents familiar with [buyer agency agreement requirements in Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) will notice Hawaii's version is notably stricter on timing—Wisconsin allows execution at any point before an offer, while Hawaii requires it before property tours.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Making Right Now

Three months into these changes, HIREC's complaint intake reveals recurring patterns. Here are the five most frequent errors showing up in enforcement files.

First, agents are using the old SRPD form. Many offices printed large batches in late 2025, and those forms are now non-compliant. The SRPD-2026 is the only accepted version, and using the prior form does not satisfy the seller's disclosure obligation under HRS 508D-4.

Second, agents are delivering the AD-2026 agency disclosure at the listing appointment or buyer consultation rather than at first substantive contact. If the agent spoke with the client by phone about property criteria or pricing before that meeting, the disclosure was already late.

Third, dual agency acknowledgments are being collected on a single signature page. The 2026 rules explicitly require separate pages for each party's consent. A single-page format—even if both parties sign—does not constitute valid informed consent under the revised HAR 16-99-3(d).

Fourth, buyer representation agreements are being executed at offer submission rather than before property tours. HIREC's guidance memorandum (GM-2026-02) clarifies that attending an open house at a buyer's request constitutes a substantive buyer service triggering the BRA requirement.

Fifth, agents are not documenting climate risk conversations. While the seller completes the SRPD disclosure, the listing agent has an advisory duty under HRS 508D-5 to inform the seller of the obligation. Agents who cannot prove they advised the seller—through a signed advisory letter or transaction management notes—may share liability if the disclosure is incomplete.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry supervisory liability under HRS 467-1.6 for their agents' compliance failures. HIREC's 2026 enforcement priorities specifically target brokerage-level deficiencies, and fines against a principal broker can reach $10,000 per transaction where systemic non-compliance is documented.

**Brokers should audit every active transaction file for the following items before the next HIREC compliance sweep, which HIREC announced will begin in Q4 2026.**

Start with form inventory. Every office should have physically destroyed or digitally archived (marked "DO NOT USE") all pre-2026 disclosure forms. The SRPD-2026, AD-2026, and BRA-2026 should be the only versions accessible in your document management system.

Next, review agency disclosure timing documentation. Each file should contain a notation—ideally timestamped—showing when first substantive contact occurred and when the AD-2026 was delivered. If your agents use a transaction management platform, configure it to flag files where the agency disclosure delivery date postdates initial client communication records.

| Audit Item | Compliant Standard | Common Deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| SRPD Form Version | SRPD-2026 only | Old form version in file |
| AD-2026 Delivery Timing | At or before first substantive contact | Delivered at listing appointment (too late) |
| BRA-2026 Execution | Before property tours or offer prep | Signed at offer submission |
| Dual Agency Consent | Separate pages per party, broker countersign within 48 hrs | Single page, no broker signature |
| Climate Advisory Documentation | Written advisory to seller on file | No documentation of advisory conversation |

Brokers should also confirm that all agents completed the mandatory 3-hour climate risk CE module. Under HRS 467-11.5, a principal broker who allows an agent to practice without current CE compliance may face independent disciplinary action.

## Penalties and Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

HIREC operates under a tiered enforcement model. First-time disclosure violations typically result in a letter of caution or consent agreement with a fine ranging from $500 to $2,500. Repeated violations, pattern conduct, or failures causing consumer harm escalate to formal hearings where license suspension or revocation is on the table.

> Under HRS 467-14(20), the commission may fine, suspend, or revoke any license for "any other conduct constituting fraudulent or dishonest dealings," which HIREC interprets broadly to include knowing use of outdated mandatory forms.

Civil liability runs parallel to regulatory consequences. A buyer who received a non-compliant SRPD retains rescission rights under HRS 508D-11 regardless of how far the transaction has progressed. Post-closing rescission claims based on deficient disclosures have survived summary judgment in Hawaii courts, meaning agents and their E&O carriers face real exposure.

Transaction cancellation is the most immediate practical risk. When a buyer's attorney identifies a disclosure deficiency, the standard remedy is rescission with return of earnest money. The listing agent's commission evaporates, and the seller re-enters the market with a stigmatized listing history.
