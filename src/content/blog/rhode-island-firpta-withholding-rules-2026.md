## Rhode Island FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Agent's Liability Playbook

**Failing to identify a foreign seller before closing is one of the fastest ways to drag your buyer—and yourself—into an IRS enforcement action.** The Rhode Island firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 carry real financial consequences that extend beyond the closing table and land squarely on the agents involved. This is not a niche issue reserved for luxury waterfront deals in Newport; it applies to any residential transaction where the seller qualifies as a "foreign person" under Internal Revenue Code Section 1445.

Rhode Island adds its own layer of nonresident income tax withholding on top of the federal FIRPTA requirement. Agents who treat these obligations as "the attorney's problem" expose their clients—and potentially their own licenses—to penalties that compound quickly.

## How FIRPTA Works at the Federal Level

The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sales price and remit it to the IRS. The mechanism is straightforward: the buyer is the "withholding agent" by default. If the buyer fails to withhold, the IRS can assess the full 15% against the buyer personally, plus interest and penalties.

The IRS defines "foreign person" broadly. It includes nonresident alien individuals, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates. A green card holder is generally treated as a U.S. person, but agents should never assume status based on a seller's accent, address, or how long they have lived in the state.

> "The transferee must deduct and withhold a tax equal to 15 percent of the amount realized on the disposition." — 26 U.S.C. § 1445(a)

The 15% rate applies when the sales price exceeds $1,000,000. For properties sold between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer intends to use the property as a residence, the rate drops to 10%. Sales at or below $300,000 to a buyer who will use the property as a residence are exempt from withholding entirely.

## Rhode Island's Nonresident Withholding Requirement

**Rhode Island imposes its own withholding obligation under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 44-30-71.3, which requires the buyer or closing agent to withhold 6% of the gain (or, if gain cannot be determined, 6% of the gross sales price) from any nonresident seller.** This is not a FIRPTA add-on; it is a separate state tax withholding that applies to all nonresident sellers, whether foreign or domestic.

The Rhode Island Division of Taxation administers this requirement. The withholding must be remitted using Rhode Island Form RI-1040NR-WH (Nonresident Real Estate Withholding). The form is filed with payment within 30 days of closing.

A foreign seller will almost always trigger both the federal FIRPTA withholding and the Rhode Island nonresident withholding simultaneously. Agents must confirm that the closing attorney or title company is addressing both layers, not just one.

| Requirement | Federal FIRPTA | Rhode Island State |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory Basis | IRC § 1445 | R.I. Gen. Laws § 44-30-71.3 |
| Withholding Rate | 15% of gross price (standard) | 6% of gain or gross price |
| Who Withholds | Buyer (transferee) | Buyer or closing agent |
| Filing Form | IRS Form 8288 + 8288-A | RI Form RI-1040NR-WH |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after closing | 30 days after closing |
| Exemption Certificate | IRS Form 8288-B | RI Form RI-1040C-ES (estimated) |

## Identifying Foreign Sellers: The Agent's Front-Line Role

The listing agent is typically the first person in the transaction to know—or to miss—a seller's foreign status. Rhode Island does not have a state-mandated form that forces agents to ask about citizenship, but the practical reality is that the IRS holds the buyer's side responsible for collecting a FIRPTA affidavit (also called a non-foreign affidavit or Certificate of Non-Foreign Status).

This affidavit, structured around the requirements of Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-2(b), must include the seller's taxpayer identification number, U.S. address, and a statement under penalty of perjury that the seller is not a foreign person. If the seller refuses to sign, the buyer must withhold. If the seller provides a false certificate, the seller bears liability—but the buyer's agent should still flag the risk.

**Agents working with [wire instructions and closing procedures](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) should build foreign-person verification into their pre-closing checklist.** It costs nothing to ask early and can save six figures in withheld taxes or penalties later.

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

The IRS does not directly fine real estate agents for FIRPTA violations—it goes after the buyer first. However, under IRC Section 1445(d), the "transferee's agent" (meaning the buyer's agent or the closing agent who facilitates the transaction) can be held liable for the withholding amount if they had actual knowledge that the FIRPTA affidavit was false or that withholding was required and was not collected.

Here is the chain of risk for a Rhode Island transaction:

The buyer is assessed 15% of the gross sales price plus interest. If the buyer cannot pay, the IRS pursues the transferee's agent. The agent's liability is capped at the amount of the commission received, but the legal costs and reputational damage are uncapped. Rhode Island's Department of Business Regulation, which oversees the [Rhode Island Real Estate Commission](https://dbr.ri.gov/divisions/real-estate/), can open a disciplinary investigation if an agent's negligence causes financial harm to a client.

On the state side, failure to remit the Rhode Island 6% withholding results in penalties under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 44-30-84, including a 10% late-payment penalty and interest accruing from the closing date. The Division of Taxation can also assess the unpaid amount against the closing agent personally.

## Common Mistakes Rhode Island Agents Make

**The most frequent error is assuming that foreign person status is obvious or that the closing attorney will catch it without agent input.** Rhode Island is an attorney-state for closings, which gives agents a false sense of security. Attorneys handle the mechanics, but they rely on information the agent surfaces during listing intake.

The first common mistake is failing to ask about citizenship or tax residency status at the listing appointment. Many agents treat it as an awkward question and skip it entirely. By the time the issue surfaces at closing, the timeline for filing a withholding certificate (Form 8288-B) to reduce the rate may have already passed—that application can take 90 days or more for IRS processing.

The second mistake is confusing residency with citizenship. A seller who has lived in Providence for a decade on a work visa may still be a nonresident alien for FIRPTA purposes. Residency for state income tax and "foreign person" status under federal tax law are two different determinations.

The third mistake is neglecting the state-level withholding when a federal FIRPTA exemption applies. A sale under $300,000 to a buyer who will use the property as a residence may be exempt from federal withholding, but Rhode Island's 6% nonresident withholding still applies if the seller is a nonresident of Rhode Island.

The fourth mistake is relying on an expired or improperly formatted non-foreign affidavit. The affidavit must be signed, must include a valid TIN, and must be retained by the buyer for five years. Agents sometimes accept a photocopy from a prior transaction or an affidavit that omits the TIN field.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents on foreign-seller transactions need a documented compliance protocol. The Rhode Island Real Estate Commission expects brokers to maintain supervisory oversight under R.I. Gen. Laws Section 5-20.5-11, which outlines responsible broker duties including transaction file review.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake form | Citizenship/residency question included | No question asked or field left blank |
| Non-foreign affidavit | Signed, dated, TIN present | Missing TIN or unsigned |
| Dual withholding tracking | Both IRS Form 8288 and RI-1040NR-WH referenced | Only one layer addressed |
| Timeline documentation | Withholding certificate application date if applicable | Filed fewer than 90 days before closing |
| Closing statement review | Withholding amounts deducted correctly | Amounts missing or miscalculated |

Brokers should require agents to note in the transaction file whether the seller has confirmed U.S. person status. This documentation protects the brokerage if the IRS later challenges the transaction. It also demonstrates good faith, which can mitigate penalties under the "actual knowledge" standard in IRC Section 1445(d).

Review your transaction management workflow to confirm that foreign-seller flags are triggered automatically rather than relying on agent memory. ## Reduced Withholding and Exemptions Available to Foreign Sellers

Foreign sellers are not stuck with the full 15% if their actual tax liability will be lower. IRS Form 8288-B allows the seller (or buyer) to apply for a withholding certificate that reduces or eliminates the withholding. The application must demonstrate the expected tax on the gain and propose an alternative withholding amount.

The challenge is timing. The IRS can take 90 days or longer to process a withholding certificate application. If closing occurs before the certificate is issued, the full amount must still be withheld and deposited with the IRS using Form 8288. The seller can then claim a refund when filing their U.S. tax return.

Rhode Island offers a parallel option. If the seller can demonstrate that their Rhode Island tax liability on the transaction will be less than 6% of the gross price, they can request a reduced withholding by filing an estimated payment with the Division of Taxation and providing documentation to the closing agent. This is less formalized than the federal process but is available under the Division's administrative guidance.
