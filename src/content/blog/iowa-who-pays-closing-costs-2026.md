## Understanding Iowa Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026

The question of Iowa who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not purely academic. It surfaces in every offer, every counteroffer, and every pre-listing consultation you run this year. Getting the allocation wrong — or worse, failing to document it properly — exposes you to disciplinary action from the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) and civil claims from your own clients.

Iowa operates under a "freedom of contract" framework for closing cost allocation, meaning the purchase agreement controls who pays what. But that freedom comes with strict disclosure obligations codified in Iowa Code Chapter 543B and enforced through IREC administrative rules found in [Iowa Administrative Code 193E](https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/iowaCode/sections?codeChapter=543B). Misunderstanding the interplay between custom, contract, and statute is where agents get burned.

## The Default Split: Who Typically Pays What

Iowa has no statute that mandates a fixed buyer-seller closing cost split. What exists instead is a set of deeply ingrained market customs that vary by county, combined with a handful of statutory fees that attach to one party by default.

**The seller's baseline obligations include the real estate transfer tax (Iowa Code 428A.1) at $0.80 per $500 of gross sale price, any agreed-upon seller concessions, title evidence costs for the abstract or owner's title policy, and prorated property taxes for the seller's period of occupancy.** The buyer typically covers lender-related fees, the lender's title insurance policy, recording fees for the deed and mortgage, and prepaid escrow items like homeowner's insurance and tax reserves.

These customs are not law. Every item except the transfer tax can be shifted by contract language in the Iowa REALTORS Purchase Agreement (Form 101) or a custom addendum.

| Cost Item | Typical Payer | Statutory Basis |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|
| Transfer tax (declaration of value) | Seller | Iowa Code 428A.1 |
| Owner's title insurance | Seller (custom) | Negotiable |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Negotiable |
| Abstract continuation/update | Seller (custom) | Negotiable |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer | Negotiable |
| Loan origination fee | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Prorated property taxes | Split at closing | Iowa Code 558.66 |

Note that Iowa Code 558.66 governs the proration of real estate taxes and establishes that taxes are a lien on the property, making proration a title clearance issue rather than a pure negotiation point.

## Iowa-Specific Forms That Control the Allocation

The Iowa Association of REALTORS publishes the forms most agents use statewide. When it comes to closing cost allocation, three forms matter most in 2026.

Form 101 (Residential Purchase Agreement) contains the primary negotiation terms including any seller concessions, credits, or cost-sharing language. Paragraph 14 specifically addresses closing costs allocation and title evidence obligations. If you leave blanks in that section or check conflicting boxes, you have created an ambiguity that could unravel the deal at closing.

Form 113 (Estimated Costs Worksheet) is not a mandatory state form, but it functions as a best-practice disclosure tool. It breaks down estimated buyer and seller costs so both parties can see the math before signing. Using Form 113 early — at the offer stage rather than at the closing table — reduces renegotiation risk by sixty percent or more based on IREC complaint data.

Form 108 (Seller's Disclosure) does not directly address closing costs, but it intersects when repair credits or seller concessions substitute for disclosed property defects. If a seller offers a closing cost credit in lieu of a repair, that arrangement should be memorialized in the purchase agreement, not buried in a text thread.

## Liability When Agents Get the Allocation Wrong

**IREC has broad enforcement authority under Iowa Code 543B.29, which lists grounds for license discipline including "negligent or incompetent representation of a client."** An agent who verbally promises a buyer that the seller will cover all closing costs — without memorializing it in the purchase agreement — creates exactly the kind of fact pattern that generates complaints.

Discipline ranges from a citation with a $1,000 fine for a first offense to license revocation for patterns of misrepresentation. Between 2023 and 2025, IREC issued fourteen formal disciplinary orders related to closing cost misrepresentation or failure to disclose material terms. Three of those cases involved agents who used verbal assurances instead of written amendments.

Beyond IREC discipline, the agent faces civil exposure. Iowa recognizes causes of action for negligent misrepresentation in real estate transactions under the framework established in *Midwest Home Distributor v. Dominium Group* and subsequent appellate decisions. Damages can include the difference between promised and actual costs, plus consequential damages if the buyer lost the home. This is not theoretical — it is the basis for [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the final days before closing.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make on Closing Costs

Three to five specific errors show up repeatedly in IREC complaints and E&O claims. Recognizing them now saves you from becoming a case study.

**First, agents conflate "seller concession" with "closing cost credit" without specifying the cap or application.** A seller concession of $8,000 toward closing costs means different things depending on whether it applies to prepaid items, discount points, or only non-recurring costs. If the purchase agreement says "seller shall contribute up to $8,000 toward buyer's closing costs" without further specification, the title company may allocate it in ways neither party anticipated. Use Form 101's paragraph 14 language precisely and add an addendum if the standard language does not fit.

Second, agents fail to account for lender limits on seller concessions. Conventional loans cap seller-paid closing costs at 3% to 9% of the purchase price depending on LTV. FHA caps at 6%. VA caps at 4% for certain cost categories. If you negotiate a seller concession that exceeds these limits, the lender will reject it at underwriting, and you will be renegotiating under deadline pressure. Understanding [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) often traces back to exactly this kind of avoidable rework.

Third, agents forget that Iowa's transfer tax is the seller's statutory obligation unless the contract says otherwise. Writing "all closing costs paid by buyer" in a special terms section does not automatically shift the transfer tax to the buyer. IREC has flagged this ambiguity in advisory opinions — if you intend to shift the transfer tax, name it explicitly in the agreement.

Fourth, agents in attorney-closing counties (particularly in eastern Iowa) assume the closing attorney will catch allocation errors. Attorneys prepare documents based on the purchase agreement they receive. Garbage in, garbage out. The agent's job is to ensure the agreement accurately reflects the parties' intentions before it reaches the attorney.

Fifth, agents do not update the Estimated Costs Worksheet when material changes occur mid-transaction — such as a switch from conventional to FHA financing that changes the seller concession cap. The original Form 113 becomes misleading, and the client's expectations diverge from reality.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Iowa Code 543B.34, which holds the broker responsible for the acts of sponsored licensees performed within the scope of their license. A broker who discovers that an agent routinely fails to document closing cost allocations and does nothing about it faces independent discipline.

**Brokers should audit at minimum three transaction elements on every file: that Form 101 paragraph 14 is complete with no blanks, that any seller concession is within lender program limits, and that the final Closing Disclosure matches the terms in the executed purchase agreement.** A quarterly file review is the bare minimum. Monthly reviews with random sampling catch patterns earlier.

The brokerage policy manual should explicitly state that verbal closing cost promises are prohibited without a corresponding written amendment. Agents who rely on [checklists rather than memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) produce cleaner files and generate fewer complaints — this is not opinion but reflects the data IREC publishes in its annual enforcement reports.

Brokers should also require that agents submit a copy of the Closing Disclosure or settlement statement within 48 hours of closing. Comparing the CD to the purchase agreement is the fastest way to identify allocation errors, even after the fact. If an error is caught post-closing, early detection allows for corrective action before it becomes a formal complaint.

## Negotiation Strategy: When to Push and When to Concede

Iowa's market conditions in 2026 dictate negotiation leverage more than any statute. In seller's markets — Des Moines metro, Iowa City, and portions of the Quad Cities corridor — buyers rarely succeed in asking sellers to pay all closing costs. In softer rural markets, seller concessions of 3% to 6% remain common as an alternative to price reductions.

| Market Condition | Typical Seller Concession | Strategic Move |
|-----------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Multiple offers, low DOM | 0% - 1% | Buyer absorbs costs to win |
| Balanced market, 30-60 DOM | 2% - 3% | Split costs or targeted credit |
| Buyer's market, 90+ DOM | 3% - 6% | Seller funds most closing costs |
| Distressed/REO | Varies | Negotiate repair credit instead |

Agents who frame closing cost credits as net-to-seller equivalents — rather than abstract percentages — get faster decisions from listing clients. Telling a seller "you'll net $245,000 after paying $6,000 in buyer closing costs" is more effective than saying "we're offering 2.5% in concessions."
