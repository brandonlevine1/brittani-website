# New Hampshire Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026: What Every Agent Must Know

Getting the numbers wrong on a closing disclosure is one of the fastest ways to delay a deal or lose a client's confidence. The New Hampshire transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 carry specific statutory rates, form requirements, and penalties that agents cannot afford to guess at. This post breaks down the current rate structure, the forms you must file, and the consequences of getting it wrong.

## The Statutory Framework Behind the Transfer Tax

New Hampshire's real estate transfer tax is codified under RSA Chapter 78-B. The tax applies to every purchase, grant, or transfer of real estate or an interest in real estate within the state. The New Hampshire Department of Revenue Administration (DRA) administers the tax, but collection responsibility falls on the parties involved—and by extension, the agents and closing professionals guiding them.

**The 2026 rate remains $7.50 per $1,000 of the price or consideration, owed by each party.** That means on a $400,000 sale, the buyer owes $3,000 and the seller owes $3,000, for a combined transfer tax of $6,000. The rate has held steady since July 2023, when the legislature reduced it from $7.50 (which itself was a reduction from the prior $15.00 per $1,000 combined rate).

RSA 78-B:2 specifies that the tax attaches at the time of transfer—not at contract execution, not at inspection, but at the moment the deed is delivered or recorded. Agents who quote transfer tax amounts early in a transaction should always disclaim that the rate is as of the recording date, not the listing date.

## Recording Fees at New Hampshire Registries of Deeds

Recording fees in New Hampshire are set by RSA 478:17-g and are uniform across all ten counties. Unlike the transfer tax, these are administrative charges levied by the county register of deeds for the act of entering a document into the public record.

| Document Type | First Page | Each Additional Page | LCHIP Surcharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard deed (8.5 x 14 or smaller) | $15.00 | $4.00 | $2.00 per document |
| Non-standard size document | $15.00 + $2.00 | $4.00 | $2.00 per document |
| Discharge of mortgage | $15.00 | $4.00 | $2.00 per document |
| Plan or plat (per sheet) | $15.00 | $4.00 | $2.00 per document |

The LCHIP (Land and Community Heritage Investment Program) surcharge was established under RSA 227-M:9 and applies to every document recorded. It is not optional and cannot be waived by the register.

For a typical residential closing, you will record at minimum a deed and a mortgage, so the baseline recording cost is at least $34.00 before additional pages. Multi-page mortgages from institutional lenders routinely run 30-50 pages, which can push mortgage recording fees alone past $130.00. Agents should account for this in their preliminary net sheets and [understand how these numbers interact with who pays what at closing](/blog/wisconsin-who-pays-closing-costs-2026).

## Required Forms and Filing Procedures

The DRA requires that every transfer of real estate be accompanied by a **Declaration of Consideration**, filed on Form CD-57-P. This form captures the sale price, the identities of grantor and grantee, and the calculation of transfer tax due. Without it, the county register of deeds will refuse to record the deed.

Form CD-57-P must be completed in duplicate. One copy accompanies the deed to the registry, and the other is sent directly to the DRA. Many closing attorneys handle this filing, but agents need to verify its completion—especially in FSBO deals or transactions without title company involvement.

For transactions claiming an exemption under RSA 78-B:2, I (such as transfers between spouses incident to divorce, or transfers to revocable trusts), the CD-57-P must still be filed with the exemption box checked and the statutory basis cited. Skipping the form because "no tax is due" is a recording-stopping error.

Additionally, RSA 78-B:10 requires that the tax payment accompany the deed at the time of recording. The register will not accept a deed with a promise to pay later. Payment is typically made by the closing attorney via a single check combining buyer and seller shares, though separate checks are acceptable.

## New Hampshire Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Mistakes in this area rarely trigger malpractice claims, but they reliably cause closing delays, client frustration, and broker complaints. Here are the errors that surface most often.

**First, agents miscalculate the tax by applying the rate once instead of twice.** The $7.50 per $1,000 applies to each party independently. A $500,000 sale generates $3,750 from the buyer and $3,750 from the seller—not $3,750 total. Preliminary net sheets that show only one party's share mislead clients about their true costs.

Second, agents assume the transfer tax split is fixed by statute with no room for negotiation. While RSA 78-B:2 imposes liability on both parties, nothing prevents a contractual agreement shifting the full burden to one side. In competitive markets, sellers sometimes agree to pay the buyer's share. The key is getting that agreement in writing within the purchase and sale agreement—not leaving it as a verbal understanding that unravels at closing.

Third, agents fail to flag exempt transactions early, causing last-minute scrambles for documentation. Transfers to LLCs, interfamily conveyances, and trust-related transfers may qualify for exemptions, but only if properly documented on Form CD-57-P with supporting materials ready for the registry.

Fourth, recording fee estimates omit page counts. A 40-page mortgage at $15.00 for the first page and $4.00 for each additional page totals $171.00 plus the $2.00 LCHIP surcharge. Agents who estimate "$15 for the mortgage" on a buyer's cost sheet create a trust problem at closing.

Fifth, agents confuse the transfer tax with capital gains obligations or [FIRPTA withholding requirements](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026), which are separate federal obligations that layer on top of state-level transfer taxes.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong

RSA 78-B:7 authorizes the DRA to impose a penalty of 5% of the tax due per month of delinquency, up to 25%. Interest accrues on top of penalties at the rate prescribed under RSA 21-J:28. For a $400,000 transaction, that is $150 per month in penalties for each party's unpaid share.

Beyond DRA penalties, a deed that cannot be recorded because of a missing CD-57-P or insufficient payment leaves the buyer without record title. This exposes the buyer to third-party claims, title insurance complications, and potential lender acceleration of the mortgage. The agent who failed to coordinate the paperwork may face a complaint to the [New Hampshire Real Estate Commission](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) under its authority to discipline licensees for negligence or incompetence.

Deal cancellation is also possible. If a closing cannot proceed because transfer tax funds were not properly collected or allocated, the purchase and sale agreement's closing date may pass without performance. Depending on contract terms, the non-breaching party may have grounds to terminate—and the agent's errors become the proximate cause.

None of this is theoretical. The NHREC has disciplined licensees for failing to adequately advise clients about transaction costs, and the commission's authority under RSA 331-A extends to any conduct demonstrating unworthiness to act as a licensee.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under RSA 331-A:25-b for the acts of their affiliated licensees. A pattern of transfer tax miscalculations or recording fee errors across a brokerage reflects directly on the broker's license.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Net sheet accuracy | Transfer tax calculated per party at $7.50/$1,000 | Every transaction |
| Form CD-57-P completion | Filed in duplicate, exemption basis cited if applicable | Every transaction |
| Recording fee estimates | Page-count-based calculation, LCHIP included | Every transaction |
| Contractual tax allocation | Written agreement in P&S if non-default split | As applicable |
| Closing disclosure reconciliation | Final CD matches preliminary estimates within tolerance | Every transaction |

Brokers should maintain a compliance checklist that agents must complete before submitting a file to the closing attorney or title company. This is not about micromanagement—it is about catching the $3,750 miscalculation before it hits the closing table and erodes client trust.

Training should occur at least annually, ideally timed to any legislative session that could adjust the rate. ## Exemptions That Agents Must Understand

Not every transfer triggers a tax obligation. RSA 78-B:2 enumerates specific exemptions, including transfers where the price or consideration is $4,000 or less, transfers between spouses, transfers by devise or inheritance, and transfers to governmental entities. Each exemption still requires a filed CD-57-P—the exemption does not eliminate the filing obligation, only the payment obligation.

The LLC trap catches agents regularly. When a seller transfers property to their own LLC (or vice versa), many assume it is exempt as a transfer without consideration. However, if the LLC has other members or if debt is assumed, the DRA may assess the transfer at the value of the encumbrance transferred. Agents should advise clients to consult with their attorney before assuming any transfer qualifies.

Similarly, transfers incident to divorce are exempt only if they occur pursuant to a court order. A voluntary transfer between divorcing spouses that precedes the court decree does not automatically qualify, and the DRA has assessed taxes on such transfers retroactively.
