## Understanding Maryland Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Maryland permits dual agency, but the state wraps it in disclosure requirements that trip up even experienced licensees. If you hold a Maryland license and have ever represented both sides of a deal—or work in a brokerage where two agents might end up on opposite sides of the same transaction—**Maryland dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** demand your attention. The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) enforces these rules under the Business Occupations & Professions Article, Title 17, Subtitle 5 of the Maryland Code, and violations carry real financial and licensing consequences.

The rules are not new, but the enforcement environment has tightened. MREC audits have increased since 2024, and the commission has publicly stated that agency disclosure failures remain its top complaint category. Understanding the distinction between dual agency and designated (intra-company) agency—and documenting each correctly—is now a baseline expectation, not a best practice.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: The Legal Distinction

**Dual agency in Maryland occurs when a single licensee represents both the buyer and seller in the same transaction.** The agent owes fiduciary duties to both parties, which immediately creates an inherent conflict of interest. Maryland law acknowledges this conflict and permits it only with informed, written consent from both parties.

Designated agency—sometimes called intra-company agency—is a different structure. Here, the broker assigns one licensee in the firm to represent the seller and a different licensee to represent the buyer. Each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties to their respective client, and the supervising broker acts as a neutral party managing confidential information barriers within the office.

The practical difference matters enormously in litigation. A dual agent cannot advocate for either party's price position or disclose either party's motivation. A designated agent can advocate fully for their assigned client, provided the brokerage has proper information barriers in place.

| Feature | Dual Agency | Designated (Intra-Company) Agency |
|---------|-------------|-----------------------------------|
| Number of agents involved | One licensee, both sides | Two separate licensees, same brokerage |
| Fiduciary duty scope | Limited to both parties equally | Full to assigned client |
| Advocacy permitted | No price/motivation advocacy for either side | Full advocacy for assigned client |
| Broker's role | Supervising dual agent | Neutral supervisor managing information walls |
| Written consent required | Yes, from both parties | Yes, from both parties |
| Risk level for licensee | Higher malpractice exposure | Lower, if documented correctly |

## Required Forms and Timing

Maryland agents must present the **Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent** disclosure form at the first scheduled meeting or, if no meeting is scheduled, at the first substantive discussion about a specific property. This is not optional. It is codified under COMAR 09.11.02.05 and enforced through MREC disciplinary proceedings.

When dual agency arises mid-transaction (such as when a listing agent's own buyer becomes interested), the agent must obtain the **Consent for Dual Agency** form signed by both parties before any further substantive negotiations. The consent form must clearly state that the agent will not be able to advocate exclusively for either party and that both parties waive the right to undivided loyalty.

For designated agency arrangements, the brokerage must maintain internal documentation showing which licensee is assigned to which client. MREC does not prescribe a specific form number for internal designation, but the commission expects written records that can be produced during an audit. Many brokerages use their own designation assignment memo alongside the standard disclosure forms.

The timing requirement is the piece that generates the most complaints. Presenting the disclosure form after a buyer has already toured three properties with you—or after a listing presentation has concluded—violates the regulation, even if the client ultimately signs.

## Statutory Authority and MREC Enforcement

The statutory backbone for these requirements lives in [Maryland Code, Business Occupations & Professions, Title 17](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=gbo&section=17-530&enession=true). Section 17-530 specifically addresses dual agency consent. Section 17-532 outlines the duties owed in intra-company agency. COMAR 09.11.02 provides the implementing regulations, including form requirements and timing mandates.

MREC has the authority to impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, require additional education, and issue public reprimands. Civil courts can separately award damages to harmed clients, and contract rescission is available as a remedy when a party can demonstrate that consent was not properly obtained.

**The commission processes roughly 400-500 complaints annually, and agency disclosure violations consistently rank in the top three categories.** This is not a theoretical risk. Agents who treat disclosure as a formality rather than a legal requirement are the ones appearing before the commission.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make

**First: Presenting the disclosure form too late.** The most frequent violation MREC encounters is an agent who has multiple substantive conversations with a prospective buyer—discussing financing, showing properties, even discussing offer strategy—before ever presenting the Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form. The regulation requires disclosure at first substantive contact, not at the point of writing an offer. Agents who work open houses are particularly vulnerable here, because a casual conversation can become substantive quickly.

Second: Assuming that because two agents in the same office are involved, no dual agency exists. Intra-company agency still requires disclosure and consent. The fact that two different licensees are handling buyer and seller does not eliminate the need for written documentation. The brokerage must formally designate each agent and both clients must consent to the arrangement.

Third: Failing to re-disclose when the agency relationship changes mid-transaction. An agent who starts as a buyer's agent but becomes a dual agent when the buyer wants to purchase the agent's own listing must obtain fresh consent at the point of change. The original disclosure form from the initial relationship is insufficient.

Fourth: Confusing ministerial acts with representation. Maryland law allows agents to perform ministerial acts (providing property information, scheduling showings, writing up offers at a party's direction) without establishing an agency relationship. But agents often slide from ministerial service into substantive advice without recognizing the transition—and without disclosing the implied agency relationship that has formed.

Fifth: Relying on verbal consent for dual agency. Maryland requires written consent. A recorded phone call, a text message, or a verbal "sure, that's fine" does not satisfy the statute. If it is not on paper with a wet or electronic signature, it does not exist for compliance purposes.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability for their agents' disclosure failures. Under Section 17-322 of the Business Occupations & Professions Article, a broker who knew or should have known about an agent's noncompliance can face separate disciplinary action. This is not vicarious liability in the traditional sense—it is a direct duty to supervise.

**Every managing broker in Maryland should audit three things quarterly at minimum.** First, verify that the Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form appears in every transaction file with a date that precedes or matches the date of the first showing, CMA presentation, or buyer consultation. Second, confirm that any transaction involving two agents within the firm has a designated agency memo on file and both clients' written consent. Third, review any transaction where the listing agent also represented the buyer to confirm dual agency consent was obtained before offer submission.

| Audit Item | What to Look For | Red Flag |
|------------|------------------|----------|
| Disclosure form timing | Date on form vs. date of first contact/showing | Form dated after first showing or offer |
| Dual agency consent | Separate signed consent from both buyer and seller | Missing signature, unsigned form, or consent dated after ratification |
| Designated agency memo | Internal written designation naming each agent | No documentation, or memo created after closing |
| Mid-transaction change | Fresh disclosure/consent when relationship shifts | Only original disclosure on file despite role change |
| Open house follow-up | Disclosure provided before substantive conversations continue | No form on file for buyer who first appeared at open house |

Brokers who discover gaps should not simply backdate forms. That creates a separate fraud problem. The correct approach is to document the gap, obtain proper consent immediately, and determine whether the transaction can proceed or whether the affected party needs to be informed of their right to seek independent representation.

## Parallels and Differences With Neighboring States

Maryland's approach to dual agency sits between the permissive and prohibitive ends of the spectrum. Some states have banned dual agency outright, while others allow it with minimal disclosure. Maryland permits both dual agency and designated agency but demands rigorous written consent and imposes meaningful penalties for noncompliance.

## Protecting Yourself Under Maryland Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The agents who avoid MREC complaints are the ones who treat disclosure as a first-contact habit rather than a transactional checkbox. Keep blank copies of the Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent form in your car, your open house kit, and your digital signing platform. Present it early, explain it clearly, and document the date of presentation independently of the client's signature date.

For transactions involving **Maryland dual agency & designated agency rules 2026**, the standard of care is straightforward: disclose early, obtain written consent before negotiations begin, document every role change, and ensure your broker has visibility into the file. The agents who end up before the commission are almost never those who made a good-faith effort and fell slightly short—they are the ones who skipped the step entirely or treated disclosure as an afterthought. Build the habit, audit the files, and keep your license intact.
