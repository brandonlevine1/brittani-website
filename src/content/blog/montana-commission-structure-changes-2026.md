## Montana Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026: The Ground Has Shifted

The Montana commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have fundamentally changed how agents earn, disclose, and negotiate their compensation. If you are still operating under pre-settlement habits—assuming the listing side handles everything or that verbal agreements suffice—you are exposed to regulatory action, civil liability, and deal collapse. Montana's Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Department of Labor and Industry, has aligned its enforcement posture with the new federal settlement terms, and the grace period for ignorance is over.

The settlement, finalized in 2024, eliminated cooperative compensation offers through MLS systems and required written buyer representation agreements before any property touring. Montana agents working under the Montana Association of REALTORS (MAR) forms and the statewide MLS must now treat commission negotiations as standalone, transparent events documented with specific paperwork.

## How the Settlement Changed Montana's Commission Framework

**Before August 2024, a listing agent in Montana could input a blanket offer of buyer-agent compensation directly into the MLS.** That field no longer exists. The structural change means buyer-agent compensation must be negotiated between the buyer and their agent, or separately between the buyer's agent and the seller through off-MLS channels.

Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Title 37, Chapter 51 governs real estate licensee conduct. The [Montana Board of Realty Regulation](https://boards.bsd.dli.mt.gov/realty-regulation) has issued guidance confirming that the settlement terms apply to all licensees participating in NAR-affiliated MLS systems, which covers the vast majority of practicing agents in the state. The Board has not enacted new statutes specific to the settlement, but existing rules under MCA 37-51-321 (grounds for disciplinary action) and MCA 37-51-313 (broker supervisory duties) now encompass settlement compliance failures.

The MAR has updated its standard forms to reflect these requirements. Agents must now use the MAR Buyer Broker Compensation Agreement (Form 120-R, revised 2025) alongside the existing Buyer Broker Exclusive Employment Agreement (Form 102). The compensation terms must be specific—no open-ended percentages tied to "whatever the listing side offers."

## Written Buyer Agreements: What Montana Law and the Settlement Demand

Every buyer's agent in Montana must secure a signed written agreement before showing a property. This is not optional. The agreement must include three non-negotiable elements: the specific amount or rate of compensation, a clear statement of services provided, and a defined term with an expiration date.

Form 102, the MAR Buyer Broker Exclusive Employment Agreement, satisfies the written agreement requirement when properly completed. The revised version includes fields for fixed-fee compensation, percentage-based compensation, or hourly arrangements. Agents must fill in an actual number—leaving the compensation field blank or writing "TBD" violates both settlement terms and Board expectations.

**The agreement must be signed before the first showing, not at offer time.** Agents who tour properties with unrepresented buyers and then attempt to retroactively formalize the relationship are creating liability for themselves and their brokers. The Board has clarified in its 2025 guidance memo that "material service" begins at the first showing, and compensation terms must be locked before that point.

## Seller-Side Changes: What Listing Agents Need to Document

Listing agents face their own compliance obligations. The seller's listing agreement (MAR Form 101, Exclusive Right to Sell) must clearly state what, if any, compensation the seller is willing to offer to a buyer's agent. This offer cannot appear on the MLS but can be communicated through agent-to-agent negotiation, seller concession terms at offer, or separate written agreements.

| Document | Purpose | Who Signs | When Required |
|----------|---------|-----------|---------------|
| MAR Form 101 (Listing Agreement) | Establishes listing terms and any seller-offered buyer-agent compensation | Seller and listing agent | Before marketing |
| MAR Form 102 (Buyer Broker Agreement) | Establishes buyer-agent compensation and services | Buyer and buyer's agent | Before first showing |
| MAR Form 120-R (Compensation Agreement) | Documents separate compensation arrangement between buyer's agent and seller | Buyer's agent and seller (or listing agent on behalf) | Before or at offer |
| Disclosure of Compensation (integrated into Form 102) | Confirms buyer understands payment responsibility | Buyer | At agreement signing |

If a seller declines to offer any buyer-agent compensation, the buyer's agent must look solely to their client for payment unless other arrangements are documented. Listing agents should not promise compensation to cooperating agents without written seller authorization captured in the listing agreement.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Are Making Right Now

The transition has exposed several recurring errors that risk disciplinary action, deal failure, or malpractice claims. Here are five specific mistakes showing up in Montana transactions.

**First, agents are using outdated forms.** The pre-2025 version of Form 102 does not contain the required specificity fields for compensation terms mandated by the settlement. Using the old version creates a defective agreement that may not be enforceable and will not satisfy Board scrutiny during an audit or complaint investigation.

Second, some buyer's agents are still relying on verbal commission agreements and delaying paperwork until the offer stage. Under the settlement rules and MCA 37-51-321(1)(a), failing to reduce material terms to writing before providing services constitutes a potential violation. The Board can impose fines up to $1,000 per occurrence and suspend or revoke licenses for pattern violations.

Third, listing agents are embedding commission offers in MLS remarks fields or supplements rather than the now-removed compensation field. This violates MLS rules and exposes the agent to settlement non-compliance, which can result in loss of MLS access and NAR membership suspension. The intent of the settlement was to remove all compensation offers from MLS data—creative workarounds defeat that purpose and invite enforcement.

Fourth, agents are failing to explain to buyers that commission is negotiable. The settlement specifically requires that buyer agreements not state compensation is set by law or standard practice. Montana agents who present their rate as non-negotiable or industry-standard without explaining the buyer's right to negotiate are violating disclosure obligations under ARM 24.210.641 (Administrative Rules of Montana governing licensee conduct).

Fifth, dual agency situations are creating undisclosed compensation conflicts. Montana permits consensual dual agency under MCA 37-51-102(6), but agents acting as dual agents must now disclose how compensation flows from both sides in writing. The revised Consent to Dual Agency form (MAR Form 103) includes a compensation disclosure section that many agents are leaving incomplete.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in 2026

Supervising brokers carry the heaviest compliance burden. Under MCA 37-51-313, the designated broker is responsible for all licensee conduct within the brokerage. A single agent's failure to secure a proper buyer agreement or disclose compensation terms can trigger Board investigation of the entire office.

Brokers should audit for these specific items on every transaction file: presence of a signed Form 102 dated before the first showing date, completion of all compensation fields with specific dollar amounts or percentages, absence of any MLS-based compensation language in listing files, and proper execution of Form 120-R when seller-funded buyer-agent compensation exists. Missing any of these creates exposure.

| Audit Item | Where to Check | Red Flag |
|------------|---------------|----------|
| Buyer agreement timing | Form 102 date vs. first showing date in showing service records | Agreement dated same day or after first showing |
| Compensation specificity | Form 102, Section 4 | Blank fields, "TBD," or ranges instead of fixed amounts |
| MLS compliance | Active listing remarks and supplements | Any language referencing buyer-agent compensation |
| Dual agency disclosure | Form 103, compensation section | Incomplete or unsigned compensation disclosure |
| Seller authorization | Form 101, compensation section | Buyer-agent compensation offered without seller signature |

Brokers who discover non-compliance after closing face the uncomfortable choice of self-reporting to the Board or hoping the deficiency goes undetected. The safer path is proactive file review at three stages: listing or buyer agreement execution, offer submission, and closing. ## Penalties and Liability: What Failure Looks Like

The consequences of non-compliance operate on multiple levels. At the regulatory level, the Montana Board of Realty Regulation can impose fines, require additional education, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely under MCA 37-51-321. Pattern violations or willful disregard trigger the harshest responses.

At the transaction level, a defective buyer agreement may render the agent's compensation claim unenforceable. If a buyer refuses to pay a commission that was never properly documented, the agent has no legal basis for collection. Montana courts have historically required clear written agreements for commission claims—the settlement has only heightened that standard.

**Civil liability extends beyond lost commissions.** A buyer who discovers post-closing that their agent received undisclosed compensation from the seller may bring a claim for breach of fiduciary duty. Under Montana's agency law framework in MCA 37-51-102 and the common law duty of loyalty, undisclosed financial arrangements between an agent and a party adverse to their client constitute a serious breach. Damages can include disgorgement of the commission plus consequential losses.

The risk also flows uphill to brokers. E&O carriers are updating their policy requirements to mandate settlement compliance documentation. Brokers who cannot demonstrate supervisory oversight of compensation agreements may find claims denied or premiums increased. Similar enforcement patterns are emerging in other states—[Wisconsin's commission structure changes](/blog/wisconsin-commission-structure-changes-2026) show how quickly regulators adapt once the framework shifts.
