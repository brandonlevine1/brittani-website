Selling real estate in South Dakota is straightforward until a foreign national shows up on the seller side of the transaction. At that point, every agent and broker in the deal needs a working understanding of South Dakota firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 — because the consequences of getting it wrong fall on the buyer, the closing agent, and potentially the licensees who failed to flag the issue. FIRPTA, the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, is a federal statute, but its practical impact lands squarely on the shoulders of agents managing the day-to-day mechanics of a closing.

South Dakota's lack of a state income tax makes it an attractive market for international investors. That same tax advantage, however, creates a false sense of simplicity around foreign seller transactions. Federal withholding obligations do not disappear just because Pierre has no state-level counterpart to enforce.

## How FIRPTA Works at the Federal Level

FIRPTA, codified under [26 U.S.C. Sections 1445 and 897](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1445), requires that when a foreign person disposes of a U.S. real property interest, the buyer must withhold 15% of the amount realized — typically the gross sale price — and remit it to the IRS. The withholding rate dropped from earlier thresholds but has held at 15% for amounts realized above $1 million since the PATH Act of 2015.

For properties sold at $300,000 or less where the buyer intends to use the property as a residence, the withholding rate is zero. Sales between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer will use the home as a primary residence carry a reduced 10% rate. Every other disposition of U.S. real property by a foreign person triggers the full 15%.

| Sale Price | Buyer's Intended Use | Withholding Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Personal residence | 0% |
| $300,001 – $1,000,000 | Personal residence | 10% |
| Over $1,000,000 | Any use | 15% |
| Any amount | Investment or rental | 15% |

The forms that drive this process are IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both must be filed within 20 days of the closing date.

## South Dakota firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026: The State-Level Twist

**South Dakota is one of the few states with no individual income tax, which means there is no state-level withholding overlay on top of FIRPTA.** Under South Dakota Codified Laws Title 10, the state does not impose an income tax on individuals, trusts, or estates. This is confirmed by the South Dakota Department of Revenue's published guidance, which makes no provision for a state withholding mechanism on real property dispositions.

That zero-state-tax status simplifies part of the equation but creates a dangerous blind spot. Agents in South Dakota never deal with state withholding certificates, so they are far less likely to have FIRPTA protocols baked into their transaction checklists. In states like California or Maryland, agents encounter state withholding forms routinely, which keeps federal FIRPTA awareness alive. South Dakota agents often go years without touching a foreign seller deal, and that gap in repetition breeds compliance failures.

The South Dakota Real Estate Commission, operating under ARSD 20:69 (Administrative Rules of South Dakota), requires licensees to act competently and in compliance with applicable law. While the Commission does not administer FIRPTA, a licensee who ignores or mishandles a known federal obligation could face disciplinary review for negligence or incompetence under SDCL 36-21A-71.

## Who Bears the Liability When Withholding Fails

**The buyer — not the seller — is the "withholding agent" under FIRPTA.** If the required withholding is not remitted to the IRS, the buyer is personally liable for the tax, plus interest and penalties. That liability can reach the full 15% of the amount realized, regardless of whether the buyer ever received those funds.

Here is where agents and brokers enter the picture. While a real estate licensee is not the statutory withholding agent, the IRS has held that transferees and their agents can be held responsible for failing to ensure compliance. Title companies and closing attorneys in South Dakota typically handle the mechanical withholding and remittance, but the listing agent who knows the seller is a foreign person and says nothing has created a liability gap that everyone at the closing table may end up filling.

Deal cancellation is another real risk. If a buyer discovers at closing that 15% of the purchase price must be withheld and nobody mentioned it earlier, the transaction can collapse. Buyers who planned their cash-to-close down to the dollar may not have funds to cover a withholding that effectively reduces the seller's net proceeds — and if the contract does not address FIRPTA, the parties may disagree on who absorbs the shortfall.

| Party | FIRPTA Role | Risk if Non-Compliant |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer (Transferee) | Statutory withholding agent | Full 15% liability, plus IRS penalties and interest |
| Seller (Foreign Person) | Subject to withholding | Potential IRS audit; reduced or delayed proceeds |
| Closing Agent / Title Co. | Facilitates remittance | Professional liability; potential IRS penalties |
| Listing Agent | Disclosure duty | Disciplinary action; malpractice exposure |
| Buyer's Agent | Duty to advise client | Client liability creates agent liability risk |

## Common Mistakes South Dakota Agents Make on Foreign Seller Transactions

The first mistake is assuming "foreign" means the seller lives overseas. Under FIRPTA, a "foreign person" includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates. A Canadian citizen who owns a hunting cabin near Aberdeen and visits every fall is a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes, even if they have a U.S. mailing address or a local bank account. Agents routinely fail to ask the right questions because the seller seems local.

The second mistake is relying on the title company to catch everything. South Dakota title companies are generally competent at handling FIRPTA when alerted, but they depend on accurate information from the agents. If the listing agent never mentions the seller's citizenship status — or worse, never asks — the title company may process the closing as a standard domestic transaction. The IRS does not accept "the title company should have caught it" as a defense.

**The third mistake is failing to address FIRPTA in the purchase agreement.** The South Dakota Association of REALTORS purchase agreement forms include provisions for seller representations, but agents frequently skip or gloss over the foreign person certification. Without a signed affidavit of non-foreign status (often modeled on the language in IRS regulations at 26 CFR 1.1445-2(b)), the buyer has no protection against future IRS claims.

The fourth mistake is confusing a withholding certificate application with an exemption. Sellers can apply for a reduced withholding amount using IRS Form 8288-B, but this must be filed before closing, and the IRS can take 90 days or more to process it. Agents who promise sellers that "we can reduce the withholding" without understanding the timeline create unrealistic expectations that can delay or derail closings.

The fifth mistake is not flagging the 20-day remittance deadline. Forms 8288 and 8288-A must reach the IRS within 20 days of the transfer date. Missing that deadline triggers penalties. Agents who hand off the file at closing and assume it is done are leaving their clients exposed.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in South Dakota carry supervisory obligations under SDCL 36-21A and the administrative rules adopted by the South Dakota Real Estate Commission. Those obligations extend to ensuring agents handle FIRPTA-affected transactions competently, even though FIRPTA is a federal requirement.

**Every brokerage should have a written policy that requires agents to determine the seller's citizenship or residency status before the listing goes active.** This does not mean agents need to become immigration lawyers. It means the intake checklist should include a direct question: "Is the seller a U.S. citizen or resident alien?" If the answer is no — or unclear — the broker should require that a FIRPTA-qualified closing agent or tax attorney be brought in before the property hits the MLS.

Brokers should also audit purchase agreements to confirm that FIRPTA provisions are included. A quarterly file review that checks for the presence of a non-foreign affidavit or a FIRPTA withholding addendum will catch gaps before they become IRS problems. Training sessions that cover [how checklists prevent compliance failures](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) are worth the time investment, particularly for teams that handle agricultural land or recreational property near the Missouri River corridor, where foreign investment is more common than agents expect.

Transaction management systems should flag foreign seller status as a discrete data field, not a note buried in an email thread. If a broker's file management does not surface FIRPTA status at a glance, the audit process will always be reactive rather than preventive.

## The Withholding Certificate Alternative and Its Limits

Sellers who believe the 15% withholding exceeds their actual tax liability can apply for a withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B. This form asks the IRS to approve a reduced withholding amount — sometimes zero — based on the seller's expected gain and applicable tax rate.

The catch is timing. The IRS says it will process Form 8288-B within 90 days, but in practice, processing often takes longer. If the application is pending at closing, the buyer still must withhold the full amount, place it in escrow, and wait for the IRS determination. This creates cash flow complications that agents must communicate clearly to both parties well before the closing date.

Some sellers also qualify for exemptions through treaty provisions or by certifying their status through a domestically controlled REIT. These are narrow exceptions that require legal and tax counsel — not something an agent should attempt to evaluate independently.

## Practical Steps for Agents Handling a Foreign Seller in 2026

Start with identification. At the listing appointment, ask directly about citizenship and residency status. Document the answer in writing. If the seller is a foreign person, notify the title company and the buyer's agent immediately so that the withholding logistics can be built into the transaction timeline.

Draft the purchase agreement with FIRPTA in mind. Include language that allocates responsibility for the withholding, specifies whether the withholding comes from the seller's proceeds or is an additional buyer obligation, and sets a deadline for the seller to provide a non-foreign affidavit or apply for a withholding certificate. The South Dakota Association of REALTORS addendum forms can be adapted for this purpose, and many title companies in the state maintain their own FIRPTA addenda.
