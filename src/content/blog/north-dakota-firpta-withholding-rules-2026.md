Roughly 7.8% of North Dakota's population is foreign-born, and a meaningful share of those residents hold real property they may eventually sell. If you represent a foreign seller or work the buy side of one of these transactions, the **North Dakota firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026** will determine whether the deal closes cleanly or blows up in escrow. Getting the withholding wrong does not just inconvenience the parties. It shifts personal tax liability onto the buyer and exposes agents to claims of negligence.

FIRPTA — the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, codified in Sections 1445 through 1445(e) of the Internal Revenue Code — has been federal law since 1980. Yet it remains one of the most misunderstood compliance obligations in residential real estate, especially in states like North Dakota where cross-border transactions with Canadian sellers are routine.

## How FIRPTA Withholding Works at the Federal Level

**Under IRC Section 1445, the buyer of U.S. real property is required to withhold 15% of the gross sale price when the seller is a "foreign person."** The term "foreign person" includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates. Residency for FIRPTA purposes is determined by the seller's federal income tax status, not by where they physically live or whether they hold a green card.

The withheld amount gets reported and remitted to the IRS using [Form 8288](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8288) (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both must be filed within 20 days after the date of transfer. The seller receives a copy of Form 8288-A to attach to their U.S. income tax return, where the withheld amount is credited against their actual tax liability.

There are exceptions that reduce or eliminate withholding. If the sale price is $300,000 or less and the buyer intends to use the property as a personal residence, no withholding is required. For sales between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer will use the property as a residence, the rate drops to 10%. The seller can also apply for a withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B to reduce the withholding to the expected tax liability, but that application must be filed before closing.

## North Dakota's State-Level Tax Obligations for Foreign Sellers

North Dakota does not layer a separate state withholding requirement on top of FIRPTA for foreign sellers. This distinguishes North Dakota from states like California, Hawaii, and Maryland, which impose their own withholding regimes. However, the absence of a state-level withholding obligation does not mean the seller owes nothing to the state.

A foreign person who sells North Dakota real property is subject to North Dakota income tax on the gain from that sale under N.D.C.C. Chapter 57-38, the state's income tax statute. The seller must file a North Dakota individual income tax return (Form ND-1) or, for foreign corporations, Form 40 (Corporate Income Tax Return). The North Dakota Office of State Tax Commissioner administers these filings.

| Obligation | Federal (FIRPTA) | North Dakota State |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding required at closing? | Yes, 15% of gross price (with exceptions) | No separate withholding |
| Forms at closing | IRS Form 8288, Form 8288-A | None at closing |
| Post-sale filing by seller | U.S. income tax return (Form 1040-NR or 1120-F) | Form ND-1 or Form 40 |
| Filing deadline | By the applicable tax return due date | By the applicable tax return due date |
| Administering body | Internal Revenue Service | ND Office of State Tax Commissioner |

The practical takeaway for agents: even though North Dakota will not require you to hold back state funds at closing, you should advise foreign sellers to consult a tax professional about their state filing obligation. Failing to mention it is not technically your liability, but it is the kind of omission that generates complaints.

## The Agent's Role and Liability Exposure

**FIRPTA liability falls squarely on the buyer, but agents and closing agents are deeply implicated in practice.** Under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(d), the "transferee's agent" — defined as any person who represents the buyer in negotiating, executing, or closing the transaction — can be held liable for the withholding tax if they had actual knowledge that the seller was a foreign person and failed to notify the buyer of the buyer's withholding obligation.

The penalty for an agent who knew about the seller's foreign status and stayed silent is significant: the agent becomes liable for the withholding tax up to the amount of the agent's compensation in the transaction. For a $400,000 sale with a 3% commission, that means $12,000 in personal liability. The IRS does not need to prove intent to defraud. Actual knowledge plus silence is enough.

Listing agents face a parallel exposure. Although the seller's agent is not directly subject to the withholding obligation, the seller's agent can be liable under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(d)(2) if they perform functions normally performed by the transferee's agent and has actual knowledge of the seller's foreign status.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With FIRPTA in North Dakota

The five errors below show up repeatedly in North Dakota transactions involving foreign sellers. Each one is avoidable with basic due diligence.

**First, agents assume that a seller with a U.S. mailing address or Social Security Number is not a foreign person.** FIRPTA status is determined by federal income tax residency, not by whether someone has a local address or an SSN. Many Canadian citizens own property in the Williston Basin oil patch or along the northern border and have U.S. mailing addresses without being U.S. tax residents. The only reliable way to confirm non-foreign status is to obtain a signed IRS Form W-9 or a FIRPTA affidavit (sometimes called a Non-Foreign Affidavit or Certificate of Non-Foreign Status under IRC Section 1445(b)(2)) from the seller.

Second, agents confuse the withholding certificate process with an exemption. When a seller files IRS Form 8288-B to request a reduced withholding amount, the application can take 90 days or longer for the IRS to process. Agents who assume the application alone suspends the withholding requirement at closing are wrong. The closing agent must still withhold the full 15% and hold it in escrow until the IRS issues the certificate, unless the parties agree to delay closing.

Third, agents fail to flag the FIRPTA issue early enough in the transaction. The withholding certificate application, the seller's tax planning, and the escrow instructions for holding withheld funds all require lead time. Raising FIRPTA for the first time at the closing table creates delays, renegotiations, and canceled deals.

Fourth, agents neglect to document the buyer's intended use of the property when relying on the personal-residence exception. The reduced withholding rates for properties under $1,000,000 only apply if the buyer signs an affidavit confirming the property will be used as a residence. Without that affidavit, the full 15% rate applies, and the buyer cannot retroactively claim the exception.

Fifth, agents do not coordinate with the title company or closing agent about who is preparing and filing Form 8288. In North Dakota, most closings are handled by title companies, but there is no state statute that assigns FIRPTA filing responsibility to the title company. If the title company's closing instructions do not address FIRPTA, the obligation defaults to the buyer, and the buyer's agent may be the only person in the room who knew about the seller's status.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under North Dakota Century Code Section 43-23-11.1 and the rules of the North Dakota Real Estate Commission. If an agent under your supervision mishandles a FIRPTA transaction, the commission can investigate whether your office had adequate training and compliance procedures in place.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check |
|---|---|
| Transaction file completeness | Verify FIRPTA affidavit or W-9 is in every file involving a seller who could be foreign |
| Agent training records | Confirm agents have received FIRPTA training within the past 24 months |
| Closing coordination | Review whether closing instructions address withholding responsibilities |
| Escalation protocol | Ensure agents know to involve a tax attorney or CPA when seller is foreign |
| Post-closing documentation | Confirm copies of Form 8288 and 8288-A are retained in the transaction file |

**Brokers should not treat FIRPTA as a "title company problem."** The title company may voluntarily handle the filing, but if it does not, the regulatory exposure lands on your agents and, by extension, on your brokerage. A one-page internal policy memo that requires agents to collect a FIRPTA affidavit or W-9 from every seller — not just suspected foreign sellers — eliminates the guesswork entirely.

A mandatory form at listing intake catches the issue before it becomes a crisis at closing. The [North Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://www.realestatend.org/) has not issued FIRPTA-specific guidance, but its general supervisory standards require brokers to maintain reasonable oversight of agents' compliance with federal and state law.

## Practical Steps for a Clean FIRPTA Transaction in North Dakota

Start at the listing agreement. Include a question on your intake form asking whether the seller is a U.S. citizen, U.S. resident alien, or foreign person for federal income tax purposes. If the seller is foreign, immediately advise them to consult a tax professional and begin the Form 8288-B withholding certificate application if they expect their actual tax liability to be less than 15% of the gross price.

At contract execution, make sure the purchase agreement addresses FIRPTA in writing. North Dakota does not mandate a specific FIRPTA clause in purchase agreements, but the standard forms used by the North Dakota Association of REALTORS include optional addenda for tax withholding. If you are using a custom contract, add a clause that identifies the seller's foreign status, the withholding amount, and the responsible party for filing Forms 8288 and 8288-A.

Before closing, coordinate with the title company to confirm that the withheld funds will be remitted to the IRS and that the forms will be filed within the 20-day window. Get this confirmation in writing. If the title company declines to handle the filing, the buyer must arrange for the remittance independently, and you should document that the buyer was informed of this obligation.
