## Understanding Iowa Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) operates under Iowa Code Chapter 543B and Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 193E, and both received meaningful updates heading into 2026. If you hold an active license in this state, the Iowa commission & licensing rules 2026 framework affects how you structure compensation, disclose agency relationships, and maintain your license status. Ignoring these rules does not just risk a slap on the wrist — it risks your livelihood.

IREC reports to the Iowa Professional Licensing Bureau and holds disciplinary authority over roughly 14,000 active licensees. The commission meets monthly, publishes enforcement actions publicly, and has shown an increasing appetite for audits tied to compensation disclosure and trust account irregularities. Agents who operated on autopilot in prior years will find that the post-NAR-settlement environment has tightened scrutiny on commission transparency at the state level.

This article breaks down the specific statutes, forms, and deadlines you need to know — along with the consequences of getting them wrong.

## Licensing Structure and Renewal Requirements

Iowa uses a three-tier licensing model: salesperson, broker associate, and broker. Salespersons must complete 60 hours of pre-license education and pass both the national and state exam portions administered through PSI. Broker applicants need 24 months of active licensee experience plus 72 additional classroom hours covering brokerage management, trust accounts, and Iowa law.

**License renewal in Iowa operates on a three-year cycle, not the two-year cycle many neighboring states use.** Each renewal requires 36 hours of continuing education, including a mandatory 8-hour IREC-approved ethics and law update. The renewal fee is $225 for salespersons and $225 for brokers, payable through the Iowa Professional Licensing Bureau's online portal.

Failure to renew by the expiration date triggers an automatic inactive status under Iowa Administrative Code 193E-5.3. You cannot earn commission, show property, or negotiate on behalf of clients while inactive. Any commission earned during a lapsed period is subject to disgorgement, and the broker who allowed you to practice faces separate disciplinary exposure.

## Commission Disclosure and Written Agreements

Iowa Code 543B.56A requires that all compensation arrangements be disclosed in writing before the licensee provides brokerage services. This statute predates the NAR settlement changes but aligns with the same principles — the buyer or seller must understand who pays, how much, and under what circumstances.

For buyer representation, agents must execute a written buyer agency agreement. IREC does not mandate a single statewide form, but the Iowa Association of Realtors (IAR) publishes Form 101 (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) and Form 102 (Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement). Both forms include fields for compensation amount, payment source, and duration. Leaving any of these fields blank or ambiguous constitutes a violation of Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.1(3).

| Form Number | Purpose | Required Before |
|---|---|---|
| IAR Form 101 | Exclusive Buyer Representation | Showing property or submitting offers |
| IAR Form 102 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Representation | Providing brokerage services beyond initial contact |
| IAR Form 120 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Making a binding offer |
| IAR Form 205 | Agency Disclosure | First substantive contact with consumer |

On the listing side, IAR Form 106 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) governs seller-agent compensation. The form requires explicit disclosure of any portion of commission offered to cooperating brokers. Since the MLS compensation field is no longer populated through the Iowa Regional MLS systems in the same manner, agents must communicate cooperating compensation through separate written offers or addenda attached to the purchase agreement.

## Trust Accounts and Earnest Money Handling

**Iowa Code 543B.46 places trust account responsibility squarely on the designated broker, not the individual agent.** Every brokerage must maintain at least one trust account at an Iowa-based financial institution, and all client funds — including earnest money deposits — must be deposited within five business days of receipt unless the purchase agreement specifies otherwise.

Commingling personal funds with trust account funds remains the single most common basis for license revocation in Iowa. IREC's published enforcement reports from 2024 and 2025 show that trust account violations accounted for over 30 percent of formal disciplinary actions. The commission does not issue warnings for commingling; it proceeds directly to formal charges under Iowa Administrative Code 193E-12.4.

Agents who collect earnest money checks must deliver them to their broker or the designated closing agent immediately. "Desk drawer deposits" — holding checks until an offer is accepted — violate Iowa law even if the funds are never lost. The [Iowa Real Estate Commission's guidance documents](https://plb.iowa.gov/real-estate) explicitly state that receipt of funds triggers the deposit timeline regardless of offer acceptance status.

## Common Mistakes That Trigger Enforcement Action

Knowing the rules matters less if you do not recognize the patterns that get agents into trouble. Here are five specific errors IREC has flagged in recent disciplinary proceedings.

**First, practicing on an expired license.** This happens more often than agents admit. The three-year cycle creates a false sense of security, and agents who miss their renewal window sometimes continue showing homes or writing offers for weeks before realizing the lapse. Under Iowa Code 543B.31, this constitutes unlicensed practice — a serious misdemeanor carrying fines up to $1,875.

Second, failing to provide the Agency Disclosure (Form 205) at first substantive contact. Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.2 defines "substantive contact" as any conversation about a specific property or the consumer's real estate needs. Many agents wait until a showing or open house follow-up, which is too late. The disclosure must happen at the point of initial meaningful interaction.

Third, advertising without broker supervision. Iowa Administrative Code 193E-11.1 requires that all advertising by a salesperson include the brokerage name as registered with IREC. Social media posts, Zillow profiles, and even email signatures that display only the agent's name without the brokerage violate this rule. IREC issued 47 advertising-related citations in 2024 alone.

Fourth, dual agency without written consent from both parties. Iowa permits disclosed dual agency under Iowa Code 543B.57, but both buyer and seller must execute written consent on IAR Form 205D (Consent to Dual Agency) before the licensee acts for both. Verbal consent is insufficient and unenforceable.

Fifth, collecting referral fees without disclosure. If you refer a client to another agent or receive compensation from a service provider, Iowa Code 543B.34 requires written disclosure to all parties. Undisclosed referral fees are grounds for license suspension and can void the underlying transaction if challenged in court.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability for every licensee operating under their brokerage. Iowa Administrative Code 193E-14.1 outlines the broker's duty to establish written policies, maintain transaction files, and conduct periodic reviews. Simply having policies on paper is not enough — IREC expects documented evidence of enforcement.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Regulatory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Iowa Code 543B.46 |
| License status verification | Quarterly (minimum) | Iowa Administrative Code 193E-5.3 |
| Agency disclosure file review | Per transaction | Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.2 |
| Advertising compliance | Ongoing | Iowa Administrative Code 193E-11.1 |
| Buyer agreement documentation | Per transaction | Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.1 |

**Brokers who delegate compliance oversight to an unlicensed office manager do so at their own peril.** IREC has held brokers personally liable for violations they did not commit but failed to catch during reasonable supervision. The 2025 disciplinary case of a central Iowa brokerage that lost its license after an agent mishandled three consecutive trust deposits underscores this point — the broker claimed ignorance, and IREC revoked the brokerage license anyway.

Transaction file retention is another audit priority. Iowa Administrative Code 193E-14.2 requires brokers to retain all transaction documents for five years from closing or termination. This includes signed agency disclosures, commission agreements, and all written correspondence material to the transaction. Brokers who rely on agents to self-file are building a compliance gap that IREC will eventually find. ## Liability When Things Go Wrong

The consequences of non-compliance in Iowa extend beyond IREC discipline. Civil liability attaches when consumers suffer financial harm due to a licensee's failure to follow commission or disclosure rules. Iowa courts have awarded damages in cases where undisclosed dual agency caused a buyer to overpay, and where undisclosed referral arrangements created conflicts of interest.

IREC's disciplinary options include formal citation, civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation under Iowa Code 543B.29, license suspension, and permanent revocation. The commission also has authority to require restitution to harmed consumers. A single trust account violation can cascade into multiple charges — one for the commingling itself, one for failure to supervise (against the broker), and one for misrepresentation if the consumer was told their funds were secured.

Criminal liability enters the picture when unlicensed practice or misappropriation of funds is involved. Iowa Code 543B.31 classifies unlicensed practice as a serious misdemeanor. Theft of trust funds is prosecuted under Iowa's general theft statutes and can carry felony charges depending on the amount. ## Continuing Education and the 2026 Cycle

Iowa's CE requirements for the current cycle include 36 hours total, with specific mandates that agents often overlook. The 8-hour mandatory module covers legislative updates, ethics refreshers, and administrative rule changes. The remaining 28 hours are elective but must be approved by IREC in advance — courses taken from non-approved providers do not count, regardless of content quality.

IREC has added emphasis on commission transparency education for the 2026 cycle, reflecting the broader industry shift. At least one approved elective now covers buyer representation agreements, compensation negotiation disclosures, and the interaction between Iowa law and MLS policy changes. Agents who completed their CE early in the cycle may have missed this content and should verify their transcript against current requirements.

The deadline trap catches agents every cycle. Completing CE in the final month before expiration leaves no buffer for processing delays. IREC's online system can take up to 10 business days to reflect completed courses. Agents who file renewals without confirmed CE credits on their transcript receive automatic denials, triggering the inactive-status cascade described earlier.
