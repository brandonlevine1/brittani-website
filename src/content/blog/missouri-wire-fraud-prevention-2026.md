## Missouri Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules That Protect Your License

Wire fraud losses in real estate exceeded $446 million nationally in 2023 according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and Missouri transactions are not immune. Understanding Missouri wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional—it is a license-level obligation that the Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC) expects every active agent to follow. A single compromised email thread can redirect hundreds of thousands of dollars, destroy a client relationship, and trigger disciplinary proceedings that end careers.

The Missouri Association of Realtors (MAR) and MREC have both tightened expectations around written disclosures, verification protocols, and broker oversight since the wave of business email compromise schemes that hit the state's major metro markets in 2024 and 2025. This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, and enforcement mechanisms that apply to you right now.

## Statutory Framework Governing Wire Fraud Disclosures in Missouri

Missouri does not have a single standalone "wire fraud prevention statute." Instead, agent obligations arise from the intersection of several existing laws and regulatory directives. RSMo 339.100 establishes the duty of licensees to disclose material facts and exercise reasonable care in protecting client interests. MREC has interpreted this duty to include warning clients about wire fraud risks in writing before any funds are transferred.

RSMo 570.030 (stealing by deceit) and RSMo 570.223 (financial exploitation) provide criminal penalties when wire fraud occurs, but the more immediate concern for agents is the civil and administrative exposure under the licensing statute. MREC's 2025 advisory bulletin explicitly stated that failure to provide wire fraud warnings constitutes a potential violation of RSMo 339.150, which authorizes the Commission to suspend, revoke, or censure a license for "demonstrating incompetence to act as a real estate licensee."

> "A licensee who fails to warn clients about known risks to their funds, including wire transfer fraud schemes, may be found in violation of RSMo 339.150(2) and subject to disciplinary action." — MREC Advisory Bulletin 2025-03

The [Missouri Real Estate Commission](https://pr.mo.gov/realestate.asp) publishes these bulletins on its website and expects agents to monitor them between renewal cycles.

## Specific Forms and Documents Agents Must Use

**MAR Form 2640 (Wire Fraud and Electronic Communication Advisory) is the primary disclosure document Missouri agents should present to buyers and sellers.** This form warns parties that email communications can be intercepted, that wiring instructions should never be trusted without verbal verification, and that the brokerage is not responsible for losses resulting from fraudulent instructions the brokerage did not send.

Some brokerages have developed proprietary wire fraud advisories that supplement or replace MAR Form 2640. Either approach satisfies the disclosure obligation, provided the form includes specific language about callback verification procedures, warns against relying solely on emailed instructions, and is signed by the client with a date.

| Form / Document | Purpose | When to Present | Who Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAR Form 2640 | Wire fraud advisory disclosure | At contract execution or before first wire discussion | All parties to the transaction |
| Brokerage-specific wire advisory | Supplemental or alternative disclosure | Same as above | Client(s) and presenting agent |
| Title company wire verification letter | Confirms verified wiring details via separate channel | 48-72 hours before closing | Title officer and receiving party |
| MREC Complaint Form 300 | Used by consumers to report agent non-compliance | Post-incident | Complainant |

Agents who work with [transaction coordinators](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) should confirm that wire fraud disclosures are included in their standard checklist and tracked to signature.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of ignoring wire fraud prevention protocols are severe and multi-layered. MREC can open an investigation based on a single consumer complaint filed on Form 300, and the burden shifts to the agent to demonstrate that reasonable precautions were taken. Without a signed wire fraud advisory in the file, that demonstration becomes extremely difficult.

**Civil liability is the most immediate financial risk.** Missouri courts have allowed negligence claims against agents who failed to warn clients about wire fraud, particularly when the agent's email was the vector of compromise. E&O insurance policies increasingly contain wire fraud exclusions or sub-limits, meaning the agent or brokerage may bear the full loss.

MREC disciplinary outcomes range from a letter of censure to outright license revocation. Between those extremes, the Commission frequently imposes mandatory additional continuing education hours, supervised practice periods, and fines up to $2,500 per violation under RSMo 339.150. Repeat violations or cases involving actual client loss trigger the harshest penalties.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make With Wire Fraud Prevention

Even agents who understand the risk often execute prevention protocols poorly. These are the five most frequent errors MREC investigators and compliance auditors encounter in Missouri files.

**First, agents present the wire fraud advisory at the wrong time.** The disclosure must be delivered before any party initiates a wire transfer—ideally at contract execution. Agents who wait until the day before closing are technically compliant only if no wiring instructions were exchanged prior. In practice, title companies often send preliminary instructions weeks earlier, meaning a late advisory fails its protective purpose.

Second, agents forward wiring instructions from title companies via email without adding their own warning language. This creates the appearance that the agent is authenticating the instructions, which increases liability if those instructions were intercepted and altered in transit. The correct practice is to never forward wire instructions at all, and instead direct clients to contact the title company directly using a phone number the client obtained independently.

Third, agents use unsigned or undated versions of the advisory. A verbal warning does not satisfy MREC expectations. Without a dated signature, the disclosure is unenforceable and useless in defending against a complaint or lawsuit.

Fourth, agents fail to document callback verification attempts. When a client asks an agent to confirm wiring details, the agent should log the date, time, and phone number used for verification. This documentation matters if the transaction is later compromised.

Fifth, agents assume the title company or [closer handles everything](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) related to wire security. Delegation does not eliminate the agent's independent duty to disclose risks. The title company's verification process protects their own liability—your disclosure obligation protects yours.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear heightened responsibility under RSMo 339.120, which requires supervising brokers to maintain "adequate supervision" over all licensees affiliated with their office. In the context of wire fraud prevention, this means brokers must implement written policies, verify compliance, and document their oversight.

| Broker Obligation | How to Audit | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Wire fraud advisory in every file | Random file review (minimum 10% of closed transactions) | Quarterly |
| Agent training completion records | CE tracking system or internal LMS | Annually |
| Email security standards (MFA, encryption) | IT audit or managed service provider report | Semi-annually |
| Incident response plan documented | Review and update written plan | Annually |
| Client callback verification logs | Spot-check agent documentation | Monthly |

A broker who discovers non-compliance and fails to correct it faces their own disciplinary exposure. MREC has sanctioned brokers separately from their agents in cases where systemic failures were evident. The Commission's position is clear: a brokerage without a written wire fraud prevention policy is a brokerage operating below the standard of care.

Brokers should also audit whether their agents are using personal email addresses for transaction communications. Gmail, Yahoo, and other consumer email services lack the enterprise-level security controls that reduce interception risk. A brokerage-provided email domain with multi-factor authentication and advanced threat detection should be mandatory for all transaction-related correspondence.

## Training and Continuing Education Expectations

MREC requires 12 hours of continuing education every two years for license renewal, and the Commission has signaled that wire fraud content should be part of every licensee's education plan. While no single CE course is mandated by number, MREC's 2025 advisory explicitly recommended that brokerages incorporate wire fraud training into their annual compliance meetings.

Several MREC-approved providers now offer courses specifically addressing real estate wire fraud prevention, including modules on identifying phishing emails, implementing callback procedures, and understanding [the points where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) due to communication failures. Agents who complete these courses should retain certificates of completion in their personal files and provide copies to their supervising broker.

The practical reality is that one training session per year is the minimum. Threat tactics evolve quarterly—AI-generated phishing emails now mimic writing styles of specific title officers, and deepfake voice calls have been reported in multiple Missouri metro areas. Agents must stay current or risk being the weak link in the closing chain.

## Building Wire Fraud Prevention Into Your Transaction Workflow

The most effective wire fraud prevention happens when disclosure and verification are baked into every transaction from day one, not bolted on as an afterthought before closing. Smart agents treat the wire fraud advisory the same way they treat the agency disclosure—it goes out early, gets signed immediately, and gets filed permanently.

Your transaction workflow should include a triggered reminder at three points: contract execution, title commitment receipt, and 72 hours before scheduled closing. At each point, the client receives a written reminder to verify any wiring instructions through an independently obtained phone number. Agents who [struggle with deadline tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) should build these triggers into their CRM or task management system rather than relying on memory.
