## Utah Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of "Utah attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" comes up every time an out-of-state buyer or a newly licensed agent encounters the settlement process for the first time. Utah falls squarely in the title-company camp, but the nuances of who does what — and where agent liability begins — are more layered than a one-sentence answer suggests. Getting this wrong exposes you and your brokerage to regulatory complaints, deal cancellations, and potential civil claims.

Utah does not mandate attorney involvement at closing. The Utah Department of Insurance licenses title insurance companies and agents under Utah Code Title 31A, Chapter 23a, and those entities are authorized to conduct settlements, prepare closing documents, and disburse funds. An attorney can appear at any closing voluntarily — retained by either party — but no statute forces that participation.

## How Utah's Statutory Framework Assigns Closing Authority

**Utah Code 31A-23a-406 governs the conduct of title insurance transactions and establishes that a licensed title agent or company may act as the settlement agent.** This section defines permissible activities including escrow handling, document preparation related to the title insurance transaction, and recording of conveyance instruments. The Utah Insurance Department oversees licensing, while the Utah Division of Real Estate regulates agent conduct on the other side of the transaction.

The distinction matters because real estate agents sometimes blur the line between facilitation and legal advice at the closing table. Utah Code 78B-3-404, which addresses the unauthorized practice of law, does not carve out an explicit exception for licensees beyond completing approved contract forms. The Utah Supreme Court's advisory opinion in *Conder v. Harl* (1992) confirmed that drafting legal documents outside pre-approved forms constitutes practicing law without a license.

For agents accustomed to states like [Wisconsin's closing procedures](/blog/wisconsin-title-and-closing-procedures-2026), Utah's framework will feel familiar but not identical. Wisconsin similarly allows title companies to conduct closings, yet the regulatory bodies and form ecosystems differ in meaningful ways.

## The REPC and Form Numbers Every Agent Must Know

Utah's standard residential purchase contract is the Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC), Form 24 — currently maintained by the Utah Association of Realtors. Section 7 of the REPC addresses settlement, and it designates how parties select the title company and allocate closing costs. Agents fill in the title company name, but the form itself does not require attorney designation.

Additional forms that surface at or before closing include:

| Form | Purpose | Who Completes It |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| REPC (Form 24) | Purchase agreement | Buyer's agent drafts, both parties sign |
| Seller's Property Condition Disclosure | Material defect disclosure | Seller |
| Addendum No. 1 (Financing) | Loan terms and contingency | Buyer's agent |
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (pre-1978) | Federal requirement | Seller and agents |
| Settlement Statement (CD/HUD) | Final cost allocation | Title company/lender |

Agents do not prepare the settlement statement. That document is the title company's domain under TRID rules (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure). If an agent attempts to draft or materially alter a Closing Disclosure, they have crossed into unauthorized practice territory — both under state law and federal CFPB enforcement guidance.

## What Happens When Agents Cross the Line

**Failing to respect the boundary between facilitation and legal practice carries real consequences in Utah.** The Utah Division of Real Estate, operating under the Department of Commerce, processes complaints against licensees under Utah Code 61-2f-401. Violations related to unauthorized legal advice or document preparation can result in license suspension, revocation, fines up to $5,000 per violation, or mandatory continuing education.

Beyond the Division's administrative actions, an agent who drafts legal instruments or advises on title defect resolution could face civil liability. If that advice leads to financial loss — a missed lien, an unresolved boundary dispute, an improperly drafted addendum that voids a contractual right — the buyer or seller can sue for negligence or breach of fiduciary duty. Your E&O policy may deny coverage if the act constitutes unauthorized practice of law, because most policies exclude illegal acts from their indemnity provisions.

Deal cancellation is the more immediate risk. A title company that discovers an agent-drafted instrument that doesn't conform to recording requirements will refuse to insure the transaction. The deal stalls, the buyer's rate lock expires, and your commission evaporates while both parties point fingers.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make at Utah Closings

Three to five errors recur across Utah brokerages year after year. Recognizing them is easier than fixing them after the fact.

**First, agents advise clients on how to hold title.** Suggesting "joint tenancy" versus "tenants in common" versus an LLC structure constitutes legal advice in Utah. The title company or an attorney should handle vesting guidance. Agents who scribble vesting instructions on the REPC expose themselves to liability if the ownership structure later creates tax or probate complications.

Second, agents negotiate directly with the title company on behalf of the client without written authorization. While the REPC gives the agent transactional authority, modifying settlement terms, waiving title exceptions, or directing payoff allocations requires explicit client consent — ideally documented in writing or acknowledged via an amendment.

Third, agents fail to disclose affiliated business arrangements (AfBAs). If your brokerage owns or has a financial interest in a title company, [RESPA Section 8](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/8/) mandates an Affiliated Business Arrangement Disclosure at or before the time of referral. Omitting this disclosure triggers potential CFPB enforcement, including disgorgement of fees and a $10,000 penalty per violation.

Fourth, agents pressure clients into using a specific title company by framing it as "required" or "standard." RESPA prohibits kickbacks and referral fee arrangements. The REPC's Section 7 allows negotiation, but the buyer's right to select a title provider remains protected under federal law.

Fifth, agents skip the final walkthrough coordination with the title company's closing schedule. When the walkthrough reveals issues — a broken HVAC system, missing fixtures — and the closing is minutes away, agents scramble to draft impromptu repair agreements that lack legal enforceability. Using the UAR Addendum framework (or requesting a short delay) avoids post-closing disputes that no title policy will cover.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Utah Code 61-2f-401(9), which holds designated brokers accountable for the acts of their affiliated licensees. If an agent in your office routinely provides vesting advice or drafts non-approved documents, the Division can sanction the brokerage — not just the individual agent.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|------------|---------------|-----------|
| REPC completion | All fields filled correctly, no legal advice notations | Every transaction |
| AfBA disclosures | Signed disclosure in file if affiliated title company used | Every referral |
| Vesting instructions | Agent did NOT advise; client selected independently or via attorney/title officer | Every transaction |
| Addenda usage | Only UAR-approved forms used; no agent-drafted legal instruments | Every transaction |
| Closing attendance notes | Agent did not sign as witness or notary without proper commission | Quarterly spot-check |

Quarterly file audits remain the gold standard. Review a random sample of 10-15 percent of closed transactions per agent, specifically checking whether any document in the file was prepared by the agent outside the UAR-approved form library. Flag files where title companies sent correction requests — that pattern signals an agent who is overstepping.

Brokers should also verify that their agents understand [where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) during the closing phase. Most failures trace back to miscommunication between the agent and the title company on deadlines, document delivery, or wire instructions. Implementing a consistent [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) at the brokerage level reduces fraud exposure and keeps closings on schedule.

## The Role of Attorneys When They Do Appear

Even though Utah doesn't require attorney participation, attorneys show up at closings in several scenarios: complex commercial transactions, estate sales where probate is involved, transactions with boundary disputes or easement negotiations, and 1031 exchanges requiring qualified intermediary oversight. In these cases, the attorney represents one party — not the transaction.

**Agents must understand that a party's attorney does not replace the title company's settlement function.** The title company still issues the policy, records the deed, and disburses funds. The attorney reviews documents, raises legal objections, and protects their client's interests. These roles are complementary, not interchangeable.

When an attorney is involved, agents should coordinate document delivery timelines with both the attorney and the title officer. Attorneys often request a 48-72 hour review window for closing documents. Building that buffer into your pre-closing timeline prevents last-day scrambles and avoids the "we can't close today" call that devastates everyone's schedule.

## Staying Compliant Without Slowing Down

Transaction management in Utah's title-company closing model rewards agents who know their lane. You fill out the REPC, coordinate inspections, manage contingency deadlines, and facilitate communication. You do not draft deeds, advise on title exceptions, or prepare legal instruments.
