## Understanding Louisiana Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC) has not loosened its grip on how agents earn, split, and document commissions. If you hold an active Louisiana license, the Louisiana commission & licensing rules 2026 framework demands strict compliance with statutes that carry real teeth — fines up to $2,000 per violation, license suspension, and even criminal referrals. This is not abstract regulatory trivia. It affects how you get paid, whether your deals survive scrutiny, and how exposed your brokerage is to liability.

Louisiana governs real estate licensees primarily through the Louisiana Real Estate License Law, codified at [La. R.S. 37:1430-1470](https://www.legis.la.gov/legis/Law.aspx?d=95014), along with the LREC Administrative Rules found in Title 46, Part LXVII of the Louisiana Administrative Code. Both sources were updated in late 2025 with clarifications around cooperative compensation disclosure and team advertising requirements that take effect for 2026 transactions.

The regulatory body — LREC — operates out of Baton Rouge and maintains exclusive authority to issue, suspend, revoke, and reinstate licenses. It also adjudicates complaints, conducts audits, and publishes mandatory forms that agents must use verbatim. There is no optional compliance here.

## How Commission Payments Must Flow Under Louisiana Law

**Louisiana law is explicit: commission compensation must pass through the sponsoring broker before reaching the agent.** La. R.S. 37:1455(A) states that no licensee shall pay a commission or other compensation to any person for performing real estate activities unless that person holds an active Louisiana license and acts under the supervision of a broker. Violating this rule is not just a LREC infraction — it can trigger civil liability for the broker and constitute a misdemeanor under La. R.S. 37:1460.

In practice, this means an agent cannot receive a referral fee wire directly from an out-of-state brokerage. It means a seller cannot pay the listing agent's personal LLC without routing the funds through the broker's trust or operating account. It means cooperative compensation offered through MLS systems must be paid broker-to-broker, not agent-to-agent.

The 2026 updates reinforce that any written agreement regarding compensation — whether between a buyer and their agent or between cooperating brokers — must be memorialized before services are rendered. LREC Form 110-B (Buyer Representation Agreement) and the Louisiana Exclusive Listing Contract (Form ELC-1) both contain commission disclosure sections that must be completed in full.

## Forms Every Louisiana Agent Must Know

LREC publishes mandatory forms that are updated periodically. Using outdated versions or omitting required disclosures can void a commission claim or trigger disciplinary action. Here are the forms most directly tied to commission and licensing compliance:

| Form Number | Form Name | When Required |
|---|---|---|
| Form 110 | Agency Disclosure | First substantive contact with any party |
| Form DAD | Dual Agency Disclosure | Before signing purchase agreement in dual agency |
| Form ELC-1 | Exclusive Listing Contract | At listing appointment |
| Form 110-B | Buyer Representation Agreement | Before showing properties or rendering buyer services |
| Form TDA | Transaction Disclosure of Agency | At or before the time of offer |

Agents frequently assume the listing contract alone satisfies disclosure requirements. It does not. Form 110 must be presented independently at first substantive contact — defined by LREC Rule 2901 as the first discussion of specific property features, price, terms, or client motivation. Missing this window does not merely expose you to a fine; it can give a disgruntled client grounds to challenge your commission entitlement.

**Brokers who fail to maintain signed copies of these forms face audit deficiencies that can escalate to license suspension.** LREC Rule 3101 mandates a five-year retention period for all transaction documents, including commission agreements and agency disclosures.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance in Louisiana are layered and escalating. LREC's enforcement authority under La. R.S. 37:1456 allows the Commission to impose any combination of the following: formal reprimand, mandatory education, fines up to $2,000 per violation, license suspension (up to one year), or permanent revocation.

Beyond administrative penalties, agents face civil exposure. If a licensee collects a commission without proper documentation, the paying party can sue for disgorgement — meaning you could be ordered to return the entire commission plus attorneys' fees. Louisiana courts have upheld these claims when agents failed to maintain written representation agreements or operated on expired licenses.

Criminal liability also lurks. La. R.S. 37:1460 classifies unlicensed real estate activity as a misdemeanor punishable by fines up to $500 and imprisonment for up to six months. An agent whose license lapses during a transaction and who continues to perform licensable activities — showing property, negotiating terms, presenting offers — fits squarely within this statute.

Deal cancellation is another risk that agents underestimate. If a buyer or seller discovers that their agent lacked a valid license at the time of contract execution, they may have grounds to void the transaction under Louisiana's nullity provisions in the Civil Code. This creates liability not just for the agent, but for the brokerage and potentially the closing attorney.

## Common Mistakes Louisiana Agents Make

Five specific errors surface repeatedly in LREC disciplinary actions and audit findings. Agents who understand these patterns can avoid becoming cautionary tales.

**First, failing to present Form 110 at first substantive contact.** Agents frequently wait until the offer stage or bundle it with the purchase agreement. LREC Rule 2901 is unambiguous about timing, and the Commission has fined agents who delayed disclosure even when the client later signed the form without complaint. The issue is not client objection — it is regulatory timing.

Second, advertising without proper broker identification. La. R.S. 37:1449 and LREC Rule 3501 require that all advertising by a licensee include the sponsoring broker's name in a manner that is clear and conspicuous. Team names, personal brands, and social media profiles that omit the brokerage name violate this rule. The 2026 updates expanded enforcement to include digital advertising on platforms like Instagram and TikTok.

Third, operating on an expired or inactive license during a pending transaction. Continuing education in Louisiana requires 12 hours every year (not every renewal cycle), and failure to complete CE by December 31 results in automatic inactivation on January 1. Agents who close a deal in January without having satisfied the prior year's CE are technically unlicensed at closing.

Fourth, receiving compensation from a party other than the sponsoring broker. This includes referral fees from mortgage companies, kickbacks disguised as marketing contributions, and direct payments from cooperating agents. All compensation must route through the broker.

Fifth, failing to disclose dual agency before the purchase agreement is signed. Form DAD must be executed prior to the agreement — not contemporaneously. LREC has penalized agents who obtained the dual agency signature on the same page or at the same sitting as the offer without clear prior disclosure.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Louisiana carry heightened responsibility under La. R.S. 37:1447, which imposes supervisory duties that go beyond passive oversight. If an agent under your sponsorship violates commission or licensing rules, LREC can hold the broker jointly liable — even if the broker was unaware of the violation.

A quarterly audit should verify three categories: license status of all sponsored agents, completeness of transaction files, and advertising compliance. For license status, brokers should cross-reference their roster against the LREC license verification tool monthly, particularly in January when CE-related inactivations take effect.

| Audit Category | What to Check | Frequency | LREC Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| License Status | Active status, CE completion, renewal date | Monthly | La. R.S. 37:1447 |
| Transaction Files | Forms 110, DAD, ELC-1, 110-B signed and dated | Per closing | Rule 3101 |
| Commission Documentation | Written agreements, split schedules, disbursement records | Per closing | La. R.S. 37:1455 |
| Advertising | Broker name on all materials, team compliance | Quarterly | Rule 3501 |
| Trust Accounting | Deposits within 24 hours, reconciliation, ledger accuracy | Monthly | Rule 3701 |

**Trust account compliance is where brokers face the most severe penalties.** LREC Rule 3701 requires that earnest money deposits be placed in the broker's trust account within one business day of acceptance. Commingling funds — even temporarily — can result in immediate suspension. Brokers should reconcile trust accounts monthly and maintain a per-transaction ledger that ties directly to the purchase agreement terms.

The cost of a single trust account violation — suspension of the entire brokerage — dwarfs the cost of any compliance system.

## Cooperative Compensation After the 2025-2026 Reforms

The post-settlement era has reshaped how cooperative compensation is offered and documented in Louisiana. While LREC has not banned cooperative compensation outright, the Commission now requires that any offer of compensation to a cooperating broker be documented in writing outside the MLS system. This means the listing broker cannot rely solely on an MLS field to satisfy the disclosure requirement.

Form ELC-1 has been updated to include a specific section where the listing broker and seller agree on whether cooperative compensation will be offered, the amount, and through what mechanism it will be communicated. Agents who use older versions of this form are out of compliance.

On the buyer side, Form 110-B now requires explicit disclosure of how the buyer's agent will be compensated — whether through seller-offered cooperative compensation, direct buyer payment, or a combination. This creates a paper trail that protects both the agent and the broker if a dispute arises post-closing.

## Continuing Education and License Maintenance for 2026

Louisiana requires 12 hours of continuing education annually, including 1 hour of mandatory LREC law updates and 1 hour of supervision content (for brokers). The remaining 10 hours may be fulfilled through elective courses approved by LREC. Unlike some states that operate on biennial cycles, Louisiana's annual requirement creates a tighter compliance window.

Failure to complete CE by December 31 does not generate a grace period. Your license goes inactive on January 1. Reinstatement requires completing the delinquent hours, paying a reinstatement fee, and potentially appearing before the Commission if the lapse exceeded 90 days.
