## Understanding Maryland Real Estate License Reciprocity & Portability 2026

Agents working the DC-Maryland-Virginia corridor assume cross-border transactions are frictionless. They are not. **Maryland real estate license reciprocity & portability 2026** rules carry specific statutory requirements, form filings, and penalties that trip up even experienced licensees who have operated across state lines for years. The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under the Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing within the Maryland Department of Labor, governs every aspect of who may lawfully represent buyers and sellers inside the state.

Maryland's framework is not reciprocity in the traditional sense. The state uses a cooperative licensing model that requires affirmative application, documentation, and ongoing compliance. Confusing Maryland's approach with full-reciprocity states—where you simply register and go—is the single fastest way to jeopardize a deal and your commission.

## The Legal Foundation: Business Occupations & Professions Article §17-305

Maryland's authority for non-resident licensing sits in the Annotated Code of Maryland, Business Occupations & Professions Article, Title 17, Subtitle 3. Section §17-305 establishes that a person licensed in another jurisdiction may obtain a Maryland license without completing the state's prelicensing education if certain conditions are met. Those conditions include holding an active license in a state whose requirements MREC deems "substantially equivalent" and passing the Maryland portion of the licensing exam unless specifically waived.

The Commission retains sole discretion to determine substantial equivalency. This is not a rubber-stamp process. MREC reviews the applicant's home-state education hours, exam standards, and supervisory structure before granting approval.

Agents coming from Virginia, Pennsylvania, Delaware, West Virginia, and the District of Columbia frequently qualify under §17-305, but qualification is never automatic. You must file and wait for written confirmation before conducting any licensable activity in Maryland.

## Forms, Fees, and Filing Mechanics

**The primary document is MREC Form 2.13, the Application for Non-Resident License.** This form requires the applicant's home-state license number, supervising broker information in the home state, and the name of the Maryland broker who will hold the cooperative license. Without a Maryland-based supervising broker on record, the application is dead on arrival.

| Requirement | Maryland Cooperative License | Full New License |
|---|---|---|
| Application Form | MREC Form 2.13 | MREC Form 2.01 |
| State Exam (MD portion) | Required unless waived | Required |
| Prelicensing Education | Waived if substantially equivalent | 60 hours |
| E&O Insurance | Required | Required |
| CE (every 2 years) | 15 hours including MD legislative update | 15 hours including MD legislative update |
| Application Fee | $160 | $160 |

Applicants must also submit a Certified License History directly from their home state's regulatory body. Maryland does not accept printouts from online license-lookup tools. The certification must come on the issuing commission's letterhead or through its official verification system.

Additionally, agents should be aware that errors-and-omissions insurance must meet Maryland's minimum coverage thresholds. The Commission requires at least $100,000 per occurrence and $500,000 aggregate unless the supervising Maryland broker's blanket policy covers the non-resident licensee explicitly.

## What Happens When You Practice Without Proper Maryland Licensure

This is where agents get burned. Under §17-601 of the Business Occupations & Professions Article, engaging in real estate brokerage without a valid Maryland license is a misdemeanor. Penalties include fines up to $5,000 per violation, and each transaction constitutes a separate violation. The Commission may also refer cases to the Maryland Attorney General's office for injunctive relief.

Beyond criminal exposure, the practical consequences are severe. A buyer or seller represented by an unlicensed agent can void the contract under Maryland common law principles of agency. Commissions earned during unlicensed activity are not merely at risk—they are legally unenforceable. Maryland courts have consistently held that an agent cannot recover compensation for services performed without proper licensure, regardless of the quality of work performed.

**Brokers face liability too.** Under §17-322, a supervising broker who permits an agent to practice without verifying their Maryland licensure status may be subject to disciplinary action including suspension or revocation of the broker's own license. The compliance burden runs uphill, not just downhill.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Maryland Portability

The errors below surface repeatedly in MREC disciplinary records and transaction disputes. Any one of them can derail a closing or trigger a formal complaint.

The first mistake is assuming a pending application equals an active license. Agents submit Form 2.13 and begin showing properties or writing offers the same week. Maryland law is unambiguous: you may not perform any licensable activity until MREC issues your license number. Processing times vary from two to six weeks depending on Commission workload and whether any documentation is missing.

The second mistake involves failing to affiliate with a Maryland broker before applying. Form 2.13 requires a Maryland broker's name and license number. Some agents attempt to file without this, hoping to secure a broker after approval. The application will be returned incomplete, resetting the clock entirely.

Third, agents forget Maryland's continuing education requirement applies to non-resident licensees with equal force. The 15-hour biennial requirement—including the mandatory 3-hour Maryland legislative update course—is not optional for cooperative license holders. Missing the deadline results in automatic license expiration, not suspension. Reactivation after expiration requires a new application, a new fee, and potentially retaking the state exam. For a broader look at how continuing education obligations interact with licensing, the framework is [similar to what Washington State enforces](/blog/washington-continuing-education-requirements-2026).

Fourth, agents operating under cooperative agreements fail to disclose their non-resident status to consumers. While Maryland does not mandate a specific disclosure form for non-resident agents, the general duty of disclosure under Code of Maryland Regulations (COMAR) 09.11.02.03 requires agents to provide all material facts. A consumer's reasonable expectation that their agent is locally licensed and locally supervised is a material fact.

Fifth, agents assume their home-state disciplinary record stays home. MREC asks about disciplinary history on Form 2.13 and cross-checks through the ARELLO Disciplinary Action Reporting System. Omitting or misrepresenting prior discipline is grounds for denial and potential fraud charges.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers sponsoring non-resident agents carry distinct responsibilities under Maryland law. The [supervisory structures that apply in other states](/blog/washington-teams-supervision-rules-2026) offer a useful comparison, but Maryland's requirements have their own teeth.

**Every Maryland broker holding a cooperative licensee must verify three things before that agent writes their first offer.** First, confirm MREC has issued an active license number—not just received an application. Second, confirm the agent's home-state license remains in good standing, because Maryland's cooperative license is derivative; if the home license lapses, the Maryland license automatically terminates under §17-305(c). Third, confirm E&O coverage meets Maryland thresholds and names the broker or the brokerage entity.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Consequence of Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Verify MREC license status | Before each transaction | Broker disciplinary action under §17-322 |
| Confirm home-state license active | Quarterly minimum | Automatic Maryland license termination |
| E&O insurance verification | Annually or at policy renewal | Agent cannot lawfully practice; broker liable for gap |
| CE completion tracking | Every 2-year cycle | License expiration; transactions during lapse are violations |
| Consumer disclosure compliance | Every transaction | COMAR 09.11.02.03 violation; potential consumer complaints |

Brokers should also maintain a written policy addressing cooperative licensees. This policy should specify how the broker will supervise transactions conducted by agents whose primary office is in another state. MREC has signaled through informal guidance letters that remote supervision of non-resident agents requires documented check-ins, transaction file reviews, and clear escalation procedures for consumer complaints.

## Portability vs. Reciprocity: Why the Distinction Matters in 2026

The real estate industry often conflates these terms, but they describe different frameworks. Reciprocity means two states have a mutual agreement—if State A honors State B's license, State B honors State A's. Maryland does not participate in any mutual recognition agreement as of 2026. Portability, by contrast, refers to the ability of an agent to obtain licensure in a new state through a streamlined process that recognizes prior education and experience.

Maryland's system is portability without reciprocity. You can bring your credentials in, but Maryland does not promise to honor them without its own review process. And no other state is obligated to honor a Maryland license through any formal compact.

> "A non-resident applicant shall be eligible for licensure without completing the prelicensing education requirement only if the Commission determines that the applicant's home state requirements are substantially equivalent." — Md. Code, Bus. Occ. & Prof. §17-305(b)

This distinction matters operationally. Agents licensed in states with lower education thresholds—some require as few as 40 prelicensing hours—may not qualify for the education waiver. In those cases, the agent must either complete Maryland's 60-hour prelicensing requirement or pass both the national and state portions of the exam to demonstrate competency.

## Practical Steps for Agents Planning Cross-Border Work in 2026

Start the application process at least eight weeks before you anticipate conducting any Maryland transaction. Gather your certified license history, identify and confirm a Maryland supervising broker, secure compliant E&O coverage, and submit Form 2.13 with the $160 fee to MREC. Track your application status through the Maryland Department of Labor's online licensing portal.

If you are already holding a cooperative license, calendar your CE deadline now. The 15-hour requirement with the 3-hour Maryland legislative update is non-negotiable, and the [consequences of missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) extend well beyond a lapsed license—they can unwind closed transactions and expose you to commission disgorgement claims.
