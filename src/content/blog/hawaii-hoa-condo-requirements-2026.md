## Understanding Hawaii HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Hawaii's condominium market accounts for a staggering proportion of residential sales, particularly on Oahu where vertical living dominates the housing stock. That concentration makes the **Hawaii hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** one of the most consequential compliance areas for agents working the islands. Getting the disclosures wrong does not just create liability — it hands buyers an open-ended escape hatch from the deal.

The governing framework sits primarily within Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 514B, the Hawaii Condominium Property Act. This statute applies to every condominium unit resale where an Association of Apartment Owners (AOAO) exists. Separate rules under HRS Chapter 421J cover planned community associations (what mainlanders typically call HOAs), though many agents conflate the two regimes and expose themselves to gaps in compliance.

The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC), housed under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), enforces licensing standards and can discipline agents who fail to facilitate proper disclosure delivery. Understanding which documents must move, when, and from whom is not optional — it is the baseline of competent practice.

## The Statutory Framework: HRS 514B and 421J

**HRS Section 514B-154 is the engine of condo resale disclosures.** It requires the AOAO to furnish a disclosure document to any prospective purchaser upon request. The document must include the association's financial statements, the declaration, bylaws, house rules, current budget, any pending special assessments, outstanding litigation, and insurance coverage details.

For planned community associations governed by HRS Chapter 421J, Section 421J-14 imposes a parallel obligation. The association must provide its governing documents, financial records, and information about pending claims or litigation. Agents frequently overlook this statute because it lacks the specificity of 514B, but the disclosure obligation is just as real.

The distinction matters because some developments fall under 514B, some under 421J, and some under both. A mixed-use project with condominium property regime governance and a master planned community overlay requires agents to verify which statutory framework controls disclosure — or deliver documents under both.

## Specific Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

Hawaii does not use a single state-mandated HOA disclosure form the way some mainland states do. Instead, agents must manage multiple documents from different sources simultaneously. Here is how the core disclosure package breaks down for a standard condo resale:

| Document | Source | Governing Statute | Delivery Deadline |
|----------|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Seller's Real Property Disclosure (Form RR-105) | Seller via agent | HRS 508D | Before acceptance or per contract |
| AOAO Disclosure Document | Association | HRS 514B-154 | Within 10 days of request |
| Condominium Public Report (if applicable) | Developer/HIREC | HRS 514B-51 | Prior to binding contract |
| Planned Community Disclosure | Association | HRS 421J-14 | Upon request |
| Association Financial Audit | Association | HRS 514B-148 | Included in 514B-154 package |

Form RR-105, published by the Hawaii Association of Realtors, captures seller-side disclosures including knowledge of HOA disputes, special assessments, and pending rule changes. The [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/wyoming-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) vary state by state, but Hawaii's version explicitly prompts for association-related information that sellers frequently leave blank or answer incorrectly.

Agents should also be aware of HIREC's Condominium Governance Registry. Every AOAO in Hawaii must register with HIREC and file annual reports. If an association is not registered, that itself is a red flag that should be disclosed to buyers and documented in the transaction file.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Timing

The buyer's cancellation window is where most deals live or die in Hawaii condo transactions. Under [HRS Section 514B-86](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/hrscurrent/Vol12_Ch0501-0588/HRS0514B/HRS_0514B-0086.htm), a buyer of a condo unit may cancel the purchase agreement within 30 calendar days after receiving all required disclosures. This is not 30 days from contract signing — it is 30 days from the last required document hitting the buyer's hands.

**If any portion of the required disclosure package is missing or incomplete, the 30-day clock never starts.** This means a buyer could theoretically rescind weeks into escrow if the AOAO dragged its feet or the seller failed to request the 514B-154 document promptly. Agents who assume the general inspection contingency period covers this exposure are wrong.

The cancellation must be in writing and delivered to the seller. No reason is required. The buyer receives a full refund of all deposits. This right cannot be waived in the purchase contract — any provision attempting to shorten or eliminate the 30-day window is void under the statute.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make

Five recurring errors account for most of the disclosure-related complaints HIREC investigates in condo and HOA transactions.

**First, agents fail to request the 514B-154 document early enough.** The AOAO has 10 days to produce it, but many associations take longer, particularly smaller self-managed buildings. If an agent waits until week two of escrow to request the document, the buyer's 30-day cancellation window may extend well past the anticipated closing date, creating scheduling chaos and potential deal collapse.

Second, listing agents treat the seller's RR-105 responses about the association as a substitute for the actual AOAO disclosure. They are not interchangeable. The seller's knowledge about special assessments does not replace the association's official financial statements. Both must be delivered independently, and both trigger different cancellation rights.

Third, agents confuse the condominium public report requirement with the resale disclosure requirement. The public report under HRS 514B-51 applies to developer sales of new units. Resales require the 514B-154 package instead. Delivering the wrong document set — or delivering neither because the agent assumes the other applies — leaves the transaction exposed.

Fourth, buyer's agents fail to document when their client actually received the disclosure package. Without a signed acknowledgment with a date, disputes about whether the 30-day window has expired become he-said-she-said battles. The acknowledgment should be a separate document, not buried in email threads.

Fifth, agents neglect the 421J disclosure entirely for townhome or planned community transactions because the property "doesn't look like a condo." The legal structure, not the architectural style, determines which statute applies. A single-family home within a planned community association still falls under 421J disclosure rules. Review the declaration of covenants before assuming disclosure obligations do not apply.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under HRS Section 467-1.6 for the acts of their agents. When a disclosure failure leads to a rescission, complaint, or lawsuit, HIREC investigates the broker's systems alongside the individual agent's conduct. A broker who cannot demonstrate active oversight risks their own license.

| Audit Item | What Broker Should Verify | Frequency |
|------------|---------------------------|-----------|
| 514B-154 request date | Logged within 3 days of mutual acceptance | Every condo transaction |
| RR-105 completion | All HOA/AOAO fields answered, not left blank | Every listing |
| Buyer acknowledgment of receipt | Signed and dated, stored in transaction file | Before closing |
| 421J applicability check | Declaration reviewed for planned community regime | Every non-condo attached sale |
| 30-day window calculation | Closing date set after window expiration | Transaction coordination review |

Brokers should implement a transaction checklist that flags condo and HOA sales at intake and routes them through an enhanced compliance track. The [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) principle applies doubly here because the timing requirements are unforgiving and the cancellation remedy is absolute.

Quarterly file audits should sample at least 10 percent of closed condo transactions and verify that all five items above are present. If a broker discovers a pattern of missing acknowledgments or late disclosure requests, that is a training problem that must be addressed before it becomes a HIREC enforcement action. ## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

An agent who fails to facilitate proper disclosure delivery faces three distinct categories of risk. The first is transactional: the buyer can cancel and recover all deposits, potentially after the seller has already committed to purchasing a replacement property. The second is regulatory: HIREC can impose fines up to $2,500 per violation, require additional education, suspend, or revoke a license under HRS 467-14. The third is civil: buyers who discover undisclosed defects or association problems post-closing may sue the agent for negligent misrepresentation or failure to disclose material facts.

**Hawaii courts have consistently held that agents cannot disclaim responsibility for disclosure failures by blaming the association's slow response.** The agent's duty is to request the documents promptly, follow up diligently, and advise the client about the status of the disclosure package. Passivity is not a defense.

Errors and omissions insurance will typically cover civil claims, but policy exclusions for "known violations of statute" may apply if the agent demonstrably ignored the disclosure timeline. Brokers should confirm their E&O policies explicitly cover 514B-related claims and consider whether their deductibles adequately protect against the cost of defending these actions.

## Practical Workflow for 2026 Compliance

The most reliable approach is to build disclosure management into your transaction coordination workflow from day one. On the listing side, request the 514B-154 or 421J disclosure document before the property hits the MLS. Pre-packaging this document eliminates the timing risk entirely and gives buyers confidence that the seller is operating transparently.

On the buyer side, confirm receipt of every required document in a single dated acknowledgment form. Track the 30-day window as a hard deadline in your transaction management system and do not schedule closing within that window. If the buyer needs to close quickly, the only safe path is delivering all disclosures before or simultaneous with contract execution.
