## Understanding Vermont Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Vermont treats earnest money as a trust obligation, not a casual deposit. If you hold a Vermont real estate license and handle buyer or seller funds, the rules under **Vermont earnest money & escrow laws 2026** carry real teeth — license suspension, civil liability, and potential criminal exposure for mishandling trust funds. The Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC), operating under the Office of Professional Regulation, enforces these requirements through audits, complaint investigations, and disciplinary proceedings.

The governing statute is [26 V.S.A. Chapter 41](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/26/041), which lays out licensing requirements, fiduciary duties, and the consequences for violations. VREC's administrative rules further detail how escrow accounts must be maintained, how disputes are resolved, and what documentation brokers must retain. Every agent working under a Vermont brokerage should know these rules as well as they know their commission splits — because getting them wrong can end a career faster than a failed inspection kills a deal.

## How Vermont Defines and Regulates Earnest Money

Vermont does not set a statutory minimum or maximum for earnest money deposits. The amount is always a negotiated term between buyer and seller, typically memorialized on the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) Purchase and Sale Contract, commonly referenced as the **VAR Form 400** series. Market norms in Vermont generally range from one to three percent of the purchase price, though rural transactions and land deals sometimes use flat-dollar amounts.

**What the law does mandate is how those funds are handled once they change hands.** Under VREC rules, earnest money received by a licensee must be deposited promptly into a designated escrow or trust account held by the supervising broker. "Promptly" has been interpreted by the Commission to mean within three business days of contract execution, unless the purchase agreement states a different timeline. Holding a check in a desk drawer for a week is not just sloppy — it is a regulatory violation.

The escrow account must be a demand deposit account at a federally insured financial institution located in Vermont. Brokers cannot commingle escrow funds with personal or business operating funds under any circumstances. Even a brief, accidental commingling during a bank transfer can trigger a Commission inquiry.

## Key Forms and Contract Provisions Agents Must Know

Vermont residential transactions rely heavily on VAR-published forms, and agents need to understand how earnest money provisions appear across multiple documents. The **VAR Form 400 (Purchase and Sale Contract)** includes specific fields for earnest money amount, deposit deadline, and the identity of the escrow holder. A separate **VAR Form 402 (Addendum)** may modify deposit terms when negotiations shift after initial execution.

The **VAR Form 401 (Lead Paint Disclosure)** does not directly address earnest money, but missing this form can trigger contract rescission rights that put deposits in limbo. Similarly, the **Vermont Seller's Property Disclosure Form** — required under 27 V.S.A. Section 376 — can create grounds for a buyer to demand deposit return if material facts were omitted or misrepresented.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Earnest Money Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| VAR Form 400 | Purchase and Sale Contract | Sets deposit amount, deadline, escrow holder |
| VAR Form 402 | Contract Addendum | Modifies deposit terms post-execution |
| VAR Form 401 | Lead Paint Disclosure | Rescission rights may affect deposit return |
| Seller's Property Disclosure (27 V.S.A. § 376) | Condition disclosure | Misrepresentation can trigger refund claims |
| Escrow Agreement (brokerage-specific) | Trust account terms | Defines disbursement and dispute procedures |

Agents who skip form fields or leave the escrow holder blank are creating ambiguity that becomes expensive when disputes arise. Every blank on those forms exists for a reason, and VREC investigators will examine completed forms during any complaint proceeding.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**License discipline in Vermont is not theoretical — VREC publishes disciplinary actions, and the consequences are public record.** Under 26 V.S.A. § 2296, the Commission can revoke, suspend, or condition a license for mishandling escrow funds, failing to account for deposits, or commingling trust money. Fines can reach several thousand dollars per violation, and the Commission has authority to require restitution to harmed parties.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money disappears due to agent negligence or misconduct can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and potentially fraud. Vermont courts have awarded damages that include not only the lost deposit but consequential damages — think the cost of lost housing opportunities, temporary rentals, or price increases on replacement properties. Brokerages carry errors and omissions insurance for a reason, but E&O policies often exclude intentional acts or regulatory violations.

Criminal exposure is the third rail. Misappropriating escrow funds — even temporarily "borrowing" from the trust account — can constitute embezzlement under Vermont criminal law. Prosecutors do not need to prove intent to permanently deprive; using funds for an unauthorized purpose, even briefly, is enough. If you think this sounds extreme, review the VREC disciplinary archives — cases involving trust account violations appear regularly.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make With Earnest Money

Five errors come up repeatedly in VREC complaints and brokerage audits. Knowing them is the first step toward avoiding them.

**First, agents accept earnest money checks made payable to themselves rather than the brokerage escrow account.** This creates an immediate commingling risk the moment the check is deposited. All earnest money checks should be payable to the brokerage or the designated escrow entity named in the purchase agreement. Agents who endorse checks over to the brokerage account are adding an unnecessary step that invites scrutiny.

Second, agents fail to document the deposit timeline. VREC expects a clear paper trail showing when the check was received, when it was delivered to the broker, and when it was deposited into the escrow account. Without timestamped records, agents cannot defend themselves if a buyer or seller alleges mishandling — and the burden of proof falls on the licensee, not the complainant. A simple email confirmation to the broker at time of receipt solves this problem and costs nothing.

Third, agents misunderstand contingency deadlines and prematurely authorize deposit releases. Vermont purchase contracts typically tie earnest money forfeiture or return to specific contingency expiration dates — inspection, financing, appraisal. When an agent tells a closing coordinator to release funds before a contingency has formally expired or been waived in writing, the result can be a dispute that [stalls or kills the deal entirely](/blog/where-deals-break-down). The contract language controls, not verbal agreements between the parties.

Fourth, agents neglect to update escrow instructions when contract amendments change the deposit terms. If the parties agree to increase the earnest money deposit via addendum, but nobody notifies the escrow holder, the broker's records will not match the contract — a discrepancy that surfaces during audits and can suggest sloppy accounting or worse.

Fifth, agents operating on teams assume someone else handled the deposit. Shared responsibilities without clear assignment create gaps, and VREC does not accept "I thought my teammate did it" as a defense. Every transaction needs one identified person responsible for tracking the earnest money from receipt through disbursement. This is exactly the kind of [deadline tracking failure](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that compounds across a busy book of business.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Broker responsibility in Vermont goes beyond signing commission checks and keeping the lights on. Under 26 V.S.A. § 2295, the supervising broker is personally accountable for escrow account compliance within their brokerage. If an agent under your supervision mishandles trust funds, you share liability — period.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Bank reconciliation | Monthly | Escrow account balance matches sum of all pending transaction deposits |
| Deposit timeliness | Per transaction | Funds deposited within three business days of receipt |
| Check payee verification | Per transaction | All checks payable to brokerage escrow, not individual agents |
| Disbursement authorization | Per transaction | Written authorization from all parties or court order before any release |
| Record retention | Ongoing | Transaction files retained for minimum of six years per VREC rules |

**Monthly reconciliations are not optional — they are the single most important safeguard a broker has.** The reconciliation must compare the escrow account bank statement to the brokerage's transaction ledger and identify any discrepancy, no matter how small. A $50 variance is not a rounding error; it is a red flag that demands immediate investigation. Brokers who delegate reconciliation to unlicensed staff must still personally review and sign off on each month's report.

Brokers should also conduct periodic internal audits of deposit documentation. Pull five to ten random transaction files per quarter and verify that each one includes a copy of the earnest money check (or wire confirmation), a deposit receipt from the bank, and a timestamped record of when the agent delivered the funds to the office. If your brokerage does not have a standardized intake process for earnest money, build one now — before VREC builds your case file for you.

Retention requirements matter as well. Vermont mandates that transaction records be kept for a minimum of six years. This includes escrow documentation, even for deals that fell apart before closing. Destroyed records during an active investigation can result in adverse inferences — the Commission may assume the worst about missing files.

## Disputed Deposits: How Vermont Handles Earnest Money Conflicts

When buyer and seller disagree about who gets the earnest money, the broker holding the funds is stuck in the middle. Vermont does not have a statutory interpleader requirement for real estate brokerages, but filing an interpleader action in Vermont Superior Court is the safest path when parties cannot reach agreement. An interpleader deposits the disputed funds with the court and asks a judge to determine who gets them, insulating the broker from liability to either side.

Brokers who unilaterally release disputed funds to one party risk a lawsuit from the other and a VREC complaint on top of it. The only safe disbursements are those made pursuant to written mutual agreement signed by both buyer and seller, a court order, or a fully executed release form — such as the **VAR Form 406 (Mutual Release)** — that both parties have signed without duress.

Some Vermont attorneys recommend including a mediation or arbitration clause in the purchase agreement to handle deposit disputes outside of court. These clauses can accelerate resolution, but they must be carefully drafted to comply with Vermont arbitration law under 12 V.S.A. Chapter 192. Agents should not draft these clauses themselves; this is attorney territory, and unauthorized practice of law carries its own penalties.
