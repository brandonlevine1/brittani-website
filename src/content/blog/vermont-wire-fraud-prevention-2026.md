# Vermont Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Do Now

Wire fraud has cost real estate consumers over $446 million nationally in a single year, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Vermont is not immune, and the state has sharpened its expectations for how agents handle this threat. Understanding Vermont wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer a best-practice suggestion — it is a compliance obligation that carries real consequences for agents who ignore it.

The Vermont Real Estate Commission, operating under 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41, has made clear through updated guidance and enforcement posture that licensees bear an affirmative duty to warn clients about wire fraud risks. This article breaks down exactly what you need to do, what forms to use, and what happens if you fall short.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind Vermont's Wire Fraud Rules

Vermont does not have a single standalone wire fraud statute aimed exclusively at real estate transactions. Instead, the compliance obligation sits at the intersection of several authorities. The Vermont Real Estate Commission enforces licensee conduct standards under [26 V.S.A. Chapter 41](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/26/041), which includes the duty to protect client interests, disclose material facts, and avoid negligent misrepresentation.

The Commission's administrative rules, found in the Vermont Secretary of State's Professional Regulation division, require agents to act competently and to inform clients of known risks that could affect a transaction. Wire fraud qualifies. The Commission has treated the failure to warn a client about wire fraud the same way it treats any failure to disclose a material risk — as a potential violation of the licensee's fiduciary and statutory duties.

At the federal level, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) have issued guidance that indirectly affects real estate practitioners. Vermont's attorney general has also cited the Vermont Consumer Protection Act, 9 V.S.A. Chapter 63, in cases involving fraudulent wire transfers, which means agents could face scrutiny beyond just their license.

## What Forms Vermont Agents Must Use

**The primary document agents need in every transaction file is the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) Form 801, the Wire Fraud Advisory.** This form was updated in late 2025 to reflect current FBI guidance and Vermont Real Estate Commission expectations. Form 801 serves as the written disclosure that warns buyers and sellers about the risks of email-based wire instruction fraud.

Form 801 must be signed by the client and retained in the transaction file. It is typically presented at or before the signing of the purchase and sale agreement, not at closing when it is too late. Some brokerages also require it as part of their listing packet for sellers who will receive wire proceeds.

In addition to Form 801, the VAR Form 800 series includes related disclosures that touch on cybersecurity and data handling. Agents who work with out-of-state buyers wiring funds should also cross-reference the wire verification processes used by the closing attorney's office. Vermont is an attorney-closing state, and the closing attorney typically provides separate wire instructions — but that does not relieve the agent of the duty to provide Form 801.

| Document | Purpose | When to Present | Who Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| VAR Form 801 (Wire Fraud Advisory) | Written warning about wire fraud risks | At or before P&S signing | Buyer and/or Seller |
| Closing attorney wire instructions | Verified bank details for funds transfer | Prior to closing, via secure channel | Buyer (acknowledged) |
| Brokerage internal wire fraud addendum | Firm-specific compliance supplement | Varies by brokerage policy | All parties as required |
| VAR Form 400 series (Agency Disclosure) | Agency relationship and duties | First substantive contact | All parties |

If your brokerage has its own supplemental wire fraud advisory, use it in addition to Form 801 — not as a replacement. The Commission recognizes Form 801 as the standard, and omitting it creates a gap that a disciplinary review will notice.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of skipping wire fraud prevention steps in Vermont are not hypothetical. An agent who fails to provide a wire fraud advisory could face disciplinary action from the Vermont Real Estate Commission, including license suspension, fines, or revocation under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296. The Commission has broad authority to sanction licensees for incompetence, negligence, or failure to protect the interests of the public.

**Beyond license discipline, agents face direct civil liability.** If a buyer wires $300,000 to a fraudster because nobody warned them about email spoofing, the buyer's attorney will look at every party in the transaction. The agent who failed to deliver Form 801 becomes a target. Even if the closing attorney's email was the one compromised, the agent's omission of a written warning shifts contributory negligence onto the licensee.

Errors and omissions insurance may not save you. Many E&O carriers now include wire fraud compliance as a condition of coverage. If the carrier can show you did not follow your brokerage's wire fraud policy or the VAR-recommended Form 801 process, they may deny the claim entirely. That leaves the agent personally exposed for the full loss — which in Vermont's residential market can easily exceed $400,000.

Deal cancellation is also a risk. A buyer who discovers after the fact that no wire fraud advisory was provided may argue they were inadequately informed and seek to rescind the contract, especially if they suffered an actual loss. While rescission is not automatic, the absence of Form 801 weakens the agent's position in any dispute.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make on Wire Fraud Prevention

Knowing the rules is one thing. Applying them consistently across every transaction is where agents stumble. Here are the most frequent errors the Vermont Real Estate Commission and brokerages see.

**First, agents treat Form 801 as optional.** Some agents believe that because the closing attorney handles wire instructions, the agent has no separate obligation. This is wrong. The agent's duty to disclose material risks exists independently of the closing attorney's role. Form 801 is the agent's own compliance step, and skipping it exposes the agent regardless of what the attorney does.

Second, agents present the wire fraud advisory too late. Handing a buyer Form 801 at the closing table — or worse, emailing a PDF after closing — defeats the purpose. The advisory is meant to change behavior before the buyer receives wire instructions. If the buyer has already wired funds by the time they see the form, the disclosure served no protective function, and a court will view it as a checkbox exercise rather than genuine compliance.

Third, agents relay wire instructions via email. This is the single most dangerous thing an agent can do. Even if the agent received verified instructions from the closing attorney, forwarding those details via unencrypted email creates an interception opportunity. The correct practice is to tell clients to call the closing attorney's office directly, using a phone number obtained independently — not from any email — to verify all wiring details. For a deeper look at this process, see our post on the [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process).

Fourth, agents fail to document the advisory in the transaction file. A signed Form 801 that never makes it into the file is the same as no Form 801 at all during an audit. Every signed copy must be scanned, uploaded, and retained according to your brokerage's document retention policy and the Commission's record-keeping requirements under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299.

Fifth, agents do not update clients when closing is delayed. Wire fraud schemes often exploit timing confusion. If a closing date shifts, the buyer may receive a spoofed email with "updated" wire instructions. Agents who fail to re-alert clients during a delay leave them vulnerable during the exact moment fraudsters are most likely to strike.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability in Vermont under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296(a), which means a single agent's wire fraud compliance failure can become the broker's problem. The Commission holds brokers accountable for establishing, communicating, and enforcing transaction policies — and wire fraud prevention is now squarely within that expectation.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|---|---|---|
| Verify Form 801 in every transaction file | Per transaction | Signed copy in file before closing |
| Review agent email practices for wire instruction forwarding | Quarterly | Email policy acknowledgment on file |
| Confirm cybersecurity training completion | Annually | Certificate or LMS record per agent |
| Test brokerage email domain security (SPF/DKIM/DMARC) | Semi-annually | IT audit report |
| Update brokerage wire fraud policy manual | Annually or upon regulatory change | Dated policy with agent sign-off |

Brokers should conduct spot audits of transaction files at least quarterly to confirm Form 801 is present and properly signed. An annual audit is not frequent enough — wire fraud patterns evolve faster than that. Any file missing the advisory should trigger an immediate correction and a documented conversation with the responsible agent.

Cybersecurity training should be mandatory for every agent in the brokerage, not just recommended. The Vermont Real Estate Commission's continuing education requirements under Administrative Rule 15.3 allow cybersecurity topics to count toward CE credits, so there is no excuse for agents to skip it. Brokers who can show a documented training program are in a far stronger position if a fraud incident occurs.

Brokers also need to audit their own technology. If the brokerage email domain lacks proper SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records, it is trivially easy for a fraudster to spoof a brokerage email address. This is a brokerage-level infrastructure issue, not something individual agents can fix. The cost of configuring email authentication is negligible compared to the cost of a single compromised transaction.

## How Vermont Compares to Neighboring States

Vermont's attorney-closing requirement sets it apart from states where title companies handle disbursement independently. This adds a layer of professional oversight but does not eliminate agent responsibility. In fact, the attorney-closing model sometimes gives agents a false sense of security, leading them to assume the attorney's office has wire fraud prevention fully covered.

New Hampshire and Maine have similar attorney-involvement norms but have moved faster on explicit wire fraud disclosure mandates at the regulatory level. Vermont's approach has been guidance-driven rather than statute-specific, relying on the Commission's broad authority over licensee conduct rather than enacting a wire-fraud-only law. This means agents cannot point to the absence of a specific wire fraud statute and claim ignorance — the duty exists under existing fiduciary and competency standards.

Massachusetts enacted specific wire fraud notification requirements through its Board of Registration of Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons, creating a clear precedent that Vermont regulators have referenced in their own guidance materials. Vermont agents who also hold licenses in neighboring states should ensure they meet the highest standard across all jurisdictions where they practice.
