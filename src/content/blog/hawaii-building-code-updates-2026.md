## Hawaii Building Code Updates 2026: The Shift That Matters

The Hawaii building code updates 2026 represent the most significant overhaul to residential construction standards the state has adopted in nearly a decade. The Hawaii State Building Code Council, operating under HRS Chapter 107, has formally adopted the 2021 International Building Code (IBC) and the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) with Hawaii-specific amendments that took effect for all permit applications filed on or after January 1, 2026. For agents working active listings or representing buyers eyeing properties with recent renovations, these changes create new disclosure obligations and due diligence requirements that did not exist eighteen months ago.

The four counties—Honolulu, Maui, Kauai, and Hawaii County—each maintain their own building departments, but the state code sets the floor. County-level amendments can be more restrictive, never less. If you are listing a property on the Big Island with a post-2020 addition and the permit was pulled under the old 2012 IBC adoption, the work may technically comply with the code in effect at that time, but buyer expectations and lender requirements are already shifting toward the 2026 standards.

## What Actually Changed in the 2026 Adoption

**The 2026 amendments focus on three areas that directly affect residential transactions: wind resistance in coastal and elevated zones, energy efficiency under the Hawaii State Energy Conservation Code, and structural resilience in volcanic and seismic hazard areas.** Hawaii's amendments to IBC Section 1609 now require residential structures in Wind Speed Zone V (common along the Windward sides of all islands) to meet 150 mph basic wind speed design standards, up from 130 mph under the previous adoption.

The energy efficiency changes derive from the adoption of IECC 2021 with tropical climate zone amendments. New residential construction must now meet a HERS index of 55 or below, and renovations exceeding 50% of the conditioned floor area trigger compliance with current energy code. This matters for agents because buyers financing with FHA or VA loans are increasingly seeing appraisers flag non-compliant energy systems as conditions requiring repair.

Volcanic hazard zone amendments under the Hawaii County-specific overlay require structures in Lava Zones 1 and 2 to meet enhanced foundation anchoring standards. Properties in Puna and parts of Kona that obtained permits before 2026 are grandfathered, but any modification requiring a new permit now triggers full compliance with the updated standards.

| Code Area | Previous Standard (2012 IBC) | 2026 Standard (2021 IBC + HI Amendments) | Impact on Transactions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind Resistance (Zone V) | 130 mph basic wind speed | 150 mph basic wind speed | Older homes may need retrofit for insurability |
| Energy Efficiency (HERS) | No residential HERS requirement | HERS index 55 or below for new construction | Renovation triggers for 50%+ floor area |
| Lava Zone 1-2 Foundations | Standard foundation per IRC | Enhanced anchoring and breakaway requirements | Permit modifications trigger full compliance |
| Flood Zone Construction | FEMA minimum standards | FEMA + 2 ft freeboard (HI amendment) | Elevated construction costs, disclosure issues |
| Solar Readiness | Solar water heater required | Solar PV readiness + storage-ready wiring | New homes must be pre-wired for battery storage |

## Disclosure Obligations Under HRS Chapter 508D

Hawaii's Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement remains the primary disclosure vehicle, and the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC) updated its guidance in early 2026 to address building code compliance questions. The Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement (form available through HIREC) requires sellers to disclose known building code violations, unpermitted improvements, and any work performed without required inspections.

**Agents must understand the distinction between the seller's disclosure duty and the agent's independent duty under HRS Section 467-14(20), which prohibits licensees from making any misrepresentation concerning a property.** If you walk through a listing and observe an obvious unpermitted addition—no permit sticker on the electrical panel, visible construction that does not match the tax map key (TMK) records, or a converted garage without egress windows—you cannot simply ignore it because the seller did not disclose it on the form.

The standard Purchase Contract (DROA, or Deposit Receipt Offer and Acceptance, Hawaii Association of Realtors Standard Form RR) includes contingency provisions in Section G that allow buyers to investigate the property's condition. Agents representing buyers should ensure the inspection contingency period includes sufficient time to pull permit histories from the relevant county building department. This is not optional guidance—it is [the kind of deadline management that prevents deals from collapsing](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

HIREC has enforcement authority under HRS Chapter 467 to discipline licensees who fail to disclose material facts about a property's condition, including building code compliance issues. Disciplinary actions range from a formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation. Civil liability is the larger financial risk.

A buyer who purchases a property with undisclosed code violations can pursue damages under HRS Section 508D-16, which allows for recovery of actual damages plus attorney's fees if the seller (or the seller's agent) knowingly failed to disclose a material fact. Hawaii courts have consistently held that building code violations affecting habitability, safety, or insurability constitute material facts. The 2019 Hawaii Supreme Court decision in *Flores v. Campbell* reinforced that agents cannot hide behind the disclaimer that they are not building code experts when they observe conditions that a reasonable licensee should recognize as potentially non-compliant.

Deal cancellation is the most common immediate consequence. When a buyer's inspector identifies unpermitted work or code violations during the contingency period, and the seller cannot or will not cure, the buyer exercises their contingency rights under the DROA and receives their earnest money deposit back. The listing agent loses the commission, the seller loses the buyer, and the property returns to market with a stigma that makes the next offer lower.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Issues

The first error agents make is assuming that "grandfathered" status means a property has no disclosure obligations. A home built in 1985 complies with the code in effect at its time of construction, but if the owner added a bedroom, converted the carport, or installed a new electrical subpanel without permits, those improvements are judged against the code in effect when the work was done—not when the home was originally built. Agents routinely list these properties without investigating permit histories, assuming that age alone creates a blanket exemption.

**The second mistake is conflating a home inspection with a code compliance review.** Home inspectors in Hawaii operate under HRS Chapter 444-25.5 and follow Standards of Practice that explicitly state they are not performing code compliance inspections. When a buyer's agent tells their client that "the inspector will catch any code issues," they are creating a false sense of security. A separate permit history review and, in some cases, a consultation with the county building department is the appropriate due diligence step.

Third, agents frequently fail to document their recommendations to clients in writing. If you advise a seller to disclose a known unpermitted improvement and they refuse, you need that conversation memorialized in an email or a written advisory. Verbal recommendations provide no protection when the buyer files a complaint with HIREC six months after closing. This documentation discipline is precisely [why checklists and systematic processes outperform memory alone](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Fourth, buyer's agents on the neighbor islands sometimes skip permit verification because county offices are understaffed and response times are slow. Hawaii County's Building Division can take two to three weeks to respond to permit history requests. That timeline needs to be factored into the contingency period at contract formation—not discovered after the inspection deadline has already passed.

Fifth, agents working condominiums assume the AOAO (Association of Apartment Owners) is responsible for building code compliance on individual units. While the AOAO handles common elements, interior modifications by individual owners—kitchen remodels, bathroom additions, lanai enclosures—remain the unit owner's responsibility to permit. The [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) vary by state, but Hawaii's statute places the burden squarely on the unit owner for interior improvements.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry supervisory liability under HRS Section 467-1.6 for the acts of their sponsored licensees. HIREC's administrative rules (HAR Title 16, Chapter 99) require brokers to maintain policies and procedures that ensure compliance with disclosure laws. Building code issues are not carved out—they fall under the general duty to supervise.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Red Flag Indicators |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form completion review | Every listing before MLS entry | Blank or "unknown" answers on improvement questions |
| Permit history recommendation documentation | Every listing with visible additions | No email or advisory letter in file |
| Contingency period adequacy check | Every buyer offer submitted | Inspection periods under 15 days on neighbor islands |
| Unpermitted work response protocol | When identified pre-closing | No written seller communication in file |
| Agent training on code adoption updates | Annually (minimum) | No CE verification for code-related coursework |

Brokerages should require agents to include a written recommendation to sellers at the listing appointment that any known unpermitted work be disclosed on the Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement. This recommendation should be documented in the listing file regardless of whether the seller follows the advice. If the seller refuses to disclose and the agent has reason to believe a material code violation exists, the broker must evaluate whether to continue the listing relationship.

HIREC's [Real Estate Commission administrative proceedings database](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) shows that failure to supervise is the most common basis for broker discipline in disclosure-related complaints. The Commission does not accept the defense that a broker was unaware of an agent's omission if reasonable supervisory procedures would have caught it.

## How the 2026 Code Interacts With Insurance and Lending

Insurance carriers operating in Hawaii have already begun adjusting their underwriting criteria to reflect the 2026 wind resistance standards. Properties in Wind Speed Zone V that do not meet the 150 mph design standard may face non-renewal at their next policy cycle, even if the home was code-compliant at the time of construction. Agents listing older coastal properties should proactively address insurability in their listing strategy.

Lenders are following suit. FHA's Minimum Property Requirements and VA's Minimum Property Standards both reference local building codes as the baseline. An appraiser evaluating a property with obvious non-compliant conditions—missing hurricane straps visible in the attic, non-tempered glass in bathrooms, or unpermitted electrical work—may condition the loan on repairs that bring the property into compliance with current code. This creates a scenario where deals fall apart not because of inspection contingencies but because of appraisal conditions that neither party anticipated.
