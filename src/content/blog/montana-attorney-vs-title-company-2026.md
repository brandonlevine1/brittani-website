## Montana Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of Montana attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 matters more than most agents realize. Montana is one of the states that does not mandate attorney involvement in residential real estate closings, but that single fact alone does not protect you from liability. Understanding exactly who must sit at the table — and who may sit at the table — separates agents who close cleanly from those who face board complaints.

Montana operates as a "title state," meaning a licensed title insurance company or agent typically conducts settlement. The Montana Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Department of Labor and Industry, governs agent conduct during closings. Missteps around unauthorized practice of law remain one of the top complaint categories the board processes each year.

## What Montana Law Actually Says About Closing Authority

**Montana Code Annotated (MCA) Title 33, Chapter 25 governs title insurance companies and their authority to facilitate closings.** Under MCA 33-25-301, a title insurer or its authorized agent may issue commitments, examine title, and close transactions in connection with the issuance of title insurance. This is the statutory basis for title company closings statewide.

There is no companion statute requiring attorney presence. Unlike states such as New York, Georgia, or South Carolina, Montana's legislature has never imposed an attorney-mandatory closing framework. The Montana Supreme Court addressed the boundary between title company activities and unauthorized practice of law in several advisory opinions, most notably holding that completion of standard form documents incidental to a title insurance transaction does not constitute the practice of law.

MCA 37-61-201 defines the unauthorized practice of law and makes it a misdemeanor. Agents must understand this line because any advice about legal rights, document drafting beyond fill-in-the-blank forms, or interpretation of contract clauses at closing could cross it.

## Title Company vs. Attorney: Side-by-Side Comparison for Montana Agents

The practical differences matter at the transaction level. Here is how closing functions break down between the two options Montana buyers and sellers may choose:

| Function | Title Company | Attorney |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| Title search and commitment | Yes — standard service | Yes — but often outsources to title company |
| Document preparation (standard forms) | Yes — deeds, affidavits, settlement statements | Yes — all documents including custom instruments |
| Legal advice to parties | No — prohibited | Yes — to their client only |
| Escrow and fund disbursement | Yes — regulated under MCA 33-25 | Yes — through trust account |
| Dispute resolution at closing table | No — must refer out | Yes — can advise client on options |
| Title insurance issuance | Yes — primary function | Only if also licensed as title agent |

Most Montana residential transactions close through a title company. Attorney closings are more common in complex commercial deals, estate sales, or situations involving boundary disputes and quiet title actions.

## The Forms Montana Agents Must Know

Montana's standard residential forms come from the Montana Association of Realtors (MAR). The key forms relevant to closing procedures include:

The MAR Residential Real Estate Purchase Agreement (Form 100) contains the closing provisions agents must follow, including the designation of the closing agent and timeline requirements. Form 100 Section 12 specifically addresses the closing process and which entity the parties select to handle settlement.

**Agents must ensure the closing agent designation in Form 100 matches the entity actually conducting closing — a mismatch creates ambiguity that can delay funding or trigger disputes.** The MAR Addendum for Title Insurance (Form 109) outlines the title commitment delivery timeline and objection procedures. Form 111, the Seller's Property Disclosure Statement, must be delivered before closing but is not a closing-table document.

The Montana Department of Insurance also requires title companies to comply with [ALTA settlement statement standards](https://www.alta.org/), which replaced the HUD-1 after TRID implementation. Agents who still reference HUD-1 in their communications risk confusing clients and title officers.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make at Closing

Five recurring errors surface in board complaints and E&O claims related to Montana closings. Each one is preventable with proper transaction management.

**First, agents answer legal questions at the closing table.** When a buyer asks whether they should sign a document or what a particular clause means legally, the correct response is to direct them to their own attorney. Agents who offer interpretations — even well-meaning ones — expose themselves to UPL complaints under MCA 37-61-201 and potential E&O claims if their interpretation proves wrong.

Second, agents fail to verify that the closing agent is properly licensed. MCA 33-25-301 requires title agents to hold active licenses with the Montana Commissioner of Securities and Insurance. An agent who closes through an unlicensed entity risks deal cancellation if the title insurer refuses to underwrite the policy.

Third, agents assume the title company will handle all deadline tracking. Montana's Form 100 places specific performance obligations on agents — including ensuring the title commitment is delivered within the contractual timeline and objections are raised within the specified period. Missed deadlines can result in waiver of title objections, leaving the buyer without recourse and the agent facing a negligence claim.

Fourth, agents mix earnest money handling with closing funds. Montana's trust account rules under ARM 24.210.641 require strict segregation. Agents who direct earnest money to their brokerage trust account but fail to coordinate timely transfer to the closing agent create last-minute funding gaps that delay settlement.

Fifth, agents neglect to confirm wire instructions through a separate verified channel. Wire fraud attempts targeting Montana closings have increased, and agents who simply forward unverified wiring details from email expose their clients — and themselves — to catastrophic loss. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Broker liability in Montana attaches directly to agent conduct at closing. Under MCA 37-51-321, the designated broker is responsible for supervising all licensed activities of affiliated agents, including closing-related communications and document handling.

**Brokers should audit three specific areas on a quarterly basis at minimum.** First, review every closed file to confirm the closing agent designation in Form 100 matches the entity that actually conducted settlement. Discrepancies signal sloppy file management that could unravel if a transaction is later disputed.

Second, audit agent communications from the final week before closing. Look for any language that could be construed as legal advice — phrases like "you should be fine signing that" or "this clause means you don't have to worry about it." These informal statements in text messages and emails are discoverable in litigation and form the basis of UPL complaints.

Third, verify that every transaction file contains evidence of confirmed wire instructions through a callback or secure portal — not just an email forward. The Montana Board of Realty Regulation has signaled increased scrutiny of wire fraud prevention practices, and brokers without documented protocols face heightened regulatory risk.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Closing agent license verification | Per transaction | Screenshot of active license from MT CSI database |
| Form 100 closing agent designation match | Per transaction | Signed form compared to settlement statement |
| Agent communication review (legal advice) | Quarterly | Random sample of 10% of closed files |
| Wire instruction verification protocol | Per transaction | Callback log or secure portal confirmation |
| Earnest money transfer timing | Per transaction | Trust account ledger showing transfer date |

Brokers who fail to maintain these audit trails face potential disciplinary action from the Board of Realty Regulation, including license suspension under ARM 24.210.625. The board has broad authority to hold brokers accountable for patterns of agent noncompliance.

## When an Attorney Should Be Involved — Even Though Montana Does Not Require One

The absence of a legal mandate does not mean attorneys are irrelevant to Montana closings. Agents should proactively recommend attorney involvement in specific scenarios rather than waiting for problems to surface.

Estate sales where the seller is a personal representative acting under letters testamentary benefit from attorney review. Transactions involving boundary disputes, easement questions, or mineral rights — particularly common in eastern Montana's energy-producing counties — demand legal analysis that no title company or agent should attempt. Short sales, foreclosure purchases, and transactions involving IRS liens also warrant attorney participation.

The agent's role is to identify these situations early and recommend counsel without themselves providing legal guidance. This distinction protects the agent under MCA 37-51-313, which outlines licensee duties, and aligns with MAR's published best practices for agent conduct. ## Liability Consequences for Noncompliant Agents

The consequences of crossing the line in Montana are concrete and escalating. A first UPL complaint to the Board of Realty Regulation typically triggers an investigation under ARM 24.210.601, which can result in a letter of warning, mandatory continuing education, or formal discipline.

Repeated violations or a single egregious instance can result in license suspension for up to one year, a fine of up to $1,000 per violation under MCA 37-51-321, and referral to the Montana Supreme Court's Commission on Unauthorized Practice of Law. Civil liability is separate — agents who give incorrect legal advice at closing face negligence claims from buyers or sellers who relied on that advice to their detriment.

Deal cancellation is also possible. If a title insurer discovers that an unlicensed party prepared legal documents or that the closing was conducted outside the scope of MCA 33-25-301, it may decline to issue the final title policy. Without title insurance, most lenders will not fund, and the transaction collapses — often after both parties have incurred inspection, appraisal, and moving costs.
