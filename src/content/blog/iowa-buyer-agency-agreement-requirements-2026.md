## Iowa Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: The Statute-Level Breakdown

The phrase "Iowa buyer agency agreement requirements 2026" now triggers more compliance anxiety than any other topic in Iowa brokerage offices—and for good reason. Since the NAR settlement practice changes took hold in August 2024, the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) has sharpened its enforcement posture around written buyer representation. Agents who once relied on informal handshake arrangements or delayed paperwork until offer time are discovering that the regulatory ground beneath them has shifted permanently.

Iowa Code Chapter 543B governs real estate licensee conduct. Iowa Administrative Code 193E—Chapter 14 specifically addresses agency relationships, requiring that the nature and scope of representation be reduced to writing before services are rendered. The IREC has made clear through published guidance that "services" includes property showings, not just offer preparation.

## Which Forms Iowa Agents Must Execute—and When

**The Iowa Association of REALTORS publishes the forms most agents rely on, and getting the form number wrong on an audit is an avoidable headache.** IAR Form 105 (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) is the standard vehicle for exclusive relationships. IAR Form 106 covers non-exclusive buyer representation for agents who want a lighter commitment structure.

Both forms require execution before any brokerage services begin. The timing trigger is not the offer—it is the first substantive act of representation. That means before a showing, before a CMA delivered on a buyer's behalf, and before any negotiation communication with a listing agent.

Form 105 includes fields for compensation structure, duration, geographic or property-type limitations, and a clear termination clause. Since 2024, the form also requires that any buyer-broker compensation be stated as a specific dollar amount or percentage, not left open-ended or subject to "whatever the seller offers." This aligns with [MLS policy changes across the country](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) that decoupled cooperative compensation from listing data.

## Compensation Disclosure and the New MLS Reality

Iowa's regional MLS systems—Des Moines Area Association of REALTORS MLS, Iowa City Area Association MLS, and others—no longer display offers of buyer-agent compensation in listing data. This operational shift means the buyer agency agreement is now the sole contractual anchor for an agent's commission expectation.

The agreement must specify who pays the buyer's agent and how much. If the buyer is responsible but expects to request a seller concession at offer time, that must be addressed in writing within the agreement or an addendum. Ambiguity here creates post-closing disputes that the IREC has signaled it will not treat kindly.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences for skipping or botching the buyer agency agreement fall into three categories: regulatory, financial, and transactional.

**Regulatory penalties from the IREC can include fines up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory continuing education, license suspension, or revocation.** Iowa Administrative Code 193E—14.1 gives the commission authority to discipline licensees who fail to establish written agency relationships before providing services. The commission publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and buyer agency violations have appeared with increasing frequency since late 2024.

Financially, an agent without a valid agreement has no enforceable claim to compensation. If a dispute arises and the buyer refuses to pay—or a seller's concession falls through—the agent's brokerage has no contractual leg to stand on in court. Iowa courts have consistently held that oral agreements for real estate brokerage compensation do not satisfy the statute of frauds under Iowa Code 622.32.

Transactionally, a defective or missing buyer agency agreement can give the other party grounds to challenge the deal. Listing agents and sellers' attorneys in Iowa have become more aggressive about demanding proof of buyer representation before accepting offers, particularly in competitive multiple-offer situations.

## Five Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make

Mistake one: executing the agreement at offer time instead of before the first showing. This is the single most common violation the IREC cites in disciplinary proceedings. The rule is clear—services trigger the requirement, and a showing is a service.

Mistake two: leaving the compensation field blank or writing "TBD." The post-settlement environment demands specificity. A blank compensation field renders the agreement essentially unenforceable for commission recovery and may constitute a violation of Iowa Administrative Code 193E—14.2, which requires material terms to be defined.

Mistake three: using an expired or outdated form version. IAR updates its forms periodically, and agents who pull Form 105 from a 2023 template folder risk missing required disclosures added in the 2024 and 2025 revisions. Always download the current version from the [Iowa Association of REALTORS](https://www.iowarealtors.com) form library.

Mistake four: failing to provide a signed copy to the buyer within a reasonable time. Iowa Administrative Code 193E—14.5 requires that all parties receive copies of executed agreements. Agents who collect a signature on a tablet at a showing and never email the final PDF create an evidence gap that becomes a liability in complaints.

Mistake five: not addressing termination rights clearly. Buyers who feel trapped in an exclusive agreement they do not understand file IREC complaints at higher rates than almost any other consumer grievance category. A clear, conspicuous termination clause with a defined notice period (commonly 3–7 days written notice) reduces complaint risk dramatically.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Designated brokers in Iowa carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under Iowa Code 543B.29.** The statute makes the broker responsible for supervising all licensees operating under their license. When an agent skips a buyer agency agreement, the broker's license is on the line alongside the agent's.

Brokers should implement a file-review checkpoint that verifies a signed buyer representation agreement exists before any offer is submitted through the brokerage. This is not optional best practice—it is the minimum defensible standard given current IREC enforcement patterns. Firms that rely on agents to self-report compliance are asking for trouble during a random audit.

Brokers should also track agreement expiration dates. An agent who continues to show property after the agreement's stated end date is operating without authorization—a violation that exposes the brokerage to the same penalties as never having an agreement at all. Calendar-based alerts or [automated deadline tracking](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) systems eliminate this risk.

## Duration, Scope, and the Reasonableness Standard

Iowa does not cap the duration of buyer agency agreements by statute. However, the IREC has issued guidance recommending "reasonable" timeframes and has scrutinized agreements exceeding 12 months during complaint investigations. Most practicing agents in Iowa default to 90–180 days with an option to renew.

Scope limitations deserve more attention than they typically receive. An agreement can be limited by geography (e.g., Polk County only), property type (e.g., single-family residential), or price range. Narrowing the scope protects the agent from disputes where a buyer claims the agreement should not apply to a transaction outside the original search parameters.

The agreement should also address what happens if the buyer purchases a property listed by the same brokerage. Iowa permits designated agency under Iowa Administrative Code 193E—14.3, but the buyer agreement must acknowledge this possibility and the buyer must consent to the dual-agency or designated-agency arrangement in advance or at the time it arises.

## Training, Documentation, and Staying Ahead of Enforcement

The IREC's continuing education requirements for the 2024–2026 cycle include mandatory coursework on agency relationships and disclosure. Agents who completed their CE before the NAR settlement changes may not have received updated training on buyer agreement execution timing. Brokers should verify that every agent in their office has completed post-settlement agency training—regardless of what the CE transcript shows.

Documentation habits separate agents who survive complaints from those who do not. Every buyer interaction should generate a paper trail: the initial consultation where agency options are explained, the signed agreement, any amendments, and the termination or expiration record.
