## Understanding Montana Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The **Montana commission & licensing rules 2026** landscape has shifted in ways that demand attention from every active licensee in the state. Following national changes to commission transparency requirements and Montana-specific regulatory updates from the Board of Realty Regulation (BRR), agents who operate on autopilot risk disciplinary action, deal fallout, or unenforceable commission claims. This post breaks down exactly what Montana law requires, where agents commonly trip up, and what brokers must enforce to stay compliant.

Montana's real estate licensing framework is governed primarily by Title 37, Chapter 51 of the Montana Code Annotated (MCA) and the administrative rules adopted by the BRR under the Department of Labor and Industry. The BRR has enforcement authority over all licensed salespersons, broker associates, and brokers operating within the state. Every commission dispute, licensing violation, or disclosure failure ultimately lands on their desk.

## Written Commission Agreements: The Non-Negotiable Requirement

**Montana Code Annotated Section 37-51-321 makes one thing absolutely clear: no written agreement, no enforceable commission.** This statute requires that the employment relationship between a licensee and a client be memorialized in writing before any compensation obligation arises. Verbal promises, handshake deals, and email threads do not satisfy the statutory requirement.

The required form for buyer representation in Montana is the Buyer Broker Agreement (Montana Association of Realtors Form No. 18-Buyer), and for seller representation, the Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement (MAR Form No. 01-Listing). These forms must specify the compensation amount or calculation method, the duration of the agreement, and the scope of services. Since the 2024 NAR settlement changes took effect, Montana agents must also ensure that buyer-side compensation is not published on the MLS as an automatic offer but instead negotiated and documented per transaction.

Failing to secure a signed written agreement before performing brokerage services does not just create a collections problem. The BRR can treat it as practicing outside the scope of licensure, opening the agent to disciplinary proceedings under ARM 24.210.641.

## Montana Licensing Structure and Renewal Requirements

Montana operates a two-tier licensing system: salesperson and broker. A salesperson license requires completion of 60 hours of pre-licensing education, passage of the state and national exam portions, and affiliation with a licensed broker. A broker license requires an additional 60 hours of broker-specific coursework, at least two years of active practice, and a separate broker exam.

Renewal cycles run every two years. The BRR requires 12 hours of continuing education (CE) per cycle, and at least one of those courses must cover Montana-specific law updates. The renewal deadline falls on December 31 of odd-numbered years. Missing that deadline triggers a lapsed status, and any transaction activity during a lapse period constitutes unlicensed practice under MCA 37-51-302.

| Requirement | Salesperson | Broker |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-licensing education | 60 hours | 120 hours total |
| Experience requirement | None | 2 years active practice |
| CE per renewal cycle | 12 hours | 12 hours |
| Renewal deadline | Dec 31 (odd years) | Dec 31 (odd years) |
| Supervising broker required | Yes | No |

The [Montana Board of Realty Regulation](https://boards.bsd.dli.mt.gov/realty-regulation) publishes approved CE providers and course lists on its website. Agents should verify course approval before enrollment, as unapproved courses will not count toward renewal.

## Commission Disclosure and Transparency After the NAR Settlement

The national settlement agreements from 2024 reshaped how buyer-agent compensation is handled, and Montana was not exempt from those ripple effects. MLS systems operating in Montana, including the Montana Regional MLS, no longer permit sellers to publish blanket cooperative compensation offers. This means buyer-agent compensation must be established through a separate written agreement with the buyer before touring properties.

**Agents who show property without a signed buyer-broker agreement are now operating without a legal basis to claim compensation.** The MAR Form No. 18-Buyer has been updated to include fields for compensation terms that comply with both MCA 37-51-321 and the new MLS policies. The form now requires a clear statement of the exact commission amount or percentage, who is expected to pay it, and whether the buyer authorizes the agent to seek compensation from the seller as part of the purchase offer.

This shift makes [buyer agency agreements](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) a functional prerequisite rather than a best practice. Montana agents should treat the form like a seatbelt: it goes on before you start moving.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make

Five errors show up repeatedly in BRR disciplinary actions and civil commission disputes. Each one is preventable, and each one carries real consequences.

**First, agents let their CE lapse and continue practicing.** This is not a gray area. MCA 37-51-302 defines unlicensed practice as a misdemeanor offense, and the BRR can impose fines of up to $1,500 per violation under ARM 24.210.601. Transactions closed during a lapsed period may be voidable, and the commission earned is unenforceable.

Second, agents fail to disclose their agency relationship using the required Consent to Agency Disclosure form (MAR Form No. 30-Agency). Montana law under MCA 37-51-313 requires this disclosure at the first substantive contact with a party. Agents who wait until the offer stage are already in violation, and opposing parties can use the failure to challenge the transaction or the commission claim.

Third, agents accept compensation directly from a party other than their supervising broker. MCA 37-51-321(3) is explicit: a salesperson may only receive compensation through the broker with whom they are affiliated. An agent who takes a referral fee, bonus, or commission split directly from another brokerage, a title company, or a client has committed a violation that can result in license suspension.

Fourth, agents use expired or outdated form versions. The MAR updates its standard forms periodically, and the BRR expects agents to use current versions. Using a 2023 listing agreement in 2026, for instance, means missing required disclosure fields that have been added in response to statutory changes. This leaves the agent exposed to claims of inadequate disclosure.

Fifth, agents fail to include the license number on advertising materials. ARM 24.210.625 requires that all advertising, including social media posts and email signatures, include the licensee's name and the name of their brokerage. Omitting this information is a citable offense, and the BRR actively monitors online advertising for compliance.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Montana carry supervisory liability under MCA 37-51-314. If an affiliated agent violates licensing law or commission rules, the broker can be held jointly responsible unless they can demonstrate active supervision and documented compliance procedures. The BRR does not accept ignorance as a defense.

**Every Montana broker should maintain an audit system that checks the following on a recurring basis.** First, verify that all affiliated agents have current, active licenses by cross-referencing the BRR's online license lookup tool. Second, confirm that every active transaction file contains a signed buyer-broker agreement or listing agreement with compliant compensation terms. Third, review advertising materials quarterly to ensure ARM 24.210.625 compliance on brokerage name and licensee identification.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Agent license status verification | Monthly | Unlicensed practice liability |
| Written commission agreements in file | Per transaction | Unenforceable commission, BRR discipline |
| Agency disclosure (Form 30) in file | Per transaction | Transaction voidability, fines |
| Advertising compliance review | Quarterly | Citable violation, fines |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | License revocation (ARM 24.210.626) |

Trust account management deserves particular attention. ARM 24.210.626 requires brokers to maintain all earnest money deposits in a state-approved trust account, reconcile the account monthly, and retain records for at least five years. A trust account violation is one of the fastest paths to license revocation in Montana. If you want to understand how [earnest money mishandling](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026) creates cascading liability, the principles apply across state lines.

## Penalties and Enforcement: What Non-Compliance Actually Costs

The BRR has a graduated enforcement approach, but the consequences escalate quickly. A first-time advertising violation might result in a letter of warning. A failure to maintain proper agency disclosure or commission documentation typically triggers a formal complaint investigation, which can result in a consent order requiring the agent to pay fines, complete additional CE, or accept a period of probation.

More serious violations, such as unlicensed practice, trust account mismanagement, or accepting undisclosed compensation, carry penalties including license suspension or revocation, civil fines up to $1,500 per occurrence, and referral to the county attorney for criminal prosecution under MCA 37-51-323. An agent whose license is revoked cannot reapply for a minimum of two years.

Beyond regulatory penalties, commission disputes that reach civil court often hinge on whether the agent complied with MCA 37-51-321's written agreement requirement. Montana courts have consistently held that oral commission agreements are unenforceable regardless of the services performed. The 2019 Montana Supreme Court decision in Halverson v. Kelley reinforced this principle, ruling that substantial performance of brokerage duties does not override the statutory writing requirement.
