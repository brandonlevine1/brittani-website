## Why Missouri Commission & Licensing Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC) enforces a licensing and compensation framework that trips up even experienced agents. Understanding Missouri commission & licensing rules 2026 is not optional—it is the difference between getting paid at closing and facing a formal complaint that jeopardizes your career. MREC disciplinary actions increased 14% between 2023 and 2025, and commission-related violations ranked among the top three complaint categories.

Missouri's statutory authority comes primarily from Chapter 339 of the Revised Statutes of Missouri (RSMo) and the administrative rules codified in Title 20, Division 2250 of the Code of State Regulations. These are not suggestions. They carry the force of law, and ignorance has never been accepted as a defense before the Commission.

This post breaks down the statutes, forms, enforcement consequences, and audit obligations that matter most to agents and brokers operating in Missouri right now.

## The Statutory Foundation: RSMo Chapter 339 and 20 CSR 2250

**Missouri's licensing authority flows from RSMo 339.010 through 339.190, which define who must hold a license, how commissions may be earned, and what conduct warrants discipline.** The Missouri Real Estate Commission operates under the Division of Professional Registration within the Department of Commerce and Insurance. All rulemaking and enforcement authority is housed there.

RSMo 339.100 is the statute that agents most often violate without realizing it. It states plainly that no person may bring an action to collect compensation for real estate services unless they held a valid license at the time the services were performed and have a written agreement authorizing that compensation. No written agreement means no legal right to collect—period.

The administrative rules in [20 CSR 2250](https://www.sos.mo.gov/adrules/csr/current/20csr/20c2250-1) expand on Chapter 339 with specific requirements for advertising, trust accounts, supervision, and continuing education. Agents who only read the statute and skip the CSR are missing half the picture.

## Commission Agreements: Forms and Written Requirements

Missouri does not mandate a single state-issued commission agreement form the way some states do. However, the Missouri REALTORS association publishes standard forms that most brokerages adopt. The most relevant forms for commission purposes include Form 100 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement), Form 101 (Exclusive Agency Listing Agreement), and Form 145 (Buyer Representation Agreement—Exclusive).

Since the 2024 NAR settlement changes took effect, Form 145 has become functionally mandatory for buyer agents seeking compensation. The form must disclose the specific amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will receive, and it must be signed before the agent provides substantive services. Missouri adopted this requirement in alignment with MLS policies across the state.

**Failing to secure a signed buyer representation agreement before showing property is now the fastest way to forfeit your commission.** If a dispute arises, MREC will ask for the signed agreement. If you cannot produce one, you lose—both the money and potentially your license standing.

| Form Number | Purpose | Required Before |
|---|---|---|
| Form 100 | Exclusive Right to Sell Listing | Marketing the property |
| Form 101 | Exclusive Agency Listing | Marketing the property |
| Form 145 | Buyer Representation (Exclusive) | Substantive buyer services |
| Form 150 | Compensation Agreement (Broker-to-Broker) | Showing or negotiating |
| Form 160 | Dual Agency Consent | Acting for both parties |

The broker-to-broker compensation agreement (Form 150) deserves special attention. With offers of compensation no longer displayed on the MLS, this form documents any cooperating compensation arrangement between listing and buyer brokerages. Without it, there is no enforceable promise.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of violating Missouri's commission and licensing rules range from financial loss to criminal prosecution, depending on severity. MREC's disciplinary process is governed by RSMo 339.100 through 339.190 and the procedures outlined in 20 CSR 2250-8.

For commission violations—such as collecting a fee without a written agreement or accepting compensation from a party other than your broker—MREC can impose fines up to $2,000 per violation, suspend your license for a defined period, or revoke it entirely. These penalties are cumulative; multiple violations in a single transaction can stack.

Beyond MREC discipline, civil liability is the larger financial risk. If you perform services without a valid license or proper written agreement, you may be forced to disgorge any commission already received. Missouri courts have consistently upheld this under RSMo 339.100, ruling that unlicensed or improperly documented activity creates no enforceable right to compensation—even if you did the work.

Criminal exposure exists as well. RSMo 339.190 makes it a Class A misdemeanor to practice real estate without a license, punishable by up to one year in jail and a $2,000 fine. Operating on an expired license counts. Referring a client for a fee while your license is inactive counts.

## Common Mistakes Missouri Agents Make

**The most frequent compliance failures are not dramatic—they are administrative oversights that compound into serious problems.** Here are five specific errors MREC sees repeatedly.

First, agents let their licenses lapse without realizing it. Missouri licenses renew on a two-year cycle, and the 12-hour continuing education requirement (including the 3-hour MREC core topic) must be completed before the renewal date—not after. If your license expires on September 30 and you close a deal on October 2 without having renewed, you earned that commission unlicensed. MREC will not grandfather your transaction.

Second, agents accept referral fees from or pay them to unlicensed individuals. RSMo 339.150 is explicit: compensation for real estate services can only go to a licensed person through their broker. Paying a "finder's fee" to a friend who sent you a lead is a violation, no matter how you characterize it on paper.

Third, agents fail to route all compensation through their designated broker. Under RSMo 339.100 and 20 CSR 2250-4.010, all commissions must be paid to the agent's employing broker. The broker then distributes to the agent per their internal agreement. An agent who receives a commission check directly from a title company—bypassing the broker—has violated the statute regardless of the amount.

Fourth, dual agency consent is not properly documented. Form 160 must be signed by all parties before the agent acts for both sides. Obtaining retroactive consent after negotiations have begun does not satisfy the statute, and MREC treats it as practicing undisclosed dual agency—a ground for suspension.

Fifth, agents advertise compensation terms that conflict with their actual written agreements. If your buyer representation agreement states a 2.5% commission but your marketing materials or verbal communications promise something different, you have created a discrepancy that can void the agreement or trigger a deceptive practices complaint under RSMo 339.100(7).

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Missouri places primary supervisory responsibility on the broker of record. Under 20 CSR 2250-4.010, the broker must maintain reasonable supervisory procedures to ensure all licensees under their authority comply with Chapter 339 and the Commission's rules. "Reasonable" is not defined with specificity, but MREC has indicated through enforcement actions what constitutes inadequate supervision.

**Brokers should conduct quarterly audits of three specific areas: written commission agreements, license renewal status for all affiliated agents, and trust account reconciliation.** Missing any of these creates broker-level liability that extends beyond the individual agent's misconduct.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Commission agreements | Signed Form 100/101/145/150 on file for every active transaction | Per transaction |
| License status | Verify active status on MREC lookup for all agents | Monthly |
| Trust accounts | Reconcile within 72 hours of any deposit per 20 CSR 2250-4.060 | Weekly |
| Advertising compliance | Confirm brokerage name appears on all agent advertising | Monthly |
| Dual agency disclosure | Form 160 signed and dated before dual representation begins | Per transaction |

The trust account requirement deserves emphasis. Missouri requires brokers to deposit earnest money within five business days of contract acceptance unless the contract specifies otherwise. Commingling personal funds with trust funds—even temporarily—is grounds for license revocation under 20 CSR 2250-4.060. This is not a warning-level offense; MREC treats it as presumptively disqualifying.

Brokers who rely on agents to self-report compliance are setting themselves up for vicarious liability. A missed renewal date or unsigned form can unravel an entire transaction's compensation structure.

## Continuing Education and License Renewal Mechanics

Missouri requires 12 hours of approved continuing education every two years for salesperson and broker license renewal. Of those 12 hours, 3 must cover the MREC-designated core topic, which changes each cycle. For the 2024-2026 cycle, the core topic focuses on changes to commission practices and buyer representation—directly relevant to everything discussed here.

Agents can complete CE through approved providers listed on the MREC website. Online courses are acceptable, but they must come from a provider approved by MREC specifically—not just any state. Missouri does not have broad reciprocity for CE credit.

If you miss your renewal deadline, your license moves to "expired" status. You have up to two years to reinstate by completing all required CE and paying a late fee. But during that expired period, you cannot practice, earn commissions, or hold yourself out as a licensee. Any activity during expiration is treated as unlicensed practice under RSMo 339.190.

## How Post-NAR Settlement Rules Interact with Missouri Law

The 2024 NAR settlement reshaped how commission offers are communicated nationally, and Missouri brokerages had to adapt their practices. Offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer appear in MLS listings. Instead, compensation must be negotiated directly and documented in writing outside the MLS.

This means Form 150 (the broker-to-broker compensation agreement) and Form 145 (buyer representation) have become the primary documentation vehicles. Missouri law already required written compensation agreements—RSMo 339.100 predates the settlement by decades—but enforcement intensity has increased because the new framework removes the MLS as a safety net.

Agents who previously relied on MLS-published co-op compensation as their "written agreement" are now exposed. That field no longer exists, and MREC has clarified that MLS data never satisfied the statute's requirement for a signed agreement between the parties. If you were operating on that assumption before, correct it immediately.
