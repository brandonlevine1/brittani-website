The Oregon commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have forced a structural overhaul of how agents earn, disclose, and negotiate their pay. If you are a licensed agent or principal broker in Oregon, the old playbook of blanket MLS commission offers and informal buyer arrangements is gone. What replaced it is a system built on written agreements, upfront disclosure, and strict prohibitions that carry real enforcement teeth.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed Commission Rules in Oregon

The August 2024 NAR settlement eliminated cooperative compensation offers from MLS systems nationwide. In Oregon, the Regional Multiple Listing Service (RMLS) removed the buyer agent compensation field from all active listings effective August 17, 2024. That single change restructured every transaction in the state.

**Oregon did not wait for federal prodding to adapt its regulatory framework.** The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA), which administers licensing under ORS Chapter 696, issued guidance aligning state rules with the settlement's practice changes. OREA's Administrative Rule OAR 863-015-0125 already required written disclosure of agency relationships, but the post-settlement landscape demanded tighter standards around compensation specifics.

The result is a two-pronged mandate: buyer agents must secure written compensation agreements before providing substantive services, and all parties must receive clear, affirmative disclosure of who pays what and how much. The era of implied compensation through MLS cooperation is over.

## Written Buyer Representation: The Non-Negotiable Foundation

Every buyer agent in Oregon must now execute a written buyer representation agreement before touring properties with a client. The Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) committee updated its standard forms to reflect these requirements. OREF Form 201 — the Buyer Representation Agreement — now includes mandatory fields for compensation amount or rate, the source of compensation, and the duration of the agreement.

This is not optional. Under the [NAR settlement terms](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs), any MLS participant who shows properties without a signed buyer agreement violates practice requirements and risks MLS sanctions. In Oregon, OREA can independently pursue disciplinary action under ORS 696.301 for failure to disclose material facts about the agency relationship.

The agreement must specify compensation in a concrete, objective manner. Vague language like "standard commission" or "customary rate" does not satisfy the requirement. You must state a dollar amount, a percentage, or a clear formula that the buyer can understand without industry jargon. If you [miss this step and a deal falls apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down), the liability exposure is significant.

## Oregon Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026: Disclosure Requirements

Disclosure happens at multiple points in an Oregon transaction, not just at signing. OREF Form 100 (the Seller's Listing Agreement) must document what, if any, compensation the seller is willing to offer a buyer's agent. This offer cannot be published on RMLS but can be communicated through broker-to-broker channels, property websites, or direct negotiation.

| Disclosure Point | Form Used | What Must Be Disclosed | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listing appointment | OREF Form 100 | Seller's willingness to pay buyer agent comp | Before marketing begins |
| Buyer engagement | OREF Form 201 | Buyer agent compensation amount/source | Before touring or showing |
| Offer submission | OREF Form 003 (Purchase Agreement) | Any seller concessions toward buyer agent comp | With offer |
| Closing | Settlement statement (ALTA/HUD) | Final disbursement of all commissions | At closing |

**The settlement statement must match the previously disclosed compensation terms.** Any deviation between what was agreed upon in OREF Form 201 and what appears on the closing statement creates an audit flag and potential OREA complaint. Title companies in Oregon are now scrutinizing these figures with heightened attention.

Oregon's dual agency rules under OAR 863-015-0140 add another layer. If a principal broker's office represents both sides, the compensation structure must be disclosed to both parties independently, and neither party can be disadvantaged by a compensation arrangement they did not affirmatively approve in writing.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Making Right Now

The first eighteen months under the new rules have revealed consistent compliance failures across Oregon brokerages. These are not theoretical risks — they are generating actual OREA complaints and civil disputes.

**First, agents are using outdated forms.** Some agents are still circulating pre-settlement versions of OREF Form 201 that lack the mandatory compensation disclosure fields. OREF publishes updated forms through its authorized vendors, and using an obsolete version does not satisfy disclosure requirements even if you handwrite compensation terms in the margins.

Second, agents are signing buyer agreements with open-ended duration. The settlement requires that buyer representation agreements include a specific, ascertainable end date. Agreements that say "until buyer purchases a property" or have no termination date violate the practice changes and leave agents exposed to disputes about when representation actually ended.

Third, listing agents are verbally communicating buyer agent compensation offers without documenting them. If a seller authorizes compensation to a buyer's agent, that authorization must exist in writing — typically within OREF Form 100 or an addendum. A phone call between agents does not satisfy Oregon's written disclosure standards under ORS 696.805.

Fourth, agents are conflating seller concessions with buyer agent compensation. A seller credit toward the buyer's closing costs is not the same as direct payment of the buyer agent's fee, even if the economic effect is similar. These must be documented separately in the purchase agreement to avoid lender red flags and TRID violations.

Fifth, some agents are asking buyers to sign representation agreements at the door of an open house. While technically satisfying the timing requirement, this practice raises informed consent concerns. OREA's guidance suggests providing the form in advance so the buyer has reasonable time to review compensation terms before committing. Rushing the signature invites rescission claims later.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Oregon carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.200. When an agent in your office fails to comply with commission disclosure rules, the broker is not merely a bystander — you are a co-respondent in any OREA disciplinary proceeding.

**Every brokerage should audit three categories on a rolling basis.** First, confirm that every active buyer client has a current, compliant OREF Form 201 on file before any showing activity. Implement a policy that no showing confirmations go out until the signed agreement is uploaded to your transaction management system. If your [current systems rely on memory rather than automated checks](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), you have a compliance gap.

Second, audit listing files to verify that seller compensation authorization matches what is communicated to cooperating brokers. If your agent tells a buyer's agent that the seller will pay three percent, the listing file must contain written authorization for that specific amount.

Third, review closing statements against signed agreements. The numbers must reconcile. If an agent negotiated compensation adjustments mid-transaction — which is permissible — those changes must be documented in a written amendment to the buyer representation agreement and countersigned by the buyer before closing.

| Audit Category | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agreements | OREF 201 signed, dated, with specific comp terms | Missing signature, vague terms, no end date |
| Listing authorization | OREF 100 comp section completed, matches broker communications | Blank comp field, verbal-only offers |
| Closing reconciliation | Settlement statement matches Form 201 and Form 100 | Mismatched percentages, undocumented adjustments |
| Open house protocols | Agreements signed before substantive service | Signatures timestamped same as showing |
| File retention | All forms stored per OAR 863-015-0260 (6-year minimum) | Missing or incomplete files |

Oregon's record retention requirement under OAR 863-015-0260 mandates that brokerages maintain transaction files for at least six years. Commission disclosure documents fall squarely within that mandate. If OREA audits your office and a file is missing a buyer representation agreement from 2026, the defense of "we had one but lost it" will not prevent disciplinary action.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance in Oregon

OREA's disciplinary authority under ORS 696.301 includes license suspension, license revocation, civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation, and mandatory continuing education. Commission disclosure violations are classified as violations of the duty to deal honestly and in good faith under ORS 696.805(1).

Beyond regulatory penalties, non-compliance creates civil liability. A buyer who was not informed about compensation structures before engaging an agent may claim breach of fiduciary duty and seek disgorgement of the commission paid. Oregon courts have historically taken a dim view of agents who fail to disclose material financial interests, and the post-settlement rules have made the standard even clearer.

Deal cancellation is also a real consequence. If a lender discovers that buyer agent compensation was structured in a way that was not properly disclosed on the loan estimate or closing disclosure, the transaction may be delayed or terminated. TRID compliance requires that all fees — including buyer agent compensation paid through seller credits — appear accurately and on time. An agent's [failure to meet deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on these disclosures can kill a deal days before closing.
