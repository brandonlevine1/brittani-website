## New Hampshire Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026

The New Hampshire commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have fundamentally altered how agents disclose, negotiate, and collect compensation on residential transactions. If you hold an active license issued by the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), the old playbook of blanket cooperative compensation offers embedded in MLS listings no longer applies. These changes took root nationally in August 2024, but 2026 brings sharper enforcement teeth at both the state regulatory and MLS level in New Hampshire.

The settlement resolved antitrust claims against the National Association of Realtors and several major brokerages. Its practical effect in New Hampshire is that buyer agent compensation can no longer be communicated through MLS fields, and every buyer-agent relationship must be memorialized in a written agreement before substantive services begin. Agents who ignore these rules face consequences ranging from MLS fines to license discipline under RSA 331-A.

## The Legal Framework Governing New Hampshire Agents

New Hampshire real estate licensees operate under RSA 331-A, the statute governing the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission. Section 331-A:25 outlines prohibited conduct, including misrepresentation and failure to disclose material facts about agency relationships and compensation. The NHREC has issued guidance clarifying that post-settlement commission practices fall within existing disclosure obligations already codified in state law.

**The NHREC did not need to pass new statutes to enforce the settlement's mandates.** Instead, the Commission treats non-compliant compensation practices as potential violations of RSA 331-A:25, II (misrepresentation) and RSA 331-A:25, XII (conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness). This means disciplinary proceedings can be initiated without waiting for new legislation.

New Hampshire's MLS systems—primarily the Northern New England Real Estate Network (NNEREN)—have updated their rules of participation to remove cooperative compensation fields from listing input sheets. NNEREN's 2025-2026 rule amendments explicitly prohibit including buyer broker compensation amounts, percentages, or bonuses in any listing data or remarks field.

## Written Buyer Representation: Forms and Requirements

Every buyer agent in New Hampshire must now execute a written buyer representation agreement before touring properties or submitting offers. The [New Hampshire Association of Realtors](https://www.nhar.com) provides Form NHAR-2026-BRA (Buyer Representation Agreement), which includes mandatory fields for compensation amount or percentage, duration of the agreement, and the specific services the agent will provide.

The form requires the buyer to acknowledge that compensation is negotiable and not set by law. It also includes a disclosure that the buyer may be responsible for paying their agent directly if the seller does not agree to contribute. Agents using brokerage-specific forms must ensure those documents contain at minimum the same disclosures found in NHAR-2026-BRA.

**Duration matters.** The agreement cannot be open-ended under NAR's practice change requirements. New Hampshire agents should specify a reasonable term—typically 90 to 180 days—and include a clear mechanism for termination by either party.

## How Compensation Gets Negotiated Now

The old model was simple: a listing agent entered a co-op commission split into NNEREN, and buyer agents knew exactly what they would earn before writing an offer. That system is gone. Compensation discussions now happen in one of three ways, each with distinct documentation requirements.

| Method | Where It's Documented | Who Initiates | Key Form |
|--------|----------------------|---------------|----------|
| Seller concession in purchase agreement | NHAR Form 2022-PS (Purchase and Sale Agreement), paragraph addressing compensation | Buyer agent request, seller acceptance | NHAR-2022-PS with addendum |
| Separate compensation agreement | Standalone document between seller and buyer's broker | Either party | Brokerage-drafted agreement |
| Buyer pays directly | Buyer Representation Agreement | Buyer and buyer's agent | NHAR-2026-BRA |
| Listing broker offer outside MLS | Direct communication between brokers | Listing agent | Written confirmation required |

Regardless of method, nothing about buyer agent compensation may appear in NNEREN listing data. Listing agents who receive calls from buyer agents asking about compensation availability may respond, but the response must be documented outside the MLS platform.

This shift demands that agents working with buyers have the compensation conversation before showing the first house—not at the offer stage. If a buyer cannot or will not pay their agent directly, the agent needs to build compensation requests into offers from day one, which requires familiarity with how to draft addenda to the NHAR-2022-PS form.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Making in New Hampshire

After monitoring enforcement patterns and brokerage audits through early 2026, several recurring errors have emerged among New Hampshire licensees.

**First, agents are showing homes without a signed buyer representation agreement.** This is the single most common infraction. Some agents treat the requirement as optional for initial tours or open houses they host themselves. The rule is unambiguous: no written agreement, no substantive buyer representation services. An open house attended casually does not trigger the requirement, but a scheduled showing arranged by the agent does.

Second, agents are embedding compensation language in MLS remarks using coded language. Phrases like "generous seller assist available" or "bonus offered—call listing agent" in the public or agent remarks violate NNEREN rules. The MLS compliance team has flagged and fined brokerages for these workarounds in Q1 and Q2 of 2026.

Third, some agents fail to specify a dollar amount or percentage in the buyer agreement, instead writing "to be determined." This creates an unenforceable contract under New Hampshire law and leaves the agent with no legal claim to compensation if a dispute arises. RSA 331-A requires that compensation terms be clear and agreed upon in advance.

Fourth, agents are neglecting to update the buyer representation agreement when the compensation source changes mid-transaction. If a buyer initially agrees to pay 2.5% but the seller later offers a concession covering that amount, the agreement should be amended to reflect the actual payment flow. Failure to do so creates confusion at closing and potential trust account issues.

Fifth, listing agents are failing to inform sellers that buyers may request compensation concessions in offers. Sellers blindsided by these requests may reject otherwise strong offers, harming the listing agent's fiduciary obligation to present all terms transparently. Agents should address this possibility in their listing presentations using the NHAR Listing Agreement (Form NHAR-2024-LA).

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear direct supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:26 for the conduct of their affiliated agents. The NHREC can impose fines and suspend a broker's license if systemic non-compliance is traced back to inadequate supervision. This means brokerage-level compliance infrastructure is not optional—it is a condition of maintaining licensure.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency | Consequence of Failure |
|------------|--------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Buyer agreements on file | Signed before first showing, correct form, compensation specified | Every transaction | NHREC discipline, MLS fine |
| MLS listing compliance | No compensation language in any field or remarks | Weekly spot-check | NNEREN fine ($500-$2,500), listing removal |
| Compensation documentation | Written proof of agreed compensation source for each closed deal | At closing review | Trust account violations, clawback risk |
| Agent training records | Annual CE plus settlement-specific training completion | Quarterly | Supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:26 |
| Listing presentation materials | No promises of "standard" commissions or fixed splits | Semi-annual review | Antitrust exposure |

Brokers should implement a transaction file checklist that includes a specific line item confirming the buyer representation agreement was executed before the first showing date. The [importance of checklists over memory in transaction management](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) cannot be overstated here—a single missed file can trigger an audit finding.

Additionally, brokers should review their independent contractor agreements to ensure commission split structures comply with the new reality. If your ICA references MLS cooperative compensation as a source of buyer-side payment, that language needs updating. Many New Hampshire brokerages are also revising their policies and procedures manuals to address scenarios where buyer clients refuse to sign representation agreements.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

The NHREC has authority under RSA 331-A:28 to impose fines up to $2,000 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional education. For commission-related violations, the Commission has historically treated first offenses with a letter of concern or consent agreement, but repeated infractions trigger formal hearings.

**MLS penalties operate on a separate track.** NNEREN's updated rules allow fines starting at $500 for a first offense of including compensation data in listing fields, escalating to $2,500 and temporary suspension for repeat violations. These fines hit the brokerage, not the individual agent, which is why broker oversight matters.

Beyond regulatory consequences, agents face transactional liability. A buyer representation agreement found to be non-compliant—missing compensation terms, unsigned, or executed after services began—may be unenforceable. This means the agent could complete an entire transaction and have no legal basis to collect a commission. New Hampshire courts have consistently held that real estate compensation agreements must be in writing and signed before services are rendered, per RSA 331-A:2, XIII-a.

Deal cancellation is another risk. If a seller discovers mid-transaction that a buyer's agent violated MLS rules or operated without proper buyer authorization, it can become grounds for the seller to question the validity of the offer itself. While this does not automatically void a purchase and sale agreement, it creates leverage for parties looking to exit a deal.
