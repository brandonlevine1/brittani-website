## Iowa New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Changes That Hit July 1

The phrase **Iowa new real estate laws 2026** is no longer a preview—it is the operating reality for every licensee in the state. Governor Reynolds signed HF 2187 and companion legislation SF 1044 into law in May 2026, and both took effect on July 1. If you have not updated your workflow, form packets, or brokerage compliance manual, every open transaction you touch right now carries risk.

The Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) issued Administrative Bulletin 2026-03 in late June, spelling out enforcement timelines and specifying which form versions are now mandatory. This article breaks down the statutes that matter, the specific forms you must swap in, and the penalties attached to non-compliance.

## Buyer Agency Agreements Are No Longer Optional

Iowa Code Section 543B.56A now mandates a written buyer representation agreement before an agent provides any "substantive real estate service" to a prospective buyer. The statute defines substantive service as scheduling private showings, preparing offers, or providing property-specific pricing analysis. A casual open-house conversation does not trigger the requirement, but a follow-up phone call recommending that buyer visit a second property likely does.

**The Iowa Association of Realtors updated its Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement (IAR Form 108-A, revised 06/2026) to satisfy this statute.** If you are using a prior version of Form 108-A, it lacks the required compensation transparency language referenced in Section 543B.56A(3). That alone could void the agreement and leave you uncompensated at closing.

Brokerages that operate across state lines should compare this mandate against [Wisconsin's buyer agency agreement requirements](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026) to spot where Iowa's language diverges—particularly on the triggering event for the written agreement.

## Updated Seller Disclosure Requirements Under Iowa Code Chapter 558A

HF 2187 amended Chapter 558A to expand the scope of mandatory seller disclosures. The revised statute adds three new disclosure categories: known flood zone classification changes (even if FEMA maps have not yet been redrawn), prior insurance claims for mold or structural movement filed in the last ten years, and the presence of any unpermitted additions regardless of age.

The form that implements these requirements is **Iowa Real Estate Commission Form 103** (Residential Property Seller Disclosure, revised 07/2026). The prior edition—dated 2019—is no longer accepted. If a listing goes active with the 2019 form attached, IREC Administrative Bulletin 2026-03 treats it as a missing disclosure.

Sellers must deliver the completed Form 103 to the buyer before the buyer's acceptance of the purchase agreement. Under the revised 558A.4, delivery after acceptance triggers a 72-hour rescission window, and the clock starts when the buyer acknowledges receipt in writing.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance range from embarrassing to career-ending, depending on the violation and whether it repeats.

| Violation | Statute | First Offense Penalty | Repeat Offense Penalty |
|-----------|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Missing buyer agency agreement | 543B.56A | $1,000 fine + mandatory 8-hour CE | License suspension up to 6 months |
| Using outdated Form 103 | 558A.2 | Written warning + 30-day cure | $2,500 fine per transaction |
| Failure to deliver disclosure pre-acceptance | 558A.4 | Buyer rescission right + $500 fine | $2,500 fine + license review |
| Broker failure to audit files | 543B.34 | $2,500 brokerage fine | Brokerage license probation |

Beyond regulatory penalties, civil liability exposure expands under the 2026 amendments. Section 558A.6(2) now explicitly grants buyers a private right of action for damages resulting from undisclosed conditions that the seller knew about and the agent "reasonably should have flagged for disclosure." That phrase puts agents on notice: you cannot hide behind the seller's signature if the defect was visible during your listing walkthrough.

Deal cancellations carry their own fallout. When a buyer exercises the 72-hour rescission right because disclosure arrived late, the earnest money returns to the buyer under [Iowa's escrow handling procedures](https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/iowaCode/sections?codeChapter=558A), and the listing agent typically bears the blame internally even when the seller caused the delay.

## Five Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

Since July 1, IREC has received a spike in compliance inquiries. Based on patterns published in the Commission's August 2026 newsletter, here are the errors showing up most often.

**First, agents are presenting buyers with the old IAR Form 108 instead of the revised Form 108-A.** The difference is a single compensation-disclosure paragraph, but using the wrong version means the agreement fails the statutory test under 543B.56A(3). Check your transaction management software templates immediately.

Second, listing agents are attaching the 2019 version of Form 103 because their MLS auto-populates the old PDF. The Des Moines Area Association of Realtors issued a tech bulletin on July 8 reminding agents to manually upload the revised version until the MLS integration is updated. If your MLS has not pushed this fix yet, do not wait.

Third, agents are confusing the disclosure delivery deadline. The statute says before buyer acceptance, not before the offer is written. Sending the disclosure alongside a counteroffer still satisfies the rule. Sending it the day after mutual acceptance does not—even if the ink is barely dry. Agents who have dealt with [why deadlines get missed](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in other contexts will recognize the pattern: it is rarely intentional, but intent does not matter to IREC.

Fourth, buyer agents are failing to retain proof of when they executed the buyer representation agreement. The statute requires the agreement to be in place before substantive service, but if you cannot prove the timestamp, you cannot defend yourself in a complaint. Date-stamp the signed PDF and store it in your transaction file the same day.

Fifth, agents are not disclosing their own compensation structure clearly enough within the buyer agreement. Section 543B.56A(3)(b) requires a "specific dollar amount or percentage" rather than language like "to be determined at closing" or "per MLS offering." Vague compensation language invalidates the agreement retroactively.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce Starting Now

SF 1044 expanded the supervising broker's direct liability under Iowa Code Section 543B.34. A broker can no longer claim ignorance of an agent's missing paperwork. The statute presumes the broker had constructive knowledge if the file was accessible in the brokerage's transaction management system for more than five business days without a compliant buyer agreement or current disclosure form.

**Brokers should run a full file audit on every transaction opened after July 1, 2026, and flag any file missing the revised Form 103 or IAR Form 108-A.** This is not optional housekeeping—it is a statutory obligation with fines attached.

Here is a practical audit checklist brokers should implement:

| Audit Item | Required Document | Deadline to Confirm |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Buyer agency executed | IAR Form 108-A (06/2026) | Before first showing scheduled |
| Seller disclosure current version | IREC Form 103 (07/2026) | Before listing goes active in MLS |
| Disclosure delivery confirmation | Signed buyer acknowledgment | Before mutual acceptance |
| Compensation specificity check | Dollar or percentage in Form 108-A | At agreement execution |
| File timestamp verification | Digital signature metadata | Within 5 business days of signing |

Brokerages that rely on [checklists over memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) already have a structural advantage here. If your transaction coordinator or office manager is still working from a pre-July checklist, the brokerage itself is exposed under 543B.34 every day that gap remains.

## Commission Structure Transparency and MLS Cooperation Changes

Iowa's 2026 legislative package also codified new MLS cooperation rules that align with—but are not identical to—NAR's national settlement terms. Under the amended Iowa Code Section 543B.60, an MLS operating in Iowa may no longer display blanket offers of buyer-agent compensation on property listings. Instead, compensation discussions must happen off-MLS, either through the buyer agreement or direct broker-to-broker communication.

This does not mean sellers cannot offer buyer-agent compensation. It means the offer cannot appear as a field in the MLS data feed. Agents who were accustomed to filtering MLS results by offered compensation will need to adjust their buyer consultation process. Explaining this change clearly to buyers—without editorializing—is now part of the agent's disclosure duty under 543B.56A(4).

Iowa's approach differs slightly from neighboring states. Wisconsin, for instance, handled its [commission structure changes](/blog/wisconsin-commission-structure-changes-2026) through administrative rule rather than statute, giving the Wisconsin DSPS more flexibility to adjust without legislative action. Iowa locked its rules into statute, meaning any future changes require a full legislative cycle.
