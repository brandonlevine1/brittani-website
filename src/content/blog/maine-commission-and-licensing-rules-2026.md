## Understanding Maine Commission & Licensing Rules 2026 for Active Agents

The Maine commission & licensing rules 2026 framework continues to evolve, and agents who ignore the details risk fines, license suspension, or lost closings. Maine's Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under the Department of Professional and Financial Regulation, enforces Title 32, Chapter 114 of the Maine Revised Statutes—the bedrock authority governing how you earn, split, and collect commissions in this state. If you haven't reviewed your compliance posture since the NAR settlement changes rippled into state-level practice, now is the time.

This post breaks down the statutes, form requirements, enforcement penalties, and operational pitfalls that trip up even experienced Maine licensees.

## The Statutory Framework: Title 32 and MREC Authority

Maine's licensing and commission statutes live primarily in [32 M.R.S. §§ 13001–13283](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/32/title32ch114sec0.html). The Maine Real Estate Commission has rulemaking and enforcement power over all licensees, including the authority to investigate complaints, conduct hearings, and impose sanctions.

**The commission collection statute, 32 M.R.S. § 13271, remains the most commonly misunderstood provision.** It requires that any agreement under which a licensee claims a commission must be in writing and signed by the party to be charged. Without that written instrument, a court will dismiss your claim—no matter how much work you performed or how clear the verbal agreement seemed.

Section 13271 also prohibits collecting a fee for referring business to another licensee unless the referral arrangement is disclosed to all parties. This matters for team leaders and multi-state agents who occasionally send leads across brokerage lines.

## Commission Structures and Written Agreements

Since the 2024 NAR settlement and subsequent MLS policy changes, buyer-side commission offers are no longer published on Maine's MLS platforms. That shift places enormous weight on the written buyer representation agreement—specifically, the [Maine Association of Realtors (MAR) Form 803](https://www.mainerealtors.com), the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement.

Agents must now secure Form 803 (or an equivalent brokerage-specific agreement) before showing property, and that form must specify the compensation arrangement. Brokers who allow agents to show homes without an executed buyer agreement expose their firm to liability under both MREC rules and the terms of the NAR settlement's practice changes.

On the listing side, Form 101 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) and Form 102 (Exclusive Agency Listing Agreement) define the seller's commission obligation. The amount, structure, and any concession to cooperating brokerages must be spelled out.

| Form | Purpose | Required Signature | Key Commission Clause |
|------|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| MAR Form 101 | Exclusive Right to Sell | Seller(s) + Listing Broker | Section 4: Compensation |
| MAR Form 102 | Exclusive Agency Listing | Seller(s) + Listing Broker | Section 4: Compensation |
| MAR Form 803 | Exclusive Buyer Agency | Buyer(s) + Buyer Broker | Section 5: Buyer Broker Compensation |
| MAR Form 810 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency | Buyer(s) + Buyer Broker | Section 5: Compensation Terms |

Without these agreements executed properly, commission disputes become nearly unwinnable in court.

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Deadlines

Maine issues three tiers of real estate licenses: salesperson, associate broker, and designated broker. All are governed by 32 M.R.S. § 13174 and renewed on a biennial cycle tied to the licensee's birth month.

**Renewal requires 21 hours of approved continuing education (CE), including a mandatory 3-hour core course set by MREC.** The remaining 18 hours may be elective, but agents should note that ethics training and fair housing coursework, while not separately mandated by Maine statute, satisfy part of the NAR membership requirement if applicable.

Failing to renew by the deadline triggers automatic lapse. A lapsed licensee cannot perform any act of brokerage—cannot negotiate, show property, write offers, or collect compensation. The reinstatement penalty within the first 90 days is a late fee plus proof of completed CE. After 90 days, MREC may require re-examination.

| Scenario | Consequence | Reinstatement Path |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Lapsed 1–90 days | Cannot practice; late fee applies | Complete CE + pay fee |
| Lapsed 91 days–2 years | Cannot practice; fine up to $1,000 | Complete CE + pay fee + possible exam |
| Lapsed 2+ years | License void | Full application, exam, pre-license education |
| Practicing while lapsed | Fine up to $5,000; commission forfeiture | Disciplinary hearing |

Agents juggling transactions near their renewal deadline need to be proactive. A closing that occurs while your license is lapsed means the commission is legally unearnable—and your broker could face discipline for allowing it, just as we see with similar issues [in other states' compliance frameworks](/blog/wisconsin-commission-and-licensing-rules-2026).

## Common Mistakes That Get Maine Agents in Trouble

Five recurring errors account for the majority of MREC disciplinary actions and commission disputes.

**First, agents fail to execute a written buyer agency agreement before showing property.** Under the post-settlement landscape, this is not optional. MREC has signaled that complaints alleging compensation without a signed agreement will be investigated as potential violations of 32 M.R.S. § 13271.

Second, agents miscalculate or misrepresent commission splits on HUD-1 replacement forms (the ALTA Settlement Statement). Even minor discrepancies between the listing agreement's terms and the closing disclosure figures can trigger audits and buyer complaints. Getting the numbers right the first time matters more than ever.

Third, agents negotiate commission directly with the other party's client. Under 32 M.R.S. § 13281, a licensee may not negotiate directly with a party whom the licensee knows to be represented by another licensee—unless that other licensee consents. Commission conversations with represented sellers (if you're the buyer's agent) violate this provision.

Fourth, agents fail to disclose referral fees. Section 13271's referral disclosure requirement applies even when the referral goes to an agent within the same brokerage. Agents who pocket undisclosed referral payments face fines and potential license revocation.

Fifth, agents allow their errors and omissions insurance to lapse. While Maine does not mandate E&O coverage by statute, most brokerages require it contractually. An uninsured agent who causes a deal to collapse due to negligence leaves the broker exposed to the full liability. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry personal liability for the compliance failures of their affiliated agents under 32 M.R.S. § 13173. MREC holds the broker responsible for supervision, and "I didn't know" is not a defense.

**Brokers should audit five specific areas on a quarterly basis.** First, confirm every active agent's license status through the MREC online verification portal. Second, review all buyer agency agreements for completeness—specifically that Section 5 (compensation) is filled in and signed before the first showing date documented in the file.

Third, reconcile commission disbursements against the corresponding listing or buyer agreements. Any disbursement that exceeds or differs from the contractual amount without a signed amendment is a red flag. Fourth, verify that all referral arrangements are documented in writing and disclosed per § 13271. Fifth, confirm that every agent's CE transcript shows compliance before the renewal deadline—not after.

Brokerages with 10 or more agents should consider systematizing these audits rather than relying on manual file reviews. Missing a single lapsed license or unsigned agreement can result in a complaint that triggers a full MREC audit of the entire office. ## Penalties and Enforcement: What MREC Can Do

MREC's enforcement authority under 32 M.R.S. § 13003 allows for escalating sanctions. A first offense for a minor paperwork violation might result in a consent agreement with a small fine (typically $500–$1,000) and mandatory additional CE hours.

More serious violations—practicing without a license, misappropriating client funds, undisclosed dual agency—carry fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension of up to one year, or permanent revocation. MREC publishes disciplinary actions in its quarterly newsletter and on the state's public license lookup, meaning your violation becomes permanently searchable.

Commission forfeiture is the civil consequence that stings most. If a court determines you lacked the required written agreement or were unlicensed at the time of the transaction, you lose the entire commission—even if the transaction closed smoothly and both parties were satisfied. The statute does not provide for partial recovery.

Agents who face MREC complaints should also know that the investigation process itself can take 6–18 months, during which your license status may be flagged. Cooperating with investigators and retaining legal counsel early are the two most protective steps you can take.

## How the NAR Settlement Reshaped Maine Commission Practices

The practice changes effective August 2024 removed cooperative compensation offers from MLS fields nationwide, including all Maine MLS platforms. For Maine agents specifically, this means several operational shifts.

Listing agents must discuss with sellers whether to offer buyer-agent compensation outside the MLS—through listing remarks, direct negotiation, or concession structures. The seller's decision must be documented in the listing agreement or an addendum. Buyer agents must disclose their compensation source to their clients in writing before an offer is submitted, typically within the Form 803 framework.

These changes did not alter Maine statutory law. The requirement for written commission agreements predates the settlement. But the settlement eliminated the practical safety net that MLS-published co-op offers provided. Agents who relied on that system without fully executing their own written agreements are now exposed in ways they weren't before—making documentation discipline the single highest-value skill for Maine agents heading into 2026.
