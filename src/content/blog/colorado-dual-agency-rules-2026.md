## Understanding Colorado Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Colorado handles agency differently than most states, and agents who move here from other jurisdictions often stumble over the distinctions. The Colorado dual agency & designated agency rules 2026 framework is built on the premise that traditional dual agency—where one licensee owes fiduciary duties to both buyer and seller simultaneously—does not exist in this state. It was eliminated in 1994 when the legislature passed the Brokerage Relationships Act, codified at C.R.S. 12-10-401 through 12-10-408.

What Colorado created instead is a three-role system: Single Agent, Transaction-Broker, and Customer. Each role carries different obligations, different disclosure requirements, and different liability profiles. Misidentifying your role or failing to disclose it properly can torpedo a deal and trigger disciplinary proceedings before the [Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC)](https://dora.colorado.gov/real-estate).

## The Three Brokerage Relationships Under Colorado Law

**Colorado defaults every licensee to the Transaction-Broker relationship unless the parties agree in writing to something else.** This is a critical distinction from states where agency must be affirmatively created. Under C.R.S. 12-10-404, the Transaction-Broker owes both parties certain duties—reasonable skill and care, honest dealing, timely disclosure of material facts—but does not owe the full fiduciary advocacy duty that a Single Agent owes.

A Single Agent relationship must be established through a written agreement, as outlined in C.R.S. 12-10-403. When an agent enters a Single Agent relationship with a buyer or seller, that agent owes fiduciary-level duties including loyalty, obedience, confidentiality, disclosure, accounting, and reasonable care. The Customer relationship, meanwhile, provides only the baseline statutory duties: honesty and reasonable skill.

| Relationship Type | Fiduciary Duty | Default Status | Written Agreement Required | Confidentiality Obligation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single Agent | Full fiduciary | No | Yes | Strict—survives termination |
| Transaction-Broker | Limited statutory | Yes (default) | No, but disclosure required | Limited to agreed scope |
| Customer | None beyond honesty | No | No | None beyond statutory |

This structure means that when both the buyer and seller in a single transaction are represented by agents within the same brokerage, the situation is not "dual agency" in the traditional sense. Instead, Colorado permits two Single Agents from the same brokerage to represent opposing parties—what other states might call designated agency—provided proper information barriers exist.

## How Colorado's Version of Designated Agency Actually Works

Colorado does not use the statutory term "designated agency." The functional equivalent occurs when a brokerage has two licensees, one acting as Single Agent for the buyer and the other as Single Agent for the seller, in the same transaction. The employing broker must ensure that confidential information from one client is not shared with the other agent.

**The employing broker in this scenario does not become a dual agent either.** Under CREC's interpretation of C.R.S. 12-10-408, the broker supervises both agents but is not considered to represent both clients in the fiduciary sense, so long as adequate policies, procedures, and physical or informational barriers are maintained. This is where many brokerages fail—they lack written office policies specifying how confidential information is siloed.

Agents operating as Transaction-Brokers for both sides of a deal face a lower bar because Transaction-Brokers never owed the full fiduciary package in the first place. But even Transaction-Brokers must disclose material facts and cannot misrepresent a party's position. The distinction matters enormously at the disciplinary hearing level when CREC evaluates whether an agent breached their duties.

## Required Forms and Disclosure Timing

Colorado Real Estate Commission rules mandate that brokerage relationship disclosures happen at the "earliest reasonable opportunity." For buyer-side agents, this means before substantive discussions about specific properties. For listing agents, this means at or before the listing presentation.

The standard forms agents must use are published by the Colorado Real Estate Commission and updated periodically. The current versions include CBS1-8-24 (Brokerage Disclosure to Buyer) and CBS2-8-24 (Brokerage Disclosure to Seller). These forms explain the three relationship types, confirm which one the agent is operating under, and require signatures. Additionally, when a change in relationship status occurs—say an agent transitions from Transaction-Broker to Single Agent—a new disclosure must be provided.

| Form Number | Purpose | When Presented | Signature Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBS1-8-24 | Brokerage Disclosure to Buyer | Before substantive property discussions | Yes, by buyer |
| CBS2-8-24 | Brokerage Disclosure to Seller | At or before listing presentation | Yes, by seller |
| CBDE | Change of Status disclosure | When relationship type changes | Yes, by affected party |

Timing errors are the single most common compliance failure CREC encounters in audits. An agent who shows property, discusses pricing strategy, and then later presents the disclosure form has already violated the "earliest reasonable opportunity" standard. The form itself acknowledges a specific transaction date and relationship type—backdating or presenting it after substantive discussions have occurred creates both regulatory exposure and potential civil liability.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

CREC enforces brokerage relationship rules through its complaint and disciplinary process under C.R.S. 12-10-201. Penalties range from letters of admonishment to license revocation. Fines can reach $2,500 per violation under the administrative penalty framework, and CREC can also require additional education as a condition of continued licensure.

**Beyond regulatory penalties, failure to properly disclose brokerage relationships opens agents and their brokers to civil lawsuits.** A buyer who discovers their "agent" was actually operating as a Transaction-Broker with no fiduciary duty may claim damages for undisclosed conflicts of interest. Courts have allowed rescission of contracts where material agency disclosure failures occurred, and the affected party may recover actual damages plus attorney fees in some circumstances.

The broker bears personal liability as well. Under C.R.S. 12-10-602, the employing broker is responsible for supervising all licensees and ensuring compliance with the Brokerage Relationships Act. An employing broker who lacks written policies, fails to audit transaction files, or ignores a pattern of disclosure failures faces their own license at risk. ## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Colorado

The first error is assuming the Transaction-Broker role requires no written disclosure. While the relationship exists by default, CREC still requires agents to present the CBS1 or CBS2 form explaining what Transaction-Broker means. The default status does not eliminate the disclosure obligation—it merely establishes the relationship type absent a different written agreement.

The second mistake involves confidentiality breaches within the same brokerage. When two agents in one office represent opposing parties as Single Agents, sharing details about a client's motivation, financial limits, or negotiation flexibility with the other agent is a fiduciary violation. Casual hallway conversations, shared office databases without access restrictions, and group staff meetings where deal details are discussed all create exposure.

Third, agents frequently confuse "facilitation" with Transaction-Brokerage. Some agents believe they can simply "help both sides" without defining their role. Colorado law does not recognize an undefined facilitation role—every licensee must operate as either a Single Agent, Transaction-Broker, or in a Customer relationship. There is no fourth category, and agents cannot create one contractually.

Fourth, agents fail to re-disclose when circumstances change. An agent who lists a property as a Single Agent for the seller but then begins working with an unrepresented buyer on that same property must define the new relationship with the buyer before proceeding. The second relationship does not default silently—it requires affirmative disclosure.

Fifth, agents neglect to document rejected disclosures. When a party refuses to sign the CBS1 or CBS2 form, the agent must note the refusal on the form itself, including the date and circumstances. A missing signature without documentation is indistinguishable from a form that was never presented, and CREC will treat it accordingly during an audit. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers carry the heaviest compliance burden under Colorado's framework. CREC expects brokers to maintain written office policies specifying how brokerage relationships are disclosed, how confidential information is protected in intra-office transactions, and how file reviews are conducted.

A broker's audit should verify five elements in every transaction file: that the correct CBS form is present and signed, that the signature date precedes or coincides with the first substantive interaction, that the relationship type selected on the form matches the duties actually performed, that any mid-transaction status changes are documented with new disclosures, and that confidentiality protocols were followed when two agents in the firm represented opposing parties.

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-report compliance are building on sand.** CREC's enforcement actions in recent years have repeatedly cited employing brokers for systemic supervisory failures rather than isolated agent errors. A quarterly file audit protocol—checking a random sample of 10-15% of closed files—provides defensible evidence of supervisory diligence.
