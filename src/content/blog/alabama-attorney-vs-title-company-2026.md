## Alabama Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of **Alabama attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026** trips up even veteran agents because the answer sits at an uncomfortable intersection of statute, case law, and local custom. Alabama is not a pure "attorney state" like Georgia or South Carolina, nor is it a straightforward "title company state" like California. The result is a hybrid model that demands agents understand exactly where their role ends and where the unauthorized practice of law (UPL) begins.

Getting this wrong does not just embarrass you at the closing table. It exposes your license to disciplinary action from the Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) and could void the transaction's title insurance policy entirely.

## The Statutory Framework Agents Must Know

Alabama Code Section 34-3-6 defines the practice of law broadly, encompassing the preparation of legal instruments, the rendering of legal opinions, and the giving of advice regarding the legal rights of parties to a transaction. The Alabama Supreme Court reinforced this in *Alabama State Bar v. Jamar Title & Escrow, LLC* (2017), holding that title companies cannot independently prepare deeds, mortgages, or other recorded instruments without attorney oversight.

**Section 34-27-36 of the Alabama Real Estate License Law separately prohibits agents from practicing law or holding themselves out as qualified to give legal advice.** Violations can result in license suspension or revocation by AREC, with no requirement that the injured party file a civil suit first. AREC publishes its disciplinary actions quarterly, and UPL-adjacent violations appear with uncomfortable regularity.

The Alabama State Bar's [Formal Opinion 2010-01](https://www.alabar.org) clarifies that a non-attorney closing agent may handle the ministerial act of witnessing signatures and collecting funds, but may not explain the legal effect of documents, resolve title objections, or modify contract language.

## Who Actually Sits at the Table — A Practical Breakdown

Most Alabama closings involve three possible configurations. Understanding which applies to your deal determines your compliance posture and your client's expectations.

| Role | Can Prepare Deed | Can Disburse Funds | Can Explain Legal Effect | Physically Required at Table |
|------|-----------------|-------------------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| Licensed Attorney | Yes | Yes | Yes | Not statutorily mandated, but customary |
| Title Company (non-attorney staff) | No | Yes (escrow only) | No | Not required |
| Real Estate Agent | No | No (unless also licensed as escrow agent) | No | Not required |

In practice, the majority of Alabama counties still follow a closing model where a licensed attorney prepares the deed and title opinion, and either the attorney or a title company agent conducts the physical closing. Mobile and Baldwin counties see more title-company-led closings, while Jefferson and Madison counties lean heavily on attorney-supervised closings. Neither model is legally superior — but the division of labor must remain clear.

**The critical point: someone licensed to practice law in Alabama must prepare the deed and render the title opinion.** Whether that person sits at the table or delegates the signing ceremony to trained non-attorney staff is a matter of firm policy, not statute.

## What Agents Are Actually Allowed to Do at Closing

AREC's Administrative Code, Chapter 790-X-3, governs agent conduct. Agents may attend the closing, answer factual questions about the contract timeline, and hand documents to the appropriate party. They may not interpret title commitments, explain lender documents, or recommend whether a client should sign despite an unresolved title exception.

The Alabama Association of Realtors (AAR) [Form No. 1](https://brittani.ai/pricing) — the standard residential purchase agreement — includes a clause acknowledging that the agent is not providing legal, tax, or environmental advice. That clause protects you only if you actually refrain from giving that advice. Agents who verbally override the written disclaimer lose the protection.

Agents should also be aware that AAR Form No. 25 (the Closing Statement Authorization) and Form No. 28 (the Wire Fraud Advisory) create documentation trails. If you fail to present Form No. 28 and a wire fraud incident occurs, you face both AREC scrutiny and potential civil exposure. ## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Make at Closing

**First, agents routinely fill in legal description blanks on the deed or amendment forms.** This is UPL. Even copying a legal description from MLS into a deed constitutes preparation of a legal instrument under Alabama precedent. The attorney's office handles this — full stop.

Second, agents attempt to resolve title objections by negotiating directly with the title examiner or opposing counsel. Your role is to relay information between the parties and their attorneys. The moment you propose a legal solution to a title defect, you have crossed the line.

Third, agents advise clients to "just sign" when the closing disclosure differs from the loan estimate by more than TRID tolerances. This constitutes legal and financial advice that belongs to the lender's counsel or the borrower's attorney. Your job is to flag the discrepancy and pause — not push the deal forward.

Fourth, agents in dual-agency transactions fail to recommend independent counsel for both parties. Alabama Code Section 34-27-36(a)(11) requires agents to advise parties of material facts. A dual agent who does not suggest each side retain independent legal counsel for closing document review is omitting a material recommendation. The resulting liability falls squarely on you and your broker.

Fifth, agents skip the closing entirely and rely on the title company to "handle everything." If a document is recorded with an error — wrong vesting, wrong legal description, missing notarization — and you never reviewed the file for accuracy before closing, your errors-and-omissions carrier will ask pointed questions about your supervision of the transaction.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Alabama bear supervisory liability under AREC Rule 790-X-3-.02, which states that the qualifying broker is responsible for the acts of all licensees operating under their license. This is not advisory language — it is a liability assignment.

**Brokers should audit three areas quarterly at minimum.** First, review whether agents are presenting AAR Form No. 28 (Wire Fraud Advisory) on every transaction. Missing forms create E&O exposure that cascades upward. Second, confirm that no agent in the office is preparing deeds, amendments to legal instruments, or title affidavits — even as a "favor" for a closing that is running behind. Third, verify that agents are recommending attorney consultation in writing any time a transaction involves a boundary dispute, easement question, or title defect.

A broker who discovers an agent engaging in UPL must report the violation to AREC. Failure to do so transforms the broker from a witness into a co-respondent. AREC's enforcement division has increased its investigative caseload by 18% since 2024, and brokerages that self-report and remediate fare significantly better in the disciplinary process.

However, AREC's updated continuing education curriculum now includes a mandatory one-hour module on UPL awareness, effective January 2026. If the title company cannot produce one, the agent faces a due diligence question that AREC may raise later.

This expands options for rural closings where attorney travel was previously a bottleneck.
