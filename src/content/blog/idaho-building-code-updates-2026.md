## Idaho Building Code Updates 2026: The Shift That Hits Your Transactions

The Idaho building code updates 2026 represent the most significant regulatory change for residential real estate in the state since the 2018 adoption cycle. The Idaho Division of Building Safety (DBS) formally adopted the 2024 International Residential Code (IRC) and 2024 International Building Code (IBC), effective January 1, 2026, replacing the previously enforced 2018 editions. For agents and brokers, this is not just a construction industry concern — it directly affects disclosures, contingencies, and your personal liability exposure on every transaction involving new builds, renovations, or properties with unpermitted work.

Idaho Code Section 39-4109 grants the Idaho Building Code Board authority to adopt and amend building codes statewide. The Board's decision to skip the 2021 editions entirely and jump to 2024 means the gap between old and new requirements is wider than usual. Energy efficiency mandates, egress window specifications, structural bracing requirements, and electrical panel standards all changed materially. If you are listing a home built or remodeled under the old codes, or representing a buyer on new construction, you need to understand how these updates ripple into your deal files.

## What Actually Changed in the 2024 IRC/IBC Adoption

**The most consequential changes for residential agents fall into four categories: energy performance, fire safety, electrical requirements, and accessibility.** Idaho's adoption includes state-specific amendments published by the DBS, so the codes are not identical to the base ICC publications. The Idaho Building Code Board exercised its amendment authority under Idaho Code Section 39-4107 to modify certain energy provisions it deemed impractical for Idaho's climate zones.

Energy performance requirements now mandate continuous insulation on exterior walls in Climate Zones 5 and 6, which covers most of Idaho outside the Snake River Plain. Blower door testing is mandatory for all new residential construction — a requirement that did not exist under the 2018 IRC as adopted in Idaho. Homes that fail the air-tightness threshold cannot receive a Certificate of Occupancy.

Fire safety provisions expand sprinkler alternatives for townhome construction and modify fire separation distances for accessory dwelling units (ADUs). Given Idaho's recent ADU legislation under House Bill 285 (2023), which preempted local zoning restrictions on ADUs, agents will see more ADU transactions where the 2024 fire separation rules apply. Electrical panel capacity minimums increased from 100-amp to 200-amp service for new residential construction exceeding 2,000 square feet.

## How This Affects the RE-25 and Disclosure Obligations

Idaho's statutory disclosure framework lives in Idaho Code Title 55, Chapter 25. The RE-25 Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Form requires sellers to disclose known material defects, and agents have an independent duty under Idaho Code Section 54-2087 not to misrepresent or conceal material facts. The code updates create new disclosure friction in three ways.

First, sellers who completed renovations between 2018 and 2025 may have pulled permits under the old code. Those improvements are grandfathered. But sellers who completed work without permits now face a sharper compliance gap because the difference between old and current code is larger. An unpermitted bathroom addition that might have passed under the 2018 IRC may fail multiple 2024 IRC standards, making retroactive permitting more expensive and less likely.

**Second, agents listing properties with known unpermitted work must disclose that fact on the RE-25, regardless of whether the work "would have passed" under any code.** The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) has issued guidance letters confirming that knowledge of unpermitted improvements constitutes a material fact. Silence is not an option.

Third, new construction transactions require agents to verify that the Certificate of Occupancy reflects compliance with the 2024 codes now in effect. A CO issued under the old codes for a project that broke ground before January 1, 2026, is still valid — but agents should confirm the timeline to avoid buyer objections during due diligence.

| Scenario | Old Code (2018 IRC) | New Code (2024 IRC) | Agent Action Required |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| New construction CO | 2018 standards | 2024 standards | Verify CO date and code edition |
| Unpermitted renovation | Smaller compliance gap | Larger compliance gap | Disclose on RE-25 |
| ADU fire separation | 5-foot minimum | Modified separation per DBS amendment | Confirm permit compliance |
| Electrical service (2,000+ sq ft) | 100-amp acceptable | 200-amp required | Inform buyer of upgrade costs |
| Blower door testing | Not required | Mandatory for new builds | Confirm test results pre-closing |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Changes

Mistakes in this area tend to cluster around the same patterns, and IREC disciplinary actions confirm the trend. Here are the errors most likely to generate complaints or liability exposure under the 2026 updates.

**Mistake one: Assuming all code compliance is the seller's problem.** Under Idaho Code Section 54-2087, agents have an independent duty regarding material facts. If you know a property has unpermitted work or code violations, you cannot hide behind the seller's failure to disclose. IREC can discipline your license even if the seller was the primary wrongdoer.

Mistake two: Failing to recommend a permit history search. While agents are not obligated to independently research permits, failing to advise buyers to request permit records from the local building department is a missed standard of care that plaintiff's attorneys love to highlight. A single sentence in your buyer advisory — documented in writing — protects you.

Mistake three: Confusing grandfathering with blanket immunity. A home built in 1995 does not need to meet 2024 IRC standards to be sold. But the moment a seller adds a deck, finishes a basement, or converts a garage, the new work must meet current code. Agents who tell buyers "the whole house is grandfathered" are making a representation that is factually wrong and potentially actionable.

Mistake four: Ignoring jurisdiction-specific amendments. Idaho allows local jurisdictions to adopt more restrictive codes under Idaho Code Section 39-4116. Boise, Meridian, and Coeur d'Alene all maintain local amendments. An agent operating across multiple jurisdictions who applies Boise rules to a Nampa transaction — or vice versa — risks giving inaccurate guidance. Always check the local building department's adopted amendments, not just the state baseline.

Mistake five: Removing inspection contingencies prematurely on new construction. The [reasons deals fall apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often trace back to agents who released contingencies before a CO was issued. Under the 2024 codes, the CO process may take longer due to mandatory blower door testing and additional inspections. Build buffer time into your contingency deadlines.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory liability under Idaho Code Section 54-2038. If an agent in your brokerage mishandles a disclosure related to building code compliance, IREC can hold you accountable for inadequate supervision. Here is what your audit process should include for 2026.

Review all active listings for properties with known renovations completed without permits. Cross-reference the RE-25 forms on file to confirm disclosure. If a seller checked "no" on the unpermitted work question but your agent has emails or texts suggesting otherwise, you have a file that needs immediate correction.

Update your transaction checklists to include a CO verification step for all new construction transactions. The [reason checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is well-documented — and this is exactly the type of binary verification task that benefits from systematization. Your checklist should specify: confirm CO was issued under 2024 IRC/IBC, or confirm permit predates January 1, 2026.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|-------------------|-----------|----------------------|
| RE-25 completion review | Every listing | Signed form in file within 5 days of listing |
| CO verification (new construction) | Every new build closing | Copy of CO with code edition noted |
| Agent training on code updates | Annually minimum | Signed attendance record |
| Permit disclosure cross-check | Quarterly file audit | RE-25 compared against MLS remarks |
| Local amendment awareness | Per jurisdiction | Written reference guide distributed to agents |

Brokers should also confirm that their agents are not making affirmative representations about code compliance in MLS remarks or marketing materials. Phrases like "fully up to code" or "meets all current building standards" are liability magnets unless supported by a professional inspection report. Train your agents to describe features factually without implying code compliance they have not verified.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Get This Wrong

IREC maintains disciplinary authority under Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20. Violations related to misrepresentation or failure to disclose material facts can result in license suspension, revocation, or civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation under Idaho Code Section 54-2064. Beyond regulatory discipline, agents face civil liability in private lawsuits.

Idaho courts have recognized negligent misrepresentation claims against agents who fail to disclose known defects. In transactions where undisclosed code violations later cost buyers tens of thousands in remediation, the agent's errors and omissions insurance will likely face a claim. Deductibles, premium increases, and the reputational damage of a complaint on your [IREC license lookup](https://irec.idaho.gov/) record are real consequences.

Deal cancellation is the more immediate risk. Buyers who discover code violations during inspection — or worse, after closing — can pursue rescission under fraud theories. Even where rescission fails, the litigation costs and settlement pressure make prevention far cheaper than defense. The [pattern of missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that leads to expired contingencies often compounds code-related disputes, because buyers lose their contractual exit ramp before discovering the problem.
