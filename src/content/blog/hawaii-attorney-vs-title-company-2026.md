## Hawaii Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Definitive Agent Guide

Hawaii sits in a unique position on the mainland-to-islands spectrum of closing customs. The question of "Hawaii attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026" comes up every time an agent from an attorney-state relocates to the islands or a new licensee closes their first deal. The short answer: Hawaii is an escrow state, and no statute mandates attorney presence at settlement — but the details matter more than the headline.

The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC), operating under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), does not require attorney oversight of closings. Instead, Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 449 governs escrow depositories, and HRS Chapter 431 Article 20 regulates title insurance companies that often double as settlement agents. Agents who conflate these roles — or assume any warm body with a notary stamp can run closing — create liability exposure that can unwind deals and trigger license discipline.

## Who Is Legally Authorized to Conduct Closing in Hawaii

**Only four categories of entities may lawfully handle real estate closings in Hawaii.** These are defined across HRS 449-1, HRS 431:20-104, and Hawaii Supreme Court Rule 1.15 for attorneys holding client funds.

| Entity Type | Governing Authority | Physical Presence Required at Closing |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed Escrow Company | HRS Chapter 449, DCCA | Not required by statute; company designates authorized signers |
| Title Insurance Company | HRS 431 Article 20 | Not required; settlement agent acts on behalf |
| Hawaii-barred Attorney | Hawaii Supreme Court Rules | Not required unless retained for legal representation |
| Financial Institution | HRS 449-1 exemption | Not required; bank trust department may facilitate |

The practical default on Oahu and the neighbor islands is for a title and escrow company — often the same entity — to handle settlement. Companies like Title Guaranty of Hawaii and First American Title operate under dual authority. The agent's job is not to choose which type of entity must attend, but to confirm that whoever is handling funds and recording documents holds proper licensure.

## What Hawaii Law Actually Says About Escrow and Title

HRS Section 449-2 defines an escrow depository as any person who receives deposits of escrow funds in connection with real estate transactions. The DCCA Division of Financial Institutions licenses these depositories and audits them annually. If an unlicensed person or entity accepts escrow funds, they commit a misdemeanor under HRS 449-17, carrying fines up to $10,000 per violation and potential imprisonment.

**Title insurance underwriters operating in Hawaii must hold a certificate of authority from the Insurance Division under HRS 431:20-104.** Their settlement agents — the people who actually sit across the table from your clients — must be employees or authorized agents of that underwriter. This is where agents sometimes get confused: a settlement agent is not the same as a title company officer, and neither is automatically an attorney.

For transactions involving Hawaiian Home Lands or properties held in trust through the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands (DHHL), additional requirements exist. DHHL leasehold transactions often require approval from the Hawaiian Homes Commission, and while an attorney is not mandated by closing law itself, the complexity of these conveyances makes legal counsel a practical necessity. Agents working DHHL deals who skip this step frequently see transactions stall for months.

## The Role of Attorneys in Hawaii Closings — Optional but Strategic

Hawaii is not an "attorney state" in the way Massachusetts, New York, or Connecticut require lawyer involvement. The [Wisconsin approach to attorney vs. title company requirements](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026) offers an interesting mainland comparison, but Hawaii's escrow-centric model functions differently.

An attorney's involvement in a Hawaii closing is elective. Buyers or sellers may retain counsel to review documents, negotiate terms, or oversee the closing. However, no HIREC rule or HRS provision mandates their attendance at the settlement table. The Hawaii Association of Realtors (HAR) standard forms — specifically the Purchase Contract (form DROA, revised 2025) — contain an attorney review clause at Paragraph C-1 allowing either party to have documents reviewed by counsel within a specified period.

When attorneys do participate, they typically handle title opinions on complex parcels, review condo documents governed by HRS Chapter 514B, or represent parties in 1031 exchanges where timing under IRC Section 1031 demands precision. Agents should understand that recommending attorney involvement for complex deals is good practice; requiring it as a condition of representation would exceed agent authority.

## Common Mistakes Hawaii Agents Make at Closing

Errors in understanding closing authority create three categories of risk: deal cancellation, personal liability, and license discipline by HIREC under HRS 467-14.

**First mistake: directing escrow funds to an unlicensed entity.** Some agents, particularly those handling for-sale-by-owner conversions or investment transactions, allow earnest money to be held by an unlicensed party. Under HRS 449-2, only licensed escrow depositories or exempt entities (banks, attorneys) may hold these funds. An agent who facilitates this arrangement faces discipline under HRS 467-14(8) for conduct constituting fraud or dishonest dealing.

Second mistake: practicing law without a license. Agents who draft addenda changing legal rights, interpret title report exceptions for clients, or advise on lease-to-fee conversion implications under HRS 516 risk violating HRS 605-14 (unauthorized practice of law). HIREC has issued guidance through its Real Estate Bulletin clarifying that explaining form provisions is permissible, but rendering legal opinions is not. The line is thinner than most agents realize.

Third mistake: failing to verify escrow company licensing before opening escrow. DCCA maintains a public database of licensed escrow companies. Agents who skip verification and later discover their escrow company operated without proper licensure face transaction unwinding and potential E&O claims. The [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wisconsin-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) that apply across states are equally relevant here — Hawaii has seen increased closing fraud involving spoofed escrow company communications.

Fourth mistake: providing title and escrow referrals tied to financial arrangements. RESPA Section 8 prohibits kickbacks, and Hawaii reinforces this through HRS 467-14(20), which authorizes discipline for any conduct that violates federal law incorporated by reference. Affiliated Business Arrangements must be disclosed on the RESPA-compliant AfBA form, and agents who receive referral fees from title companies without proper disclosure risk both federal and state penalties.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Hawaii carry supervisory obligations under HAR Administrative Rules Section 16-99-3. The broker is ultimately responsible for ensuring that agents under their supervision comply with closing procedures, and HIREC has historically penalized brokers for systemic failures — not just individual agent missteps.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Escrow Company Licensing | Verify DCCA license status for every escrow company agents use | Quarterly review of active escrow list |
| Earnest Money Handling | Confirm deposits reach escrow within 5 business days per contract terms | Every transaction |
| Attorney Review Deadlines | Ensure agents track C-1 attorney review period expiration | Every transaction |
| AfBA Disclosure Compliance | Review all title/escrow referral relationships for proper disclosure | Annual audit |
| Form Version Currency | Confirm agents use current HAR form versions (DROA, BIC, SIC) | Semi-annual check |

**Brokers who fail to maintain these audit processes expose their firms to vicarious liability under HRS 467-1.6.** HIREC's enforcement pattern shows increasing willingness to issue consent orders requiring brokerages to implement compliance monitoring as a condition of continued licensing. The days of "I didn't know my agent was doing that" as a defense are functionally over.

Transaction files should contain evidence that the closing entity holds proper authority. This means a copy of the escrow company's DCCA license or confirmation of the title insurer's certificate of authority should appear in every file. Brokers managing high-volume offices often [struggle with deadline tracking and file completeness](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), making systematic auditing rather than spot-checking the only defensible approach.

## Hawaii-Specific Forms Agents Must Know for Closing

The Hawaii Association of Realtors publishes standard forms that govern most residential transactions. The Deposit Receipt Offer and Acceptance (DROA) is the primary purchase contract. Within it, several provisions directly relate to closing authority and escrow handling.

Paragraph B-2 of the DROA identifies the escrow company and establishes which party selects the settlement agent. Paragraph F addresses title insurance and identifies who pays for the owner's and lender's policies. Paragraph C-1, as noted earlier, provides the optional attorney review window. Agents must fill these sections completely — leaving them blank creates ambiguity that title companies may refuse to close around.

Additional forms relevant to closing include the Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement (form SRPD), the Buyer's Information and Condition Sheet (BIC), and the Seller's Information and Condition Sheet (SIC). While these are pre-closing documents, title companies routinely require their presence in the file before scheduling settlement. Missing forms do not just delay closing; under HRS 508D-5, failure to provide the SRPD can give buyers a statutory right to rescind up to the earlier of 15 calendar days after receipt or closing.

> HRS 508D-5: "If the seller fails to provide the disclosure statement as required, the buyer may rescind the transaction at any time prior to the earlier of (1) fifteen calendar days after the buyer's receipt of the disclosure statement; or (2) the date of closing."

## Liability Exposure When Agents Get It Wrong

HIREC processes complaints through its Regulated Industries Complaints Office (RICO). Penalties for closing-related violations range from mandatory education courses to license revocation. Between 2023 and 2025, HIREC consent orders related to escrow and closing irregularities averaged fines of $2,500 to $7,500, with repeat offenders facing suspension.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. If a transaction fails because an agent directed funds to an unlicensed escrow company, the agent's E&O policy may deny coverage on the basis that the agent violated a known statutory requirement. The buyer or seller can pursue damages directly against the agent and the brokerage under respondeat superior principles codified in Hawaii case law.

The [Hawaii DCCA Real Estate Branch](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) publishes enforcement actions publicly. Agents should review these quarterly — not as morbid curiosity but as continuing education in what not to do. Patterns in enforcement reveal HIREC's current priorities, and escrow handling has risen sharply on that list since 2024.
