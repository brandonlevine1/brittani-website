## Understanding Delaware Continuing Education Requirements 2026

The Delaware continuing education requirements 2026 cycle catches more agents off guard than any other compliance obligation in the state. Every two years, the Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC) mandates that all active licensees complete a specific block of continuing education hours before their renewal date. Missing this deadline does not trigger a grace period or a gentle reminder — it triggers a license lapse.

Delaware's regulatory framework for real estate education falls under **24 Del. C. Chapter 29**, the statute governing the Delaware Real Estate Commission's authority over licensing, education, and disciplinary action. The Commission operates under the Division of Professional Regulation within the Delaware Department of State. All continuing education providers, course content, and credit hours must receive prior approval from the Commission before they count toward renewal.

The current renewal cycle runs on a biennial schedule, with licenses expiring on the last day of the licensee's birth month every two years. That means your deadline is not the same as your colleague's, and no blanket "renewal season" exists the way it does in some neighboring states.

## Credit Hour Breakdown: What the State Actually Requires

**Delaware requires 21 hours of approved continuing education per renewal cycle for both salespersons and associate brokers.** Brokers-in-charge carry the same 21-hour requirement but face additional supervisory expectations we will cover later. Of those 21 hours, the Commission mandates specific topic allocations that cannot be substituted with electives.

The mandatory breakdown looks like this:

| Requirement | Hours | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fair Housing | 3 | Must cover federal and Delaware fair housing law |
| Ethics | 3 | Commission-approved ethics course required |
| Agency Relationships | 3 | Delaware-specific agency law and disclosure |
| Electives | 12 | Any DREC-approved real estate topic |
| **Total** | **21** | **Per two-year renewal cycle** |

The three-hour fair housing component must address both federal requirements under the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. 3601 et seq.) and Delaware's own fair housing statute, 6 Del. C. Chapter 46. Agents who take a generic national fair housing course without Delaware-specific content risk having those hours rejected by the Commission during audit.

Elective hours offer more flexibility, covering topics like contract law, property management, commercial transactions, or technology in real estate. However, every elective course must carry a DREC approval number. If your certificate of completion does not list a Delaware approval number, the hours do not count — regardless of what the course provider told you at checkout.

## The Renewal Process and Key Forms

Delaware uses an online renewal system through the Division of Professional Regulation's licensing portal. Agents submit their renewal application electronically and must attest that they have completed all required CE hours. The Commission does not require agents to upload certificates at the time of renewal, but it conducts random audits and can request documentation at any time within two years of renewal.

**The primary form agents interact with is the Real Estate License Renewal Application, submitted through the Delaware Professional Regulation Online Services (DELPROS) portal.** Payment of the biennial renewal fee — currently $143 for salespersons and $193 for brokers — accompanies the application. Agents who hold an inactive license still must renew, though the CE requirements differ: inactive licensees are exempt from the 21-hour requirement but must reactivate through a separate process that includes completing CE if they wish to return to active status.

For agents transferring from another state, Delaware does not offer blanket CE reciprocity. The Commission evaluates [license reciprocity and portability](/blog/wisconsin-license-reciprocity-portability-2026) requests on an individual basis, and out-of-state CE credits rarely satisfy Delaware's mandatory topic requirements. You will almost certainly need to complete the Delaware-specific fair housing and agency modules regardless of what you completed elsewhere.

## What Happens When You Fail to Comply

The consequences of missing your CE deadline in Delaware are not theoretical. Under 24 Del. C. Section 2912, the Commission has authority to refuse renewal, suspend, revoke, or impose civil penalties on any licensee who fails to meet continuing education requirements. Fines can reach $1,000 per violation, and each transaction conducted on a lapsed license constitutes a separate violation.

Here is what the penalty structure looks like in practice:

| Violation | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|
| Practicing on a lapsed license | Fine up to $1,000 per transaction, possible license revocation |
| Failing to complete CE by renewal date | License lapses; cannot practice until CE is completed and renewal is processed |
| Lapse exceeding one year | May be required to retake the Delaware licensing examination |
| Falsely attesting to CE completion | Disciplinary action including suspension or revocation, plus fines |
| Broker failing to verify agent compliance | Broker faces separate disciplinary action under supervisory obligations |

**Any transaction you participate in while your license is lapsed can be challenged by the other party.** Buyers or sellers who discover their agent was unlicensed at the time of contract execution may have grounds to void the agreement, seek return of commissions, or file a complaint with DREC. The liability exposure extends to your brokerage, which may face its own disciplinary proceedings for allowing an unlicensed person to conduct business under its supervision.

This is not a hypothetical concern. DREC publishes disciplinary actions on the [Delaware Division of Professional Regulation website](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/), and CE-related violations appear regularly. The Commission treats false attestation — checking the box that says you completed your hours when you did not — as a more serious offense than simply missing the deadline.

## Common Mistakes That Put Your License at Risk

Five recurring errors show up in DREC disciplinary records and audit findings year after year. Each one is avoidable with basic planning, yet agents continue making them.

**First, agents assume that any real estate course counts toward Delaware CE.** It does not. Courses must carry specific DREC approval, and national courses from providers like NAR or large franchise training departments do not automatically qualify unless the provider has obtained separate Delaware approval. Always verify the DREC course approval number before enrolling.

Second, agents complete their hours but fail to retain certificates of completion. The Commission can audit you up to two years after renewal, and the burden of proof falls entirely on you. If you cannot produce a certificate with your name, the course title, the provider's DREC approval number, the date of completion, and the number of credit hours, those hours are treated as incomplete.

Third, agents confuse the mandatory topic requirements with electives. Taking 21 hours of elective courses does not satisfy the requirement. You must complete the specific three-hour blocks in fair housing, ethics, and agency relationships. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on the mandatory modules and scramble to find last-minute approved courses often discover that availability is limited close to renewal dates.

Fourth, agents on teams assume their team leader or transaction coordinator handles CE tracking. While a good [transaction coordinator](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) manages many compliance tasks, your continuing education is your personal licensing obligation. No one else can complete the hours or submit your renewal application on your behalf.

Fifth, agents who hold licenses in multiple states sometimes double-count hours across jurisdictions. Delaware does not accept CE credits earned to satisfy another state's requirements unless those specific courses also carry DREC approval. The hours must be independently approved for Delaware.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry a distinct supervisory obligation under Delaware law that goes beyond simply managing their own CE compliance. Under 24 Del. C. Section 2920, brokers are responsible for the supervision of all licensees affiliated with their office, and that supervision explicitly includes verifying that each agent's license is current and active.

**A broker who allows an agent with a lapsed license to conduct real estate activity faces independent disciplinary action.** This is not a vicarious liability theory — it is a direct violation of the broker's statutory duty. DREC has historically pursued brokers who failed to implement systems for tracking agent license status, even when the broker had no actual knowledge of the lapse.

Effective broker compliance programs include three elements. First, maintain a centralized record of every affiliated agent's license expiration date and CE completion status. Second, require agents to submit copies of CE certificates to the brokerage at least 30 days before their individual renewal dates. Third, run quarterly checks on agent license status through the DELPROS verification system.

Brokers should also be aware that [team supervision structures](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) create additional complexity. When agents operate under team names or team leaders who are not the broker-in-charge, the accountability chain can become murky. Regardless of internal team arrangements, the broker-in-charge remains the legally responsible party for license compliance.

## Building a System That Actually Works

Treating CE compliance as a last-minute task is the single biggest reason agents end up in disciplinary proceedings. The agents who never face audit issues are the ones who complete their mandatory hours in the first six months of the renewal cycle and their electives over the following twelve months, leaving the final six months as a buffer.

Calendar-based reminders are a start, but they fail when agents ignore them or change phone numbers. Agents and brokers who treat CE as a core business function rather than an afterthought will stay licensed, avoid fines, and keep their transactions on solid legal ground.
