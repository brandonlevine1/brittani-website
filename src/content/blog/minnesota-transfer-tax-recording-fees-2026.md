## Understanding Minnesota Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on a closing disclosure is one of the fastest ways to delay a deal, frustrate a client, and attract scrutiny from your broker. The Minnesota transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 have not changed dramatically from the prior year, but the margin for error remains razor-thin because county recorders will reject documents that arrive with incorrect fees or incomplete electronic certificates. Agents who memorize these figures—and understand the statutory framework behind them—close with fewer surprises.

Minnesota imposes two primary transfer-related taxes on real property conveyances: the state deed tax governed by Minnesota Statutes Chapter 287, and the mortgage registry tax governed by the same chapter. Recording fees are set at the county level but must conform to the fee schedule authorized under Minn. Stat. 386.77 and 386.78. The interplay between state tax, county fees, and the electronic Certificate of Real Estate Value (eCRV) system creates a compliance chain that agents need to respect from listing through post-closing.

## The State Deed Tax: Rate, Calculation, and Who Pays

**Minnesota's deed tax rate has remained at $3.30 per $1,000 of net consideration since its last adjustment.** "Net consideration" means the purchase price minus any liens assumed by the buyer, which is defined explicitly in Minn. Stat. 287.21, Subd. 1. If a buyer assumes a $50,000 existing mortgage on a $350,000 sale, the taxable consideration drops to $300,000 and the deed tax is $990 instead of $1,155.

By statute and universal custom in Minnesota, the seller pays the deed tax. This obligation shows up on the seller's side of the closing statement. Agents should confirm this allocation in the purchase agreement and verify it again on the preliminary settlement statement—misallocation generates revision requests that cost everyone time.

Hennepin County and Ramsey County also impose an additional environmental response fund tax at $0.0001 per dollar of consideration for properties within their jurisdiction, though this amount is negligible on most residential deals. Agents working metro transactions should still confirm their title company accounts for it.

## Mortgage Registry Tax: The Buyer's Side

The mortgage registry tax applies when a new mortgage is recorded against Minnesota real property. The rate is $0.0023 of the principal debt secured, or $2.30 per $1,000 of mortgage amount. A $320,000 mortgage triggers a tax of $736.

This tax is the borrower's responsibility, and it appears on the buyer's side of the closing statement. One source of confusion: refinances also trigger mortgage registry tax unless they qualify for a specific exemption, which means agents advising clients on post-close refinancing timelines should note the additional cost.

| Fee or Tax | Rate | Paid By | Statutory Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| State Deed Tax | $3.30 per $1,000 of net consideration | Seller | Minn. Stat. 287.21 |
| Mortgage Registry Tax | $2.30 per $1,000 of principal debt | Buyer | Minn. Stat. 287.05 |
| Hennepin/Ramsey Environmental Fund | $0.0001 per dollar of consideration | Seller | Minn. Stat. 287.08 |
| State Deed Tax Minimum | $1.65 (for consideration under $500) | Seller | Minn. Stat. 287.21 |

## Recording Fees: County-Level Schedules Under Minn. Stat. 386.77

Recording fees in Minnesota are authorized under Minn. Stat. 386.77 and 386.78. Counties set their own fee schedules within the statutory parameters, and there is meaningful variation between metro and outstate counties. Most counties charge a base fee for the first page and an additional per-page fee, plus a surcharge that funds the county's technology improvements.

For 2026, the typical county recording fee structure looks like this:

| Document Type | First Page | Each Additional Page | Technology Surcharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deed (Warranty, Quit Claim) | $46.00 | $2.00 | Included |
| Mortgage | $46.00 | $2.00 | Included |
| Satisfaction of Mortgage | $46.00 | $2.00 | Included |
| Assignment of Mortgage | $46.00 | $2.00 | Included |
| Notice of Lis Pendens | $46.00 | $2.00 | Included |

**Agents should not assume that every county charges the identical base amount.** Dakota County, for example, publishes a slightly different schedule than St. Louis County. Always confirm fees with the county recorder's office or rely on your title company's fee calculator. The safest practice is to verify the schedule directly from the [Minnesota Secretary of State or county recorder websites](https://www.sos.state.mn.us/) before quoting fees to a client.

## The eCRV Requirement: Form, Filing, and Rejections

Every transfer of real property in Minnesota requires an electronic Certificate of Real Estate Value filed with the Minnesota Department of Revenue. The eCRV replaced the old paper CRV (form CR-1) and must be completed online through the Department of Revenue's eCRV system. Without an accepted eCRV number, the county recorder will not record the deed—period.

The eCRV captures purchase price, property characteristics, and transaction details. It is typically completed by the closing agent, but the listing agent and buyer's agent are often the source of the data that populates it. Errors in sale price, property identification number, or exemption codes cause rejections. A rejection means the deed sits unrecorded until someone corrects and resubmits the filing.

Exempt transactions still require eCRV filing. Gift transfers, transfers between spouses during dissolution, and other exempt conveyances must be filed with the correct exemption code selected. Agents handling estate sales or intrafamily transfers cannot skip this step.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Minnesota Transfer Taxes and Recording

**Mistake one: quoting the deed tax on gross sale price when liens are being assumed.** Net consideration is the statutory basis for the tax, and failing to subtract assumed encumbrances from the calculation overstates the seller's cost. This error erodes client trust and creates last-minute closing statement revisions.

Mistake two: confusing who pays what. Some agents from other states assume the buyer splits or pays the deed tax. In Minnesota, custom and statute place it on the seller. Misallocating fees in the purchase agreement can trigger a contract dispute if the other party's agent catches it after acceptance. Review how other states handle this differently in our post on [Wisconsin transfer tax and recording fees](/blog/wisconsin-transfer-tax-recording-fees-2026).

Mistake three: failing to account for the mortgage registry tax in the buyer's estimated closing costs. A $400,000 loan triggers $920 in mortgage registry tax—hardly a rounding error for a first-time buyer. Agents who leave this off the buyer net sheet look either sloppy or dishonest when the final disclosure arrives.

Mistake four: submitting incorrect property identification numbers on the eCRV. Transposing digits or using an outdated PID from a prior split or combination delays recording. Always pull the current PID from the county's property tax records rather than relying on MLS data alone.

Mistake five: ignoring county-specific recording fees when estimating seller proceeds on listings outside the agent's home county. A $10 discrepancy per document might seem minor, but on a transaction with multiple recorded documents it compounds—and clients remember when your estimate was wrong.

## What Happens If You Get It Wrong: Liability and Consequences

The most immediate consequence of fee miscalculation is a delayed recording. When a deed cannot be recorded, the buyer does not hold record title, which creates a gap in the chain of ownership. During that gap, intervening liens or judgments against the seller could theoretically attach to the property. [Understanding why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at this stage helps illustrate the downstream risk.

Beyond recording delays, agents face contractual liability. If the purchase agreement specifies a closing date and the transaction cannot close because the eCRV was rejected or fees were shorted, the non-breaching party may be entitled to remedies under the contract. In extreme cases, the Minnesota Department of Commerce could view pattern failures as a competency issue during license renewal audits.

Title companies generally catch fee errors before they reach the recorder, but agents should not treat the title company as a safety net. Your preliminary estimate to the client sets expectations, and a significant miss—especially on the seller's side—damages the agent-client relationship in ways that show up in reviews and referral rates.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Minnesota carry supervisory liability under Minn. Stat. 82.81 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. This means a pattern of fee miscalculations or eCRV errors within a brokerage is not just an agent-level problem—it exposes the broker to Department of Commerce scrutiny.

**Brokers should audit a sample of closed transaction files quarterly to verify that deed tax and mortgage registry tax were correctly calculated and properly allocated.** A simple spreadsheet comparing the sale price, assumed liens, and tax paid will flag discrepancies in minutes. Any agent who consistently miscalculates should receive additional training or supervision.

Brokers should also confirm that agents are not quoting fees verbally without disclaimers. Minnesota statute does not prohibit agents from providing fee estimates, but providing incorrect estimates without a written disclaimer that amounts are approximate invites consumer complaints. Establish a brokerage policy requiring all fee estimates to reference the title company's preliminary figures rather than agent guesswork.

File review should also verify that every closed file contains proof that the eCRV was accepted prior to recording. The accepted eCRV confirmation number ties the transaction to the state's records. If an agent's file lacks this documentation, the broker has an incomplete file—a finding that can surface during a Department of Commerce audit. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on eCRV filings put the entire brokerage at risk.
