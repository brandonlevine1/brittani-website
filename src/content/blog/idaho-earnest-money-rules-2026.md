## Understanding Idaho Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Idaho earnest money & escrow laws 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who fail to handle trust funds correctly. The Idaho Real Estate Commission (IREC) enforces these rules under Idaho Code Title 54, Chapter 20, and the administrative rules found in IDAPA 24.33.01. Every dollar of earnest money that passes through your hands or your brokerage's trust account is subject to strict deposit timelines, accounting protocols, and disbursement restrictions.

This is not a topic where "close enough" works. IREC disciplinary actions in recent years show a consistent pattern of agents losing licenses over trust account violations that started as simple administrative oversights. The stakes are license revocation, civil liability, and deal cancellation—often all three at once.

## The Statutory Framework: Idaho Code 54-2051 and IDAPA 24.33.01

**Idaho Code Section 54-2051 is the backbone of every trust fund obligation for licensed brokers in the state.** It mandates that all earnest money, security deposits, and other trust funds received by a licensee on behalf of others must be deposited into a trust account maintained by the designated or responsible broker. The statute does not allow agents to hold checks in a desk drawer, a car console, or anywhere else while "waiting for the deal to firm up."

IDAPA 24.33.01, Rule 600 supplements the statute with specifics on trust account maintenance. Rule 600 requires that trust accounts be maintained in an Idaho-based federally insured financial institution and that records be kept for a minimum of five years. The rule also mandates monthly reconciliations of every trust account, with discrepancies reported to IREC within 30 days.

The [Idaho Real Estate Commission's official statutes page](https://irec.idaho.gov/laws-and-rules/) is the authoritative source for current rule text. Agents who rely solely on their pre-licensing course notes are playing a dangerous game with outdated information.

## Deposit Timelines and the RE-21 Purchase Agreement

The standard Idaho REALTORS form—RE-21 (Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement)—specifies that earnest money shall be delivered within three business days of acceptance unless the parties negotiate a different timeline. This three-day window is not a suggestion. It is a contractual obligation that IREC treats as a compliance requirement when investigating complaints.

**If you receive a check on Friday afternoon and acceptance occurs that same day, you have until end of business Wednesday to deposit.** Weekends and state holidays do not count as business days under Idaho's general statutory interpretation. The clock starts ticking at mutual acceptance, not at the moment you physically receive the funds.

Some agents negotiate longer deposit windows in the "Other Terms" section of the RE-21. This is permissible, but it must be explicit. A vague notation like "EMD to be deposited soon" is not a valid extension and will not protect you in a dispute.

| Scenario | Deposit Deadline | Governing Authority |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Standard RE-21 terms | 3 business days from acceptance | RE-21, Section 3 |
| Negotiated extension (written) | Per agreement terms | RE-21, Other Terms |
| Cash earnest money | Same business day or next | IDAPA 24.33.01, Rule 600 |
| Wire transfer | Must be initiated within 3 business days | RE-21 + brokerage policy |
| Post-dated check (discouraged) | Deposit on check date, within 3 business days of acceptance if date has passed | Brokerage discretion + IREC guidance |

## Who Holds the Funds: Trust Account Requirements

Idaho law places the trust account obligation squarely on the designated broker or responsible broker. Individual agents cannot maintain their own trust accounts. The RE-21 identifies the holder of earnest money—typically the listing brokerage or a title/escrow company—and that designation controls where the funds are deposited.

Rule 600 requires that trust accounts be non-interest-bearing unless the parties provide written consent for an interest-bearing account and designate who receives the interest. Most brokerages avoid interest-bearing trust accounts entirely because the administrative burden of tracking interest allocation across dozens of transactions outweighs any benefit.

Title companies acting as escrow holders in Idaho must be licensed under the Idaho Department of Insurance and comply with separate escrow regulations under Idaho Code Title 30. When the RE-21 designates a title company as the holder, the brokerage's trust account obligations shift to a delivery obligation—getting the funds to the title company within the contractual timeline.

## Disbursement Rules: When and How Earnest Money Gets Released

**Disbursement of earnest money is where most legal disputes originate, and Idaho law is unambiguous about the requirements.** Under Idaho Code 54-2051(3), a broker cannot release earnest money from trust without one of three things: mutual written agreement of the parties, a final court order, or the satisfaction of contractual conditions that explicitly authorize release.

The Idaho REALTORS form RE-36 (Cancellation/Release of Contract and Release of Earnest Money) is the standard release form. Both buyer and seller must sign the RE-36 before funds leave trust. Agents who release funds based on a verbal agreement or a single party's instruction are violating the statute and exposing their broker to liability.

When parties dispute who receives the earnest money, the designated broker has two options under Idaho law. The broker may continue to hold the funds in trust indefinitely until receiving mutual written instructions, or the broker may interplead the funds into the district court and let a judge decide. Interpleader is governed by Idaho Rule of Civil Procedure 22 and allows the broker to step out of the dispute entirely—though the brokerage typically bears the filing costs.

Agents sometimes confuse contingency deadlines with automatic release triggers. The RE-21 does contain provisions where failure to respond within specified timeframes may result in contract termination, but that termination does not automatically authorize disbursement. The RE-36 still needs signatures, or the broker needs a clear contractual provision that explicitly states funds release upon a specific triggering event without further action required.

## Common Mistakes Idaho Agents Make With Earnest Money

Five recurring errors show up in IREC disciplinary proceedings and civil litigation year after year. Recognizing these patterns can prevent career-ending violations.

**First, holding checks without depositing them.** Some agents wait to deposit earnest money until inspections clear or the buyer "feels sure." This violates the three-business-day rule regardless of the agent's intentions. IREC does not care that you were "protecting the buyer." The deposit timeline is absolute.

Second, failing to document receipt of earnest money with a written receipt that includes the date, amount, form of payment, and name of the payor. Rule 600 requires contemporaneous documentation, and "I'll write it up later" often becomes "I forgot entirely." When an IREC auditor asks for the receipt log six months later, gaps become evidence of non-compliance.

Third, depositing earnest money into the wrong account. This typically happens in offices with multiple trust accounts or when an agent new to a brokerage uses an old routing number. Even an innocent mistake constitutes a trust account violation if funds end up in an operating account, even temporarily. The commingling prohibition under Idaho Code 54-2051 has no intent requirement—strict liability applies.

Fourth, releasing earnest money to a buyer who verbally cancels without obtaining a signed RE-36. The buyer calls and says "I'm out," the agent cuts a check, and three days later the seller's attorney sends a demand letter claiming breach. The agent is now personally exposed for unauthorized disbursement.

Fifth, failing to notify the broker of a trust fund dispute. Under IDAPA 24.33.01, the designated broker must be informed immediately when parties disagree about earnest money disposition. Agents who try to "handle it" independently create liability for themselves and their brokerage. The broker cannot fulfill statutory obligations without knowledge of the dispute.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Idaho carry personal liability for every dollar in their trust accounts. IREC's enforcement history demonstrates that "I didn't know my agent did that" is not a defense. Rule 600 places affirmative supervisory duties on brokers that go beyond simply having a policy manual on the shelf.

| Broker Obligation | Frequency | Consequence of Failure |
|-------------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | License suspension, fines up to $5,000 |
| Agent trust fund receipt verification | Per transaction | Vicarious liability for agent violations |
| Five-year record retention | Ongoing | Inability to defend against complaints |
| Dispute notification response | Immediate upon notice | Unauthorized disbursement liability |
| IREC audit cooperation | Upon demand | License revocation proceedings |

Brokers should implement a system that flags any transaction where earnest money has not been deposited within 48 hours of acceptance—giving a one-day buffer before the three-day deadline hits. Waiting until the deadline to discover a problem leaves no room for correction. Monthly reconciliation should cross-reference the transaction management system against bank statements, not merely confirm that the bank balance matches a running total.

**Audit your agents' RE-36 forms with the same scrutiny you apply to listing agreements.** An RE-36 with a missing signature, an incorrect amount, or a date that precedes the actual cancellation event is a ticking liability. If your office handles more than a handful of transactions monthly, manual tracking of these deadlines becomes unreliable—and that unreliability is precisely [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in the first place.

Brokers should also verify that every purchase agreement clearly identifies the earnest money holder and that the identified holder matches where the funds are actually deposited. Discrepancies between the RE-21 designation and actual deposit location create chain-of-custody problems that become expensive during litigation.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

IREC's disciplinary authority under Idaho Code 54-2059 includes license revocation, license suspension, civil fines up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory education requirements, and probationary periods with heightened reporting obligations. Trust account violations are treated as Category 1 offenses in IREC's enforcement framework, meaning they receive priority investigation and carry the most severe presumptive penalties.

Beyond IREC discipline, agents and brokers face civil liability under common law conversion claims if earnest money is wrongfully disbursed. A buyer whose $20,000 deposit gets released to a seller without proper authorization can sue the agent, the broker, and potentially the brokerage's E&O carrier. Idaho courts have consistently held that trust account violations constitute negligence per se—meaning the plaintiff does not need to prove the agent's standard of care, only that the statute was violated and damages resulted.

Criminal liability is also possible in extreme cases. Idaho Code 18-2403 (theft by unauthorized control) could apply when an agent or broker intentionally misappropriates trust funds. While criminal prosecution is rare for negligent handling, intentional diversions of earnest money to personal use constitute felony theft in Idaho.
