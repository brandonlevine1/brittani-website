## Understanding Hawaii Continuing Education Requirements 2026

The Hawaii Real Estate Commission (HIREC), operating under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), sets the rules every licensee must follow. Hawaii continuing education requirements 2026 remain structured around a biennial renewal cycle, but recent rule clarifications and enforcement actions make this a renewal period that demands attention. Agents who treat CE as an afterthought are finding themselves locked out of transactions at the worst possible time.

Hawaii operates on a two-year license cycle. The current biennium for most agents runs from November 30, 2024, through November 30, 2026, meaning your CE hours must be completed before that expiration date. HIREC does not grant extensions, and there is no grace period that allows you to continue practicing while catching up.

The governing statute is Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 467, specifically sections 467-7.5 and 467-11, which outline education requirements and grounds for license forfeiture. Hawaii Administrative Rules Title 16, Chapter 99 provides the granular detail on approved course content and provider standards. Agents who have not reviewed these since initial licensing often misunderstand what counts.

## Credit Hour Breakdown and Core Course Mandate

**Every Hawaii licensee must complete 20 hours of approved continuing education per biennium — no exceptions for experience level or production volume.** The 20 hours split into two distinct categories: a mandatory 10-hour core course and 10 hours of elective courses from HIREC-approved providers.

The core course changes every biennium. HIREC designs it to address recent legislative amendments, disciplinary case studies, and evolving market issues specific to Hawaii's real estate environment. For the 2024-2026 cycle, the core covers updated agency disclosure obligations, condominium document requirements under HRS Chapter 514B, and fair housing enforcement trends in the islands.

Elective hours offer more flexibility, but not unlimited choice. HIREC maintains a list of approved courses and providers. Taking a course from a non-approved provider — even one recognized in another state — results in zero credit toward your Hawaii renewal. The Real Estate Branch website publishes the current approved provider list under the Professional & Vocational Licensing Division.

## What Happens When You Fail to Comply

The consequences in Hawaii are more severe than many agents realize. Under HRS 467-7.5, failure to complete CE by the renewal deadline results in automatic forfeiture of your license — not suspension, forfeiture. The distinction matters because a forfeited license requires a formal restoration process rather than a simple reactivation.

**During forfeiture, you cannot engage in any real estate activity whatsoever.** That means no showing properties, no writing offers, no attending closings, no collecting commissions on pending deals. If you close a transaction while your license is forfeited, you face potential civil liability, commission disgorgement, and HIREC disciplinary action including fines of up to $2,500 per violation under HRS 467-20.

Brokers face separate exposure. HRS 467-1.6 places affirmative responsibility on the principal broker to ensure every agent operating under their license holds a current, active license. A broker who allows a forfeited agent to practice faces independent disciplinary proceedings. The commission has publicly sanctioned brokers in recent biennia for this exact failure, issuing both monetary penalties and license restrictions.

The restoration path depends on how long your license has been forfeited. Under two years, you must complete all outstanding CE, submit the [Restoration Application](https://cca.hawaii.gov/reb/) through the DCCA eBiz system, and pay the restoration fee. Beyond two years, HIREC may require you to retake the state licensing examination — effectively starting over.

## Common Mistakes Hawaii Agents Make

Tracking continuing education errors across multiple renewal cycles reveals predictable patterns. These are the five mistakes HIREC staff and real estate attorneys encounter most frequently.

**First, agents confuse completion date with certificate submission date.** Finishing a course on November 29 does not help if the provider takes three weeks to report completion to HIREC. The commission tracks credits through provider-submitted records, not self-reporting. Agents should complete all hours no later than October 2026 to allow adequate reporting time.

Second, agents take out-of-state CE courses assuming they transfer. Hawaii does not participate in ARELLO's reciprocal CE recognition for renewal purposes. A 10-hour course approved in California counts for nothing in Hawaii unless that specific course also appears on HIREC's approved list. This catches agents who hold licenses in multiple states, a situation [discussed in detail in our licensing portability analysis](/blog/wisconsin-license-reciprocity-portability-2026).

Third, agents complete 20 hours of electives and skip the core course entirely. The 10-hour core is non-negotiable. Twenty hours of electives gives you only 10 hours of credit toward your requirement. HIREC will not accept substitutions regardless of topic overlap.

Fourth, agents renew online and assume payment equals compliance. The DCCA eBiz system allows you to pay your renewal fee before CE verification is complete. Paying does not renew your license if your education record shows incomplete hours. Agents then assume they are active when they are not.

Fifth, agents operating on teams assume their team leader or broker handled CE enrollment on their behalf. Each licensee bears individual responsibility. No broker, team lead, or transaction coordinator can complete your continuing education obligation for you — though [managing the tracking and reminders is exactly where workflow tools add value](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Hawaii carry a statutory supervision duty that extends explicitly to continuing education verification. This is not optional diligence — it is a regulatory requirement embedded in HRS 467-1.6 and reinforced by HIREC's published compliance guidelines.

**At minimum, brokers should verify every agent's CE status no later than 60 days before the renewal deadline.** The DCCA's online license verification tool (accessible at the Professional & Vocational Licensing site) shows current license status and expiration dates. Brokers who rely solely on agents self-reporting their completion status are exposing their firm to regulatory action.

|---|---|---|
| Verify all agent license expiration dates | 90 days before deadline | DCCA eBiz license lookup |
| Confirm core course enrollment/completion | 60 days before deadline | Provider completion certificates |
| Confirm elective hour completion | 60 days before deadline | Provider completion certificates |
| Document verification in agent file | Before renewal submission | HIREC compliance guidelines |
| Remove forfeited agents from active roster | Immediately upon forfeiture | HRS 467-1.6 |

Brokers who discover an agent has not started CE with fewer than 30 days remaining face a difficult decision. Allowing the agent to continue practicing through the deadline while hoping they complete courses creates liability. The safer approach is to restrict the agent's activity and document the restriction in writing.

HIREC has increased enforcement activity in recent renewal cycles. The commission's published disciplinary actions from 2024-2025 show multiple brokerages fined specifically for inadequate supervision of agent licensing status. The pattern suggests this is an enforcement priority, not a theoretical risk.

> "The principal broker is responsible for the real estate activities of all licensees associated with or employed by the principal broker." — HRS 467-1.6(a)

## Documenting Compliance: Forms and Records

Hawaii does not require agents to submit CE certificates directly to HIREC — approved providers report completions electronically. However, agents should retain their own completion certificates for at least four years (two full biennia) in case of audit or dispute.

The renewal itself happens through DCCA Form BB-35 (Biennial Renewal Application), submitted online through the eBiz system. For agents who have let their license lapse, Form BB-36 (Restoration Application) applies. Brokers updating their firm's agent roster use Form REB-12 to add or remove licensees.

Keeping organized records of compliance documentation prevents small oversights from becoming transaction-killing problems. If a buyer's attorney requests proof of your active license status mid-transaction — which happens more frequently in Hawaii's attorney-involved closings — having immediate access to your completion certificates and renewal confirmation eliminates delays. This kind of [proactive file management separates smooth closings from last-minute chaos](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

## Planning Your 2026 Renewal Timeline

The most effective approach treats November 30, 2026, as a hard wall and works backward. Agents who complete their hours by September 2026 give themselves (and their providers) a full 60-day buffer for reporting and system updates. Those who wait until November are gambling.

HIREC-approved providers offer the core course in multiple formats: in-person classroom sessions on Oahu, Maui, Kauai, and the Big Island; live-streamed distance education; and some asynchronous online options. Availability tightens significantly in September through November as procrastinators flood enrollment. Agents on neighbor islands face additional scheduling constraints for in-person options.
