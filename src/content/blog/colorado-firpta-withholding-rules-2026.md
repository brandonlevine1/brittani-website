## Understanding Colorado FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The **Colorado firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026** carry real financial teeth for every party at the closing table. If you represent a foreign national selling Colorado real estate this year, you face a dual withholding regime: the federal Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (FIRPTA) and Colorado's own state-level income tax withholding statute. Getting either one wrong exposes your client, your buyer, and potentially your brokerage to penalties that dwarf any commission check.

FIRPTA requires the buyer—not the seller—to withhold 15% of the gross sale price and remit it to the IRS when the seller is a "foreign person" as defined under IRC Section 1445. Colorado layers on its own withholding under Colorado Revised Statutes Section 39-22-604.5, which mandates that the closing entity collect either 2% of gross proceeds or the seller's estimated Colorado income tax, whichever is greater. Both obligations trigger simultaneously, and both must be satisfied before proceeds are disbursed.

## How FIRPTA and Colorado State Withholding Work Together

Most agents think of FIRPTA as a single withholding event. In Colorado, it is two distinct events with two different remittance paths. The federal piece goes to the IRS via Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding), both due within 20 days of closing. The Colorado piece goes to the Colorado Department of Revenue via Form DR 1079 (Payment of Withholding Tax on Certain Colorado Real Property Interest Transfers).

Failing to file the DR 1079 triggers a separate penalty structure from the state, independent of any IRS action. The Colorado Department of Revenue can assess penalties of up to 10% of the unpaid withholding amount plus statutory interest. These are not theoretical risks—they are enforced through automated matching of deed recordings against withholding filings.

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | Colorado State |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding Rate | 15% of gross sale price | 2% of gross or estimated tax (whichever is greater) |
| Form Filed | IRS Form 8288 + 8288-A | DR 1079 |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after closing | 30 days after closing |
| Filed By | Buyer/closing agent (transferee) | Closing entity (title company) |
| Penalty for Non-Compliance | 100% of tax due + interest | 10% of unpaid amount + interest |

## Who Qualifies as a "Foreign Person" and Why It Matters Early

**Under IRC Section 1445, a "foreign person" includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates.** A permanent resident holding a green card is not a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. However, a seller who previously held a green card but allowed it to lapse may revert to foreign person status—something that catches agents off guard during listing intake.

The determination must happen early in the transaction, ideally at listing or during the initial offer phase. Colorado's Commission-approved contracts do not include a built-in FIRPTA disclosure checkbox, which means agents must raise the question affirmatively. Waiting until the title commitment arrives to ask about citizenship status is one of the fastest ways to blow a closing timeline.

If you have handled transactions with complex [wire instructions and verification processes](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process), you already know how quickly compliance issues can derail a deal when discovered late. FIRPTA is no different—except the dollar amounts at stake are often six figures.

## The Specific Forms Colorado Agents Must Know

The Colorado Real Estate Commission does not mandate a particular FIRPTA form for agents, but the practical workflow involves several documents that agents must either prepare, review, or confirm have been submitted.

**IRS Form 8288** is the withholding return itself. IRS Form 8288-A accompanies it and serves as the seller's receipt—the foreign seller needs this document to file their own U.S. tax return and claim any refund of overwithholding. IRS Form 8288-B is the application for a withholding certificate, used when the seller seeks a reduced withholding (because the actual gain is less than 15% of gross). This form must be filed before or at closing, and the IRS typically takes 90 days to process it.

On the state side, [Colorado DR 1079](https://tax.colorado.gov/withholding-on-real-property) is the mandatory form. Title companies in Colorado routinely handle this form, but the legal obligation falls on the "transferee" or the "transferee's agent" who controls the closing proceeds. If your title company misses it, liability can cascade upstream.

| Form | Purpose | Who Files | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| IRS 8288 | Federal withholding return | Buyer or closing agent | 20 days post-closing |
| IRS 8288-A | Seller's withholding statement | Filed with 8288 | 20 days post-closing |
| IRS 8288-B | Application for reduced withholding | Seller (often via CPA) | Before or at closing |
| CO DR 1079 | Colorado state withholding payment | Closing entity | 30 days post-closing |
| FIRPTA Affidavit (non-foreign) | Certifies seller is NOT foreign | Seller | At closing |

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The consequences split into federal and state tracks, and neither is forgiving. Under federal law, if the buyer fails to withhold the required 15%, the IRS can assess the full amount of the unsatisfied withholding against the buyer personally—plus penalties and interest running from the original closing date. The buyer can then pursue the seller for reimbursement, but collection from a foreign person who has left the country is often impractical.

For agents specifically, the Colorado Real Estate Commission can initiate disciplinary proceedings under Rule E-35 if an agent's negligence contributed to a compliance failure. This is not a fine in the traditional sense—it is a license-level action that could result in a letter of admonishment, stipulated agreement, or formal hearing before the Commission. Brokers are jointly exposed under their supervisory duties outlined in C.R.S. 12-10-217.

Deal cancellation is another real outcome. When a buyer's attorney discovers mid-transaction that FIRPTA withholding will consume a massive portion of proceeds—and the seller refuses to accept a reduced net—contracts fall apart. Agents who fail to disclose this obligation early may face claims of misrepresentation or breach of fiduciary duty from their own clients, a pattern you can also see in [deals that break down over procedural failures](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## Common Mistakes Colorado Agents Make with FIRPTA

**First, agents assume the title company handles everything.** While title companies in Colorado routinely manage DR 1079 filings, they are not always aware of the seller's foreign status. If the listing agent never flags it, the title company may prepare a standard FIRPTA affidavit for the seller to sign—one that falsely certifies non-foreign status—creating a fraud problem rather than a compliance solution.

Second, agents confuse residency with citizenship. A seller who has lived in Colorado for ten years, owns property, and pays state taxes may still be a nonresident alien for FIRPTA purposes if they never obtained permanent resident status or U.S. citizenship. Physical presence does not equal legal status under IRC Section 1445.

Third, agents fail to account for the withholding amount in the net sheet. A seller expecting $800,000 in proceeds on a $1,200,000 sale will actually receive $180,000 less at closing from federal withholding alone, plus another $24,000 for the state withholding. Agents who present listing presentations or buyer offers without these calculations create trust-destroying surprises.

Fourth, agents do not advise sellers early enough to apply for a withholding certificate via Form 8288-B. Because the IRS processing time averages 90 days, a seller who lists in March and closes in May has zero chance of receiving a reduced withholding certificate in time. This timeline issue must be raised at listing, not after the inspection period.

Fifth, agents rely on verbal confirmations of citizenship status rather than obtaining the written FIRPTA affidavit (also called a Non-Foreign Affidavit under IRC Section 1445(b)(2)) at or before closing. Verbal representations carry no legal weight if the IRS later reclassifies the transaction.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers in Colorado carry independent supervisory liability under C.R.S. 12-10-217. This means that a broker cannot simply delegate FIRPTA compliance to agents and assume the problem is solved. Brokers should build three audit checkpoints into their transaction management systems.

**First, every new listing file should include a documented answer to the question: "Is the seller a U.S. citizen or permanent resident?"** This should be a required field in the listing intake process—not an optional note. If the answer is "no" or "unknown," the file should be flagged for FIRPTA review before any offer is accepted.

Second, brokers should verify that the closing disclosure or settlement statement reflects both the federal 15% and the Colorado 2% withholding amounts. If those line items are absent on a flagged file, someone missed a step. Reviewing final CDs against a [checklist-driven system](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) eliminates this gap.

Third, brokers should maintain a log of all transactions involving foreign sellers, including confirmation that Forms 8288 and DR 1079 were filed within their respective deadlines. The broker does not need to file the forms personally, but they need evidence that someone did—and that evidence should live in the transaction file permanently.

The Colorado Division of Real Estate has increased its focus on compliance audits, particularly for brokerages operating in resort markets like Aspen, Vail, Telluride, and Steamboat Springs, where foreign ownership is concentrated. Brokers operating in these markets face higher audit probability and should treat FIRPTA tracking as a standard operating procedure, not an exception workflow.

## Reducing the Withholding: Options Available to Foreign Sellers

Not every foreign seller owes 15% of the gross price in tax. FIRPTA withholding is a prepayment mechanism, not the final tax calculation. Sellers whose actual capital gain is lower than 15% of the sale price can apply for a Withholding Certificate using Form 8288-B, which asks the IRS to authorize a reduced withholding amount based on the seller's maximum tax liability.

The practical challenge is timing. The IRS commits to processing 8288-B applications within 90 days, but actual turnaround often stretches longer. Sellers who want reduced withholding must plan well ahead of closing—or negotiate an escrow holdback arrangement where the full 15% is held by the title company pending IRS determination, rather than remitted immediately.

Another option exists for sales of personal residences priced between $300,001 and $1,000,000: the withholding rate drops to 10% if the buyer intends to use the property as a residence. Below $300,000 with a buyer-residence affidavit, it drops to zero. These thresholds have not changed for 2026, but agents should confirm annually since legislative proposals frequently target these amounts.
