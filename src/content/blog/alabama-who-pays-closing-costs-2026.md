## Alabama Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Division That Trips Up Agents

The question of Alabama who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not as straightforward as many agents assume. Alabama lacks a single statute that assigns every fee to one party, which means the purchase contract controls nearly everything. Agents who treat cost allocation as "standard" without documenting it properly expose themselves—and their brokers—to liability under Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) rules.

Alabama's closing cost landscape is shaped by local custom, lender requirements, and whatever the parties negotiate in the Alabama Association of Realtors (AAR) Residential Sale Contract, Form 15. The total closing costs in a typical Alabama residential transaction range from 2% to 5% of the purchase price for buyers and 6% to 10% for sellers (including commissions). Getting the split wrong does more than annoy your client—it can blow up financing, trigger AREC complaints, and unravel deals days before settlement.

## The Default Split: What Custom Dictates Across Alabama Counties

**Alabama has no statute that mandates a specific buyer-seller closing cost split.** Instead, the allocation follows county custom unless the parties agree otherwise in writing. That distinction matters because custom varies between Mobile County, Jefferson County, and Madison County.

In most of the state, the seller customarily pays the owner's title insurance premium, the deed preparation fee, the real estate transfer tax (currently $0.50 per $500 of consideration under Ala. Code Section 40-22-1), and the cost of satisfying existing liens. Buyers customarily pay the lender's title insurance premium, appraisal fee, credit report, survey, recording fees for the new deed and mortgage, and prepaid escrow items like insurance and property taxes.

These customs are not law. They are starting points that shift based on market conditions, seller concessions, and buyer negotiation strength.

| Cost Item | Customary Payer | Negotiable? |
|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | Yes |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Yes |
| Transfer tax ($0.50/$500) | Seller | Yes |
| Deed preparation | Seller | Yes |
| Mortgage recording fees | Buyer | Yes |
| Survey | Buyer | Yes |
| Attorney/closing fee | Split or Buyer | Yes |
| Seller concessions (credits) | Seller | Yes, within lender caps |

## What the Contract Must Say: AAR Form 15 and Addenda

The AAR Residential Sale Contract (Form 15, revised 2025) contains specific sections where agents must identify cost responsibilities. Paragraph 6 addresses closing costs and seller contributions. If you leave blanks or rely on boilerplate language that says "per custom," you are inviting a dispute that neither party can cleanly resolve without an addendum or, worse, mediation.

**When documenting seller-paid concessions, the dollar amount or percentage must be stated explicitly in Paragraph 6 or in AAR Addendum Form 25 (Financing Contingency Addendum).** Vague language like "seller to assist with buyer's costs" without a cap has caused deals to crater at the closing table when lenders flag the HUD-1 replacement—the TRID Closing Disclosure—as inconsistent with contract terms.

Agents should also confirm that any agreed concession does not exceed the buyer's lender maximum. FHA loans cap seller concessions at 6% of the sales price, VA loans at 4% (plus certain allowable costs), and conventional loans range from 2% to 9% depending on down payment and occupancy type. Misstating these caps [creates the kind of deadline pressure](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that tanks transactions.

## Alabama Transfer Tax and Recording Fees: Who Actually Writes the Check

Alabama's real estate transfer tax is governed by Ala. Code Section 40-22-1 and is imposed at $0.50 per $500 of consideration (or fraction thereof). The statute does not specify who pays it—only that it must be paid before the deed is recorded. By overwhelming custom, the seller pays this tax. But if the contract is silent, the closing attorney will typically follow county convention and charge it to the seller.

Recording fees for the deed and mortgage are set by each county's probate office. As of 2026, most Alabama counties charge between $36 and $75 for the first page and $3 to $5 per additional page. The buyer customarily pays the recording fee for the new mortgage, while the seller pays to record any satisfaction or release of their existing mortgage.

One fee that catches agents off guard: the mortgage tax under Ala. Code Section 40-22-2, imposed at $0.15 per $100 of the indebtedness secured. This is a buyer cost paid at recording and can amount to $450 on a $300,000 mortgage. Failing to account for it on the buyer's estimated closing statement creates last-minute cash-to-close shortfalls.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

**Mistake 1: Failing to specify concession caps in the contract.** Writing "seller to pay up to buyer's closing costs" without a dollar figure or percentage violates the specificity standards that title companies and lenders require on the Closing Disclosure. The closing attorney will call you, and if the parties disagree on the intended amount, you have a dispute that could have been avoided with ten seconds of typing.

Mistake 2: Assuming the transfer tax is always the seller's obligation without confirming it in the contract. In rare investor-to-investor transactions or bank-owned sales, the contract may shift this cost to the buyer. If you assume custom applies without reading the actual agreement, your client's net sheet will be wrong.

Mistake 3: Confusing the owner's title policy with the lender's title policy when advising clients. These are separate products with separate premiums. Telling a seller they "don't have to pay for title insurance" because the buyer is getting a loan creates a situation where no owner's policy is issued—leaving the buyer unprotected and potentially violating your fiduciary duty under [Ala. Code Section 34-27-36(a)(7)](https://arec.alabama.gov/laws-and-rules/).

Mistake 4: Ignoring the impact of the TRID three-day rule on concession changes. If a seller concession amount changes after the initial Closing Disclosure is issued, a new three-day waiting period may be triggered. Agents who negotiate last-minute cost adjustments without understanding TRID timing cause delays that [break deals apart at the worst possible moment](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Mistake 5: Presenting net sheets based on "typical" costs without running actuals through the closing attorney's office. Every county probate office and every title company has slightly different fee schedules. A generic net sheet template from 2023 will not reflect 2026 recording fees or updated title insurance rate filings approved by the Alabama Department of Insurance.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers have an affirmative duty under AREC Rule 790-X-3-.03 to review contracts and ensure their agents are not making material misrepresentations about transaction costs. This is not optional oversight—it is a regulatory expectation that AREC investigators will examine during complaint proceedings.

**Brokers should audit every purchase agreement for three specific items:** (1) whether Paragraph 6 of Form 15 contains a specific dollar amount or percentage for any seller concession, (2) whether the concession amount is consistent with the loan program's maximum, and (3) whether the agent provided a written estimated net sheet to the seller within a reasonable time after contract execution.

A quarterly file review focusing on closing cost documentation will reveal patterns before they become AREC complaints. Brokers should also verify that agents are using current-year AAR forms. Outdated forms lack updated provisions and can create ambiguity that exposes the brokerage to errors-and-omissions claims. The cost of [building a reliable review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) is trivial compared to the cost of defending a license complaint.

| Seller net sheet | Delivered within 48 hours of ratification | AREC complaint for misrepresentation |
| Current-year form version | 2025 or later revision date | Unenforceable clauses, E&O exposure |

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

AREC has the authority under Ala. Code Section 34-27-36 to discipline licensees who make substantial misrepresentations or fail to account for funds in a real estate transaction. Penalties range from a formal reprimand to license revocation. Monetary fines can reach $2,500 per violation, and AREC can require restitution to harmed parties.

Beyond AREC action, an agent who misstates closing cost responsibility on a net sheet may face a civil claim for negligent misrepresentation. Alabama courts have recognized that buyers and sellers rely on their agent's cost estimates when deciding whether to proceed with a transaction. If a seller nets $8,000 less than the agent's estimate because the agent forgot to include the transfer tax or assigned a cost to the wrong party, that agent—and their broker—may be liable for the difference.

Deal cancellation is also a real outcome. When a buyer arrives at closing and discovers their cash-to-close is $3,000 higher than expected because an agent failed to account for the mortgage recording tax, the buyer may exercise a right to terminate if financing contingency deadlines have not yet expired. The listing agent then faces an angry seller, a relisted property, and potential days-on-market damage.
