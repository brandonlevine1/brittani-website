## Understanding Iowa Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on transfer taxes or recording fees in Iowa does not just embarrass you at the closing table — it can hold up deed recording, delay disbursement, and expose your brokerage to liability claims. The Iowa transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by Iowa Code Chapter 428A and Chapter 331, and every licensed agent operating in the state needs to know the current rates cold. This post breaks down the exact fee structure, the forms you must file, the mistakes that trip up even experienced agents, and what brokers should be checking before any file goes to closing.

Iowa's transfer tax — formally called the "real estate transfer tax" or the "declaration of value fee" — applies to nearly every conveyance of real property in the state. The rate has remained static at $0.80 per $500 of consideration for years, and 2026 is no exception. What does change from county to county is the base recording fee, supplemental page fees, and the way individual recorders handle electronic versus paper filings.

## The Current Rate Structure and How It Is Calculated

**Iowa's transfer tax rate is $0.80 for each $500 (or fraction thereof) of the total consideration paid.** That works out to $1.60 per $1,000 of sale price. On a $350,000 sale, the tax is $560 — calculated as 350,000 divided by 500, multiplied by $0.80.

The tax is collected by the county recorder at the time the deed is presented for recording. It must be paid before the recorder will accept the document. The revenue is split between the state and the county under Iowa Code Section 428A.8, with the state receiving a portion funneled through the Iowa Department of Revenue.

Recording fees for the deed itself are separate from the transfer tax. Under Iowa Code Section 331.604, the base recording fee for a standard document is $5.00 for the first page and $3.00 for each subsequent page in most counties. Some counties have adopted slightly different fee schedules for electronic submissions through the Iowa Land Records system. Always confirm the exact fee with the county recorder's office before closing day.

| Fee Type | Rate / Amount | Authority |
|----------|---------------|-----------|
| Real estate transfer tax | $0.80 per $500 of consideration | Iowa Code 428A.1 |
| Base recording fee (first page) | $5.00 | Iowa Code 331.604 |
| Additional pages | $3.00 per page | Iowa Code 331.604 |
| Declaration of Value filing | No separate fee (included with transfer tax) | Iowa Code 428A.2 |
| Exempt transaction DOV | No transfer tax due, but form still required | Iowa Code 428A.2(2) |

## The Declaration of Value Form: Iowa's Required Filing

Every conveyance that triggers recording of a deed in Iowa must be accompanied by a [Declaration of Value](https://tax.iowa.gov/real-estate-transfer-tax) (DOV) form — sometimes referenced informally as the "Iowa DOV" or "real estate transfer — declaration of value." The Iowa Department of Revenue administers this form, and it is submitted to the county recorder alongside the deed.

The DOV captures the sale price, parties involved, property description, and the type of transfer. If the transaction qualifies for an exemption under Iowa Code 428A.2(2) — such as transfers between spouses, transfers to governmental bodies, or transfers where the consideration is under $500 — the form still must be filed, but no tax payment is required. You simply check the corresponding exemption code on the form.

**Agents frequently confuse "exempt from tax" with "exempt from filing."** They are not the same thing. A deed presented without a DOV — even for an exempt transaction — will be rejected by the recorder. This causes recording delays, which can cascade into problems with title insurance policy issuance and lender disbursement timelines.

## Who Pays What: Allocation Between Buyer and Seller

Iowa law does not dictate which party must pay the transfer tax. Iowa Code 428A.1 imposes the tax on the "transfer" itself, not on a specific party. In practice, Iowa custom places the transfer tax on the seller. However, this is entirely negotiable within the purchase agreement.

The recording fee for the deed is typically paid by the buyer, since the buyer benefits from having the deed recorded in public records. Mortgage recording fees, which apply when a lender records its mortgage instrument, fall on the buyer as well. These mortgage recording fees follow the same per-page rate under Section 331.604.

| Cost Item | Typically Paid By | Negotiable? |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|
| Transfer tax (DOV) | Seller | Yes |
| Deed recording fee | Buyer | Yes |
| Mortgage recording fee | Buyer | Rarely negotiated |
| Title search / abstract | Varies by county custom | Yes |
| Additional DOV copies | Party requesting | N/A |

Agents should always specify the allocation in the purchase agreement rather than relying on "local custom." Custom varies between Iowa's 99 counties, and an ambiguous contract term invites disputes at the settlement statement stage. If you are working with [deadline-sensitive transactions](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), confirming fee responsibility early prevents last-minute surprises.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Iowa Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

Mistakes in this area rarely blow up a deal entirely, but they do cause closing delays, title issues, and occasionally Iowa Real Estate Commission complaints. Here are five recurring errors.

**First, agents miscalculate the transfer tax by dividing by $1,000 instead of $500.** The statute says $0.80 per $500 or fraction thereof. Dividing a $275,000 sale price by 1,000 and multiplying by $0.80 gives you $220 — half the correct amount. The actual tax is $440. This error shows up on preliminary settlement statements and blindsides sellers at closing.

Second, agents omit the DOV form entirely on exempt transactions, assuming no tax means no form. The recorder rejects the deed. The title company or closing attorney then scrambles to get the form completed and re-present, which can push recording to the next business day and delay the buyer's possession date.

Third, agents fail to account for "fraction thereof" rounding. A sale price of $275,100 does not yield the same tax as $275,000. That extra $100 pushes you into the next $500 bracket, adding another $0.80 to the total. Every dollar above a clean $500 increment costs the seller an additional $0.80.

Fourth, agents working electronic closings assume the DOV is handled automatically by the title company's software. In some counties, the DOV must still be submitted as a separate filing. If the closing agent does not handle it, and the listing agent assumed they would, the deed sits unrecorded.

Fifth, agents in border-county transactions confuse Iowa requirements with those of neighboring states. [Wisconsin's transfer fee structure](/blog/wisconsin-transfer-tax-recording-fees-2026) differs significantly, and agents working the Mississippi River corridor between Dubuque and Prairie du Chien need to keep the two systems distinct in their workflows.

## What Happens When Compliance Fails

A deed that cannot be recorded due to a missing or incorrect DOV remains outside the public record. This means the buyer does not have constructive notice of ownership, the title insurance policy may not issue, and the lender may refuse to fund or may demand a holdback. In cash transactions, the buyer's ownership interest is technically vulnerable to subsequent claims until recording occurs.

The Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) can pursue disciplinary action against a licensee who repeatedly fails to ensure proper documentation accompanies a closing. Under Iowa Administrative Code 193E, agents owe a duty of competence. Chronic errors in calculating transfer taxes or ensuring DOV filings may constitute a violation of that standard.

Financial liability is also real. If a seller discovers post-closing that they were overcharged on transfer tax due to an agent's miscalculation — and the closing agent relied on the agent's figures — the brokerage may face a demand for reimbursement. While the amounts are small on individual transactions, a pattern across dozens of closings adds up.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under Iowa Code Chapter 543B and IREC rules. A broker who never reviews how transfer taxes and recording fees appear on their agents' files is accepting risk without visibility.

**Start by auditing a random sample of closed files each quarter specifically for DOV accuracy.** Confirm that the transfer tax amount on the settlement statement matches the mathematical formula: sale price divided by 500, rounded up to the next whole number, multiplied by $0.80. Flag any file where the DOV exemption code was checked but no explanation exists in the file notes.

Brokers should also verify that their agents are not quoting recording fees from outdated county schedules. When a county updates its fee structure — particularly for electronic recording through [Iowa Land Records](https://www.iowalandrecords.org) — agents using stale numbers will produce inaccurate net sheets and estimated settlement statements. Require agents to confirm fees directly with the recorder's office or through the Iowa Land Records portal for each transaction.

Finally, build a [checklist-driven review](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) into your file compliance process that includes a line item for DOV completion, transfer tax calculation verification, and confirmation that recording fees match the county's current published schedule. This takes five minutes per file and eliminates the most common audit findings.

## Electronic Recording and County-Specific Variations

Iowa's 99 counties do not operate identically. While the state statute sets the base fee structure, individual county recorders have discretion on certain administrative charges, particularly around electronic submissions. The Iowa Land Records system — operated by the Iowa County Recorders Association — allows electronic filing in most counties, but not all documents qualify, and surcharges for electronic recording vary.

Some counties charge a technology fee of $1.00 to $5.00 per electronically recorded document. Others waive fees for electronic submissions to encourage adoption. Agents must verify with each county before quoting fees to clients. A per-transaction approach works best: check the county recorder's posted fee schedule for the specific county where the property sits, not the county where your office is located.

Polk County, for example, has a well-documented electronic recording program with specific formatting requirements for documents submitted electronically. Linn County and Johnson County handle things slightly differently. Never assume uniformity across county lines.
