## Understanding Iowa Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

If you hold an active Iowa real estate license, your obligations around earnest money are not suggestions—they are enforceable rules backed by fines, license actions, and civil liability. Iowa earnest money & escrow laws 2026 remain governed primarily by Iowa Code Chapter 543B and Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 193E, both administered by the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC). Knowing the precise deposit timelines, trust account rules, and disbursement protocols separates compliant agents from those facing disciplinary hearings.

Iowa's framework differs from neighboring states in several meaningful ways. The state requires that brokers—not individual licensees—maintain trust accounts, and it mandates specific recordkeeping that IREC auditors check during routine and complaint-triggered inspections. Agents who treat earnest money handling as an afterthought are betting their license on luck rather than knowledge.

## Statutory Framework: Iowa Code Chapter 543B and 193E

**Iowa Code Chapter 543B establishes the licensing authority of IREC and grants it the power to discipline licensees who mishandle trust funds.** Section 543B.34 enumerates the grounds for disciplinary action, including "failure to account for or remit any moneys coming into the licensee's possession which belong to others." That language is intentionally broad—it covers earnest money, security deposits, and any client funds an agent touches.

Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13 provides the operational detail. Rule 193E-13.1 prohibits commingling personal funds with trust account funds. Rule 193E-13.3 sets the deposit deadline: earnest money must be placed in the broker's trust account within five banking days of acceptance of the offer, unless the parties agree in writing to a different timeline.

Rule 193E-13.2 requires every trust account to be held in an Iowa-based federally insured depository. The account title must clearly indicate it is a trust or escrow account. Brokers may not earn interest on these accounts for their own benefit unless they maintain a separate interest-bearing trust account that complies with the Iowa Trust Account Commission's guidelines.

> "All trust account records shall be retained for a minimum of five years and shall be open to inspection and audit by the commission or its authorized representatives." — Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.4

## Forms Iowa Agents Actually Use

The Iowa Association of Realtors (IAR) publishes the standardized forms most agents rely on. The primary residential purchase agreement is the **IAR Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 101)**. Within that form, the earnest money section specifies the amount, the holder (typically the listing broker), and the deposit deadline.

When disputes arise over disbursement, agents turn to the **IAR Earnest Money Release Agreement (Form 140)**. This form requires both buyer and seller signatures before the holder releases funds. Without it, the broker holding the money is stuck—releasing funds to one party without mutual written consent or a court order violates IREC rules.

For transactions involving a third-party escrow holder (such as a title company), the **IAR Escrow Agreement Addendum (Form 103)** modifies the default arrangement. Agents should confirm that any third-party holder is properly licensed or exempt under Iowa law before directing funds outside the brokerage's trust account.

| Form | Purpose | When Required |
|------|---------|---------------|
| IAR Form 101 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Every residential offer |
| IAR Form 140 | Earnest Money Release Agreement | Disputes or cancellations requiring mutual consent |
| IAR Form 103 | Escrow Agreement Addendum | When a third party holds the deposit |
| IAR Form 108 | Amendment to Purchase Agreement | Changing earnest money terms post-acceptance |

## Deposit Timelines and Disbursement Rules

**The five-banking-day deposit rule is the single most commonly violated timeline in Iowa residential transactions.** Banking days exclude Saturdays, Sundays, and federal holidays. If an offer is accepted on a Friday evening, the clock starts Monday and the deposit must hit the trust account by the following Friday—assuming no holidays intervene.

Disbursement is equally regulated. Under 193E-13.3(3), the broker holding earnest money may disburse funds only under four circumstances: mutual written consent of all parties, a final court order, terms of the purchase agreement that trigger automatic release (such as an unconditional satisfaction of all contingencies), or deposit into a court registry via an interpleader action. Agents who informally "return" earnest money to a buyer without the seller's signature on Form 140 are exposing their broker to serious liability.

If both parties claim the funds and refuse to sign a release, the broker should not sit on the money indefinitely. Iowa law permits the broker to file an interpleader action in district court, depositing the disputed funds with the court and requesting to be released from liability. Filing costs are minimal compared to the risk of an IREC complaint.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make

Mistakes in this area tend to cluster around the same patterns year after year. IREC disciplinary records reveal five recurring errors that cost agents money, reputation, and sometimes their license.

**First, missing the five-day deposit deadline.** Agents who hold a personal check in their desk drawer "waiting for the inspection period to pass" are violating 193E-13.3 regardless of their reasoning. The deposit obligation attaches at acceptance, not at contingency removal.

Second, verbal release of funds. An agent calls the broker and says the buyer verbally agreed to forfeit earnest money. The broker cuts a check to the seller. No Form 140 was signed. When the buyer later disputes the release, the broker has no documentation—and IREC has a clear violation.

Third, depositing earnest money into a personal account or an incorrect business account. Even well-intentioned agents sometimes deposit a check into their team's operating account rather than the brokerage's designated trust account. This constitutes commingling under 193E-13.1, full stop.

Fourth, failing to identify the escrow holder in the purchase agreement. Form 101 includes a blank for this purpose, but agents occasionally leave it empty or write in a party that hasn't agreed to hold the funds. Ambiguity here creates confusion at closing and can delay or kill a deal.

Fifth, not updating trust account records when an amendment changes the earnest money amount. If a buyer adds additional earnest money via Form 108, the trust account ledger must reflect the new total and deposit date. Incomplete records trigger audit findings even when no money is actually missing.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear ultimate responsibility for trust account compliance under Iowa law. IREC does not discipline individual salespersons for trust account violations in isolation—the broker's license is on the line first. A broker who delegates trust account management without oversight is not absolved of liability.

| Broker Responsibility | Frequency | Source |
|----------------------|-----------|--------|
| Reconcile trust account to ledger | Monthly | 193E-13.4 |
| Verify deposit within 5 banking days | Per transaction | 193E-13.3 |
| Retain all records | 5 years minimum | 193E-13.4 |
| Confirm proper account titling | Annually or upon bank change | 193E-13.2 |
| Review disbursement authorization | Per disbursement | 193E-13.3(3) |

**Monthly reconciliation is not optional.** IREC auditors compare the trust account bank statement against the broker's ledger and against individual transaction records. A three-way reconciliation that shows zero discrepancies is the standard. Any unexplained variance—even $10—can trigger a deeper investigation.

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy on earnest money procedures, distribute it to every licensee, and require signed acknowledgment. When an agent violates procedure, the broker's documented policy and training records become the defense against a negligence claim. Without documentation, a broker arguing "I told them the rules" carries no weight in a disciplinary hearing.

For offices managing high transaction volumes, the manual tracking of deposit deadlines and disbursement authorizations becomes a genuine operational risk. Missing a single deadline in a busy week can cascade into an IREC complaint. ## Penalties for Non-Compliance

IREC's enforcement powers under Iowa Code 543B.29 include license suspension, license revocation, civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation, and mandatory continuing education. The commission can also issue cease-and-desist orders and refer cases to the Iowa Attorney General for criminal prosecution when trust fund misappropriation constitutes theft.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue the agent and broker for conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, and breach of contract. Courts may award actual damages, punitive damages in egregious cases, and attorney fees. Iowa's [statute of limitations for these claims](https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/iowaCode/sections?codeChapter=543B) typically runs five years for contract actions and two years for torts, though the discovery rule can extend these windows.

Deal cancellation is another practical consequence. When an agent's non-compliance creates uncertainty about fund availability or proper handling, the other party's attorney may advise their client to terminate and pursue damages. A single compliance failure can destroy not just one transaction but the referring relationships connected to it.
