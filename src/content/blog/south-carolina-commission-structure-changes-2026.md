## South Carolina Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026

The South Carolina commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have fundamentally changed how agents get paid — and how they document that payment. Since August 2024, the practice changes mandated by the National Association of REALTORS settlement have filtered down through every MLS system and brokerage operating in the state. For South Carolina licensees, the stakes are high because the South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) enforces its own layer of regulatory requirements on top of the NAR-driven changes.

**Agents who treat this as a paperwork shuffle rather than a structural shift are exposing themselves to disciplinary action.** The SCREC has statutory authority under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-145 to sanction licensees for failure to properly disclose compensation arrangements. This article breaks down the specific forms, deadlines, compliance risks, and audit points that matter for South Carolina agents and brokers operating under the new rules.

## The Core Rule Change: No More Blanket MLS Compensation Offers

Before the settlement, listing agents in South Carolina routinely entered a cooperating broker compensation amount in MLS fields managed by systems like the Coastal Carolinas Association of REALTORS MLS, the SC REALTORS MLS, and the Charleston Trident MLS. That field is gone. Compensation offers from sellers to buyer agents can no longer be communicated through any MLS data field, IDX feed, or syndication platform.

This does not mean sellers cannot pay buyer agent fees. It means the mechanism for offering and agreeing to that compensation must exist in a separate written agreement — either between the buyer and their agent, or through a seller concession negotiated within the purchase contract. The distinction matters because it triggers different disclosure obligations under SCREC rules.

South Carolina agents should understand that [other states have tackled similar buyer agency agreement requirements](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026), but the South Carolina forms and enforcement mechanisms are state-specific.

## Required Forms and Documentation in South Carolina

The South Carolina Association of REALTORS (SCR) has updated its standard form library to reflect the settlement requirements. The critical forms agents must know include:

| Form | Purpose | When Required |
|------|---------|---------------|
| SCR Form 400 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Before any property showing |
| SCR Form 390 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Before any property showing (alternative) |
| SCR Form 310 | Listing Agreement (updated) | At listing appointment |
| SCR Form 350 | Seller's Authorization of Buyer Compensation | When seller agrees to pay buyer agent |

**SCR Form 400 is the workhorse document that every buyer's agent must execute before touring a property.** It specifies the exact compensation the buyer agrees to pay their agent, the duration of the agreement, and the scope of services. The form must include a specific dollar amount or percentage — vague language like "customary commission" will not satisfy the settlement terms or SCREC requirements under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-350(A)(14), which mandates that licensees provide clear, written disclosure of all compensation terms.

SCR Form 350 is newer and often overlooked. When a seller independently decides to offer compensation to a buyer's agent, this form documents that authorization outside the MLS. It must be signed before the listing agent communicates any such offer to a buyer's agent.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance carries real consequences in South Carolina. The SCREC has enforcement authority that extends beyond the NAR or any MLS organization, and the disciplinary framework is codified in S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-110 through 40-57-210.

A licensee who shows property without a signed buyer agency agreement faces potential MLS sanctions (including fines up to $5,000 per violation from certain South Carolina MLS systems), SCREC disciplinary proceedings, and civil liability from the buyer who may later dispute the compensation obligation. The SCREC can impose fines, require additional education, issue public reprimands, suspend licenses, or revoke them entirely.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a buyer discovers post-contract that their agent never executed a proper Form 400 or Form 390, the compensation clause in the purchase agreement may be unenforceable. This leaves the agent's brokerage pursuing a claim with no contractual foundation — a position no broker wants to defend in arbitration or court.

The [South Carolina Real Estate Commission](https://llr.sc.gov/rec/) has published guidance indicating that compensation-related complaints have increased since 2024, and investigators are actively reviewing transaction files for compliance gaps.

## Common Mistakes South Carolina Agents Are Making Right Now

After monitoring post-settlement enforcement trends and broker feedback across the state, five errors stand out as the most frequent — and the most dangerous.

**First, agents are using verbal agreements as a substitute for written buyer representation.** A handshake or text message saying "I'll show you this house" does not satisfy the requirement. The written agreement must be executed before the showing begins, not during the offer process or at closing.

Second, listing agents are embedding compensation language in MLS private remarks or agent-only fields. Some agents treat this as a workaround for the removed compensation field. Every major South Carolina MLS has explicitly prohibited this practice, and violations trigger automatic fines.

Third, buyer agents are failing to specify a concrete compensation amount on Form 400. Writing "to be determined" or "per MLS" is non-compliant. The form must state a fixed dollar amount or a clear percentage of the purchase price.

Fourth, agents are not updating Form 400 when circumstances change. If a buyer agency agreement specifies 2.5% but the seller's Form 350 only authorizes 2%, the buyer agent must disclose the shortfall to the buyer in writing and either amend Form 400 or explain who covers the difference.

Fifth, some agents are confusing seller concessions with compensation. A seller concession applied toward the buyer's closing costs is not the same as direct payment of buyer agent commission. These are different line items on the closing disclosure and trigger different regulatory treatments.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry direct supervisory liability under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-145(B). The statute makes brokers responsible for ensuring that every licensee under their supervision operates within legal requirements. Post-settlement, this obligation has expanded significantly.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Risk if Missed |
|------------|-----------|----------------|
| Signed Form 400/390 in file before first showing | Every transaction | MLS fines, SCREC complaint |
| Form 350 executed before communicating seller compensation offers | Every applicable listing | Misrepresentation claim |
| Compensation amount matches closing disclosure | At closing | Trust account discrepancy, SCREC audit |
| Buyer acknowledgment of compensation source | Before offer submission | Breach of fiduciary duty |
| Form 310 updated language (no MLS compensation promise) | Every new listing | MLS violation, settlement non-compliance |

Brokers should implement a pre-showing compliance check. Before any agent on their team tours a property with a buyer, the file must contain a signed buyer representation agreement with a specific compensation figure. This is not optional housekeeping — it is a regulatory requirement with personal liability attached to the broker-in-charge.

Transaction files should also document the source of buyer agent compensation for every closed deal. Did the buyer pay directly? Did the seller authorize it through Form 350? Was it a credit at closing? The answer must be traceable through the paperwork. Many brokers are [finding that missed deadlines and documentation gaps](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) create downstream compliance problems that surface months after closing.

## Compensation Negotiation Is Now a Skill, Not a Default

The old model — where 5-6% was split between listing and buyer agents through the MLS — trained agents to avoid compensation conversations. That era is over in South Carolina. Buyer agents must now articulate their value proposition clearly enough to justify their fee directly to the buyer.

This changes the dynamic at the listing appointment too. Sellers are asking listing agents whether they should offer buyer compensation at all, and if so, how much. Listing agents must be careful not to steer this decision — S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-350(A)(3) prohibits licensees from making misleading representations about market practices that could influence a principal's decision.

**Agents who document every compensation discussion in writing — including why a particular amount was recommended — will be best positioned if a complaint is filed.** The SCREC evaluates reasonableness based on what was disclosed, not what was assumed.
