## How Alabama Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026 Reshape Agent Practice

The Alabama commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 represent the most significant operational shift Alabama agents have faced since the state adopted mandatory agency disclosure in 1996. Every licensee operating under the Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) must now comply with practices that separate buyer-side compensation from MLS cooperation, require written agreements before touring, and demand unprecedented transparency around who pays what. Failing to adapt is not merely a competitive disadvantage—it carries license-level consequences.

**Alabama's regulatory framework layers state-specific requirements on top of the national settlement terms, creating a compliance environment that demands close attention.** The Alabama Real Estate License Law (Code of Alabama, Title 34, Chapter 27) and AREC's administrative rules in Chapter 790-X-3 remain the enforcement backbone. Agents who assume the national settlement memo alone covers their obligations will find themselves exposed when AREC auditors come calling.

The settlement, finalized in 2024 and now fully embedded in practice through 2026, eliminated blanket offers of buyer-agent compensation through MLS systems. What replaced it is a framework requiring direct negotiation, written disclosure, and informed consent from every party before a single dollar of commission changes hands.

## The Core Rule Changes and How Alabama Implements Them

Three structural pillars define the post-settlement commission landscape in Alabama. First, no MLS participant may communicate offers of buyer-agent compensation through MLS fields. Second, buyers must sign a written representation agreement specifying compensation terms before any property showing. Third, agents cannot receive compensation exceeding what their written buyer agreement authorizes unless amended in writing.

The Alabama Association of REALTORS (AAR) responded by revising several standard forms. Form 18 (Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement) now includes explicit compensation disclosure sections where agents must specify the amount or percentage they will earn, the source of that compensation, and whether they will seek the difference if a seller concession covers only part of the agreed fee. Form 20 (Buyer Representation Agreement—Non-Exclusive) received parallel updates.

On the listing side, AAR Form 1 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) was amended to remove default language about cooperating compensation. Instead, sellers may separately authorize concessions through an addendum that travels with the offer, not the MLS listing. This matters because [understanding where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often starts with confused commission expectations between parties who never discussed them directly.

## What the Alabama Real Estate Commission Expects From Licensees

AREC operates under [Title 34, Chapter 27 of the Code of Alabama](https://arec.alabama.gov/arec/arec.aspx) and enforces rules that predate the settlement but now carry heightened relevance. AREC Rule 790-X-3-.09 governs advertising and disclosure; it requires that any representation of fees or services be truthful, not misleading, and fully documented. An agent who tells a buyer "my services are free" without a written agreement specifying who actually pays violates this rule.

**AREC Rule 790-X-3-.03 mandates that all compensation arrangements be approved by the licensee's qualifying broker before execution.** This means an individual agent cannot independently negotiate a compensation structure with a buyer unless their broker has authorized that fee arrangement in advance. The broker's supervisory responsibility is codified, not optional.

The Commission also enforces Section 34-27-36 of the Alabama Code, which lists grounds for license revocation or suspension. Relevant grounds now include "substantial misrepresentation" regarding compensation, "failure to account for or remit funds," and "conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness." Each of these maps directly onto settlement-era violations where agents fail to document or honor commission terms.

## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Are Making Right Now

**The first and most frequent error is treating the buyer agreement as a formality rather than a binding compensation contract.** Agents rush through Form 18 at the first showing, leaving compensation fields vague or writing "negotiable" without specifying a range. AREC considers this inadequate disclosure and a potential violation of 790-X-3-.09.

The second mistake involves agents accepting compensation from sellers that exceeds the amount specified in their buyer agreement. Under the settlement terms, if your buyer agreement says you earn 2.5% and the seller offers a 3% concession, you cannot pocket the difference without amending the buyer agreement in writing and obtaining the buyer's informed consent.

Third, some agents are still verbally communicating commission expectations to cooperating agents or their clients without following up in writing. Alabama's statute of frauds (Code of Alabama Section 8-9-2) requires real estate commission agreements to be in writing to be enforceable. A verbal promise from a seller to cover buyer-agent fees carries zero legal weight if disputed.

Fourth, listing agents are embedding compensation language in MLS remarks fields—"seller willing to offer buyer concession"—which violates both the settlement's prohibition on MLS-communicated comp and local MLS rules enforced by Central Alabama MLS, Baldwin County MLS, and other Alabama MLS systems.

Fifth, agents neglect to provide a copy of the executed buyer agreement to their brokerage within the required timeframe. AREC's supervision rules require brokers to maintain transaction files, and [missing deadlines on required documents](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) creates audit exposure for the entire firm.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in Their Alabama Offices

Qualifying brokers in Alabama carry personal liability for their agents' compliance failures under Section 34-27-36(a)(17), which authorizes discipline for "failure to exercise adequate supervision over the activities of licensees." This is not theoretical—AREC has historically suspended broker licenses for systemic supervision failures.

Brokers should audit five specific areas on a quarterly basis at minimum. First, verify that every active buyer file contains an executed Form 18 or Form 20 dated before the first showing occurred. Second, confirm that the compensation amount in each buyer agreement matches the compensation actually received at closing, with written amendments documenting any variances.

Third, review listing files to ensure no MLS listing contains language that functions as an offer of buyer-agent compensation, even indirectly. Fourth, check that agents are not advertising "free buyer representation" without qualifying disclosures. Fifth, confirm that all compensation agreements have broker approval documented before the agent presented them to clients.

## Penalties and Liability If You Get This Wrong

The consequences of non-compliance operate on three levels: regulatory, contractual, and civil. Regulatorily, AREC can impose fines up to $2,500 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, and require additional education. The Commission's disciplinary process under AREC Rule 790-X-1-.10 provides for informal hearings that can escalate to formal administrative proceedings.

Contractually, a buyer who signed an agreement without adequate disclosure of compensation terms can argue the contract is voidable due to lack of informed consent. Alabama courts have historically treated real estate agency agreements as fiduciary contracts subject to heightened scrutiny. A deal can unravel at closing if the buyer's attorney discovers the agent received undisclosed compensation.

**On the civil side, agents face potential claims under the Alabama Deceptive Trade Practices Act (Code of Alabama Section 8-19-1 et seq.) if a consumer can demonstrate they were misled about fees.** This opens the door to actual damages, punitive damages, and attorneys' fees—a financial exposure that dwarfs any commission earned on the transaction.
