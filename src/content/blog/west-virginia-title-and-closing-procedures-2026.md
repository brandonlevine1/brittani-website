## Understanding West Virginia Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Real estate closings in West Virginia follow a distinct path shaped by the state's attorney-centric title examination tradition and its unique mineral rights landscape. If you are an agent or broker operating under the West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC), understanding **West Virginia title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional—it is a license-protection imperative. Missed steps do not just delay closings; they expose you to disciplinary action under W. Va. Code 30-40-19 and potential civil liability.

West Virginia remains one of a handful of states where title opinions are issued exclusively by licensed attorneys. This means the closing timeline, document flow, and agent responsibilities look different here than in states that rely solely on title company processors. The 2026 regulatory environment adds updated WVREC guidance on wire fraud disclosure and revised standard-form language that every licensee must internalize.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Closings

**W. Va. Code Chapter 36 (Estates and Property) and Chapter 30 (Professions and Occupations) form the twin pillars of West Virginia's closing requirements.** Specifically, W. Va. Code 36-3-5 governs deed execution and acknowledgment, while W. Va. Code 30-2-4 restricts title certification to members of the West Virginia State Bar. Agents who attempt to offer title opinions—even informally—risk an unauthorized practice of law claim.

The [West Virginia Uniform Real Property Electronic Recording Act](https://www.wvlegislature.gov/wvcode/code.cfm?chap=39a&art=1) (W. Va. Code 39A-1-1 et seq.) enables electronic recording in participating counties, but most Mountain State counties still require wet-ink originals for deed recordation. Agents must confirm county-specific requirements with the local clerk of the county commission before scheduling settlement.

Recording fees are set by W. Va. Code 59-1-2, which establishes a base fee of $20.00 for the first page and $5.00 for each additional page. Excise tax on real property transfers is governed by W. Va. Code 11-22-2 at a rate of $1.10 per $500 of consideration for state tax, plus an additional $1.10 per $500 county excise tax—for a combined rate of $2.20 per $500 in most counties.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

West Virginia agents primarily work with forms published by the West Virginia Association of Realtors (WVAR). The most relevant forms for title and closing include:

| Form | Purpose | Agent Responsibility |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| WV-REPC Form 1 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Ensure title objection deadlines are properly calendared |
| WV-SD Form | Seller's Disclosure Statement | Confirm delivery before title work begins |
| WV-CD Form | Closing Date Extension Addendum | Execute if title defects delay settlement |
| ALTA Settlement Statement | Final cost accounting | Review for accuracy against contract terms |
| WV Deed of Trust (form varies) | Security instrument for lender | Confirm recording instructions with closing attorney |

The WV-REPC Form 1 contains specific provisions in Paragraph 8 regarding title examination, objections, and cure periods. Agents must ensure buyers receive the title commitment at least 5 business days before scheduled closing—a timeline that gets blown regularly when agents order title work too late. Missing this window does not void the contract, but it gives buyers a legitimate basis to postpone settlement.

Understanding [seller disclosure requirements](/blog/west-virginia-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026) is directly connected to the title process because undisclosed liens, judgments, or boundary disputes often surface during the title examination.

## The Title Examination and Commitment Process

A West Virginia title examination begins when the buyer's chosen title company or closing attorney orders a search of the county records. The examining attorney reviews the chain of title going back at least 40 years (the standard of practice recommended by the West Virginia State Bar's Title Examination Standards). Any break in the chain, unreleased deed of trust, judgment lien, estate issue, or mineral reservation gets flagged in Schedule B of the title commitment.

**Mineral rights present a unique challenge in West Virginia that agents from other states rarely encounter.** Under West Virginia's mineral severance doctrine, subsurface rights may have been separated from the surface estate decades ago. The title examiner must trace both the surface and mineral chains, and agents need to disclose any known severances to buyers. Failure to address mineral rights in the purchase agreement has killed deals and generated lawsuits—particularly in the state's gas-producing eastern panhandle and southern counties.

Once the title commitment is issued, the buyer has the contractual right to object to any exceptions listed in Schedule B. Under WV-REPC Form 1, Paragraph 8(b), the seller typically has 30 days to cure objections. If the seller cannot deliver marketable title within that window, the buyer may terminate and recover earnest money. Agents who let title objection deadlines pass without written notice expose their clients to waiver arguments.

## Closing Day Mechanics and Settlement Procedures

West Virginia closings overwhelmingly occur in the offices of settlement attorneys or title companies rather than at real estate brokerages. The closing attorney serves as the settlement agent, prepares the HUD-1 or ALTA Settlement Statement, handles disbursement of funds, and records documents with the county clerk.

Here is how costs typically break down between buyer and seller in West Virginia:

| Cost Item | Typically Paid By | Statutory/Custom Basis |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Owner's title insurance | Seller | Local custom (negotiable) |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| State excise tax ($1.10/$500) | Seller | W. Va. Code 11-22-2 |
| County excise tax ($1.10/$500) | Seller | W. Va. Code 11-22-2 |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer | Custom |
| Recording fees (deed of trust release) | Seller | Custom |
| Attorney/settlement fee | Split or negotiable | Custom varies by county |

Agents must verify final numbers on the settlement statement against the contract at least 48 hours before closing. The TRID rule (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure) requires the buyer to receive the Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days before consummation for federally related mortgage loans. A last-minute fee change can trigger a new 3-day waiting period, blowing the closing date and potentially the rate lock.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in West Virginia Closings

**The following errors account for the majority of agent-caused closing delays and WVREC complaints related to title and settlement.**

The first mistake is ordering title work too late. Agents who wait until financing approval to order the title search leave zero margin for curing defects. Best practice is ordering the title search within 3 business days of contract execution, regardless of financing contingency status. A 14-day search plus a 30-day cure period can consume the entire contract timeline.

The second mistake is failing to calendar title objection deadlines. Under WV-REPC Form 1, the buyer must deliver written objections within a specified number of days after receiving the title commitment. If the agent does not track this deadline—and many do not—the buyer may be deemed to have accepted all Schedule B exceptions. This is a malpractice claim waiting to happen, and it stems from the same [reasons agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in other contexts.

The third mistake involves mineral rights assumptions. Agents regularly tell buyers "you'll get everything" without confirming mineral ownership. In West Virginia, where mineral severance dates back to the 1880s in many counties, this creates material misrepresentation risk under W. Va. Code 30-40-19(a)(8), which prohibits licensees from making false or misleading representations about property.

The fourth mistake is providing wiring instructions via unverified email. Wire fraud targeting real estate closings has exploded nationally, and West Virginia is not immune. WVREC's 2025 advisory bulletin directed licensees to verbally verify all wiring instructions through a known phone number before transmitting to clients. Agents who forward wire instructions received via email without verification face potential liability for client losses.

The fifth mistake is failing to disclose dual agency or designated agency status before the closing attorney begins work. Under W. Va. Code 30-40-20, agency disclosure must occur at the earliest substantive contact. If the closing attorney discovers undisclosed dual representation at settlement, the transaction can be voided and the agent faces disciplinary proceedings.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under W. Va. Code 30-40-21 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. This means title and closing errors committed by agents flow uphill. A broker who fails to maintain reasonable supervisory systems risks license suspension, fines up to $5,000 per violation, and civil liability to harmed parties.

**Every West Virginia brokerage should audit the following items on every closed transaction file.** First, confirm that [agency disclosure](/blog/west-virginia-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) documentation is signed and dated prior to any title or closing activity. Second, verify that the title commitment was delivered to the buyer within the contractual timeframe and that any objection deadlines were tracked in writing. Third, confirm that the settlement statement reconciles with the contract terms—particularly regarding who pays excise taxes, title insurance, and attorney fees.

Brokers should also maintain a written wire fraud prevention protocol and document agent compliance. The WVREC has signaled that failure to implement wire fraud safeguards may constitute a supervision deficiency in future audits. Transaction file reviews should happen within 30 days of closing, not quarterly, to catch systemic problems before they compound.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Risk if Missed |
|------------|-----------|----------------|
| Agency disclosure in file | Every transaction | License suspension, deal rescission |
| Title commitment delivery confirmation | Every transaction | Buyer waiver claims, malpractice exposure |
| Settlement statement vs. contract reconciliation | Every transaction | Client overpayment, fiduciary breach |
| Wire fraud protocol compliance | Every transaction | Civil liability for client losses |
| Mineral rights disclosure verification | Transactions with severed minerals | Misrepresentation claim under W. Va. Code 30-40-19 |

## Liability Consequences for Non-Compliance

The WVREC has authority under W. Va. Code 30-40-23 to impose sanctions ranging from formal reprimands to license revocation. Monetary penalties can reach $5,000 per violation, and the Commission can order restitution to injured consumers. Beyond administrative action, agents face civil liability for negligence, breach of fiduciary duty, and—in cases of knowing misrepresentation—fraud claims that title insurance will not cover.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate consequence. When title defects emerge that should have been flagged earlier, or when closing dates are missed due to agent negligence, buyers exercise termination rights and recover earnest money. The listing agent's seller then faces re-marketing costs, potential price reduction, and extended carrying costs. These damages are quantifiable and recoverable in a civil action against the responsible licensee and their brokerage.

Errors and omissions insurance provides some protection, but policies universally exclude intentional acts and may not cover wire fraud losses caused by agent negligence. Agents operating without robust closing procedures are betting their license and personal assets on luck—a bet that fails often enough to fill a docket.
