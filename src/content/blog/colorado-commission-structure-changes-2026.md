## Colorado Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026: The Ground-Level Reality

The Colorado commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have reshaped how agents on both sides of a transaction earn, disclose, and negotiate their fees. The changes are not theoretical—they are encoded in revised Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC) forms, enforced through MLS compliance audits, and backed by real disciplinary consequences. If you hold an active Colorado license and have not updated your practices since August 2024, you are already behind.

Colorado was not caught flat-footed by the settlement. The state had long required written buyer representation agreements under CREC Rule E-35, which mandates that licensees disclose their brokering relationships before performing substantive services. But the NAR settlement layered new restrictions on top of existing Colorado law, particularly around how compensation is communicated, who authorizes it, and where it can be advertised.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed MLS Compensation Rules in Colorado

**The single biggest operational change is the prohibition on offers of buyer-broker compensation in MLS listing data.** Before August 2024, a listing agent in Denver could enter "2.8% to buyer's agent" directly into the REcolorado MLS. That field no longer exists. Compensation offers cannot appear in any MLS-accessible data field, including broker remarks.

This does not mean sellers cannot offer to pay buyer-agent fees. It means the communication channel shifted entirely off-MLS. Sellers who want to incentivize buyer representation must do so through separate written agreements, broker-to-broker negotiation, or marketing channels outside MLS platforms. The [NAR settlement practice changes](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) explicitly permit seller-funded buyer-agent compensation—they just cannot flow through MLS infrastructure.

For Colorado agents, this means commission conversations now happen earlier, more explicitly, and with more documentation than ever before. The days of defaulting to "whatever the listing says" are over.

## Required Colorado Forms and Where Commission Must Be Disclosed

Colorado's Division of Real Estate and the Real Estate Commission publish standardized forms that most brokerages are required to use or substantially replicate. Since the settlement, several forms carry heightened importance for commission compliance.

| Form | Number | Purpose | Commission Relevance |
|------|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Exclusive Right to Buy Contract | CBS1-7-24 | Buyer representation agreement | Must state specific compensation amount or calculation method |
| Exclusive Right to Sell Contract | LC50-6-24 | Listing agreement | Defines total compensation and any seller-offered buyer-agent payment |
| Agreement to Amend/Extend Contract | AE44-6-24 | Modifies existing contracts | Used to adjust compensation mid-transaction |
| Brokerage Disclosure to Buyer | BD68 | Relationship disclosure | Must be presented before substantive services begin |
| Commission Disbursement Agreement | Brokerage-specific | Authorizes payment splits | Required when compensation source differs from listing broker |

The Exclusive Right to Buy Contract (CBS1-7-24) is the enforcement point that most directly reflects NAR settlement mandates. Under this form, agents must specify their compensation in a dollar amount, percentage, or clearly defined calculation. Open-ended phrases like "customary commission" or "to be determined" do not satisfy CREC requirements. The form also requires the buyer's acknowledgment that compensation is negotiable and not set by law.

If your brokerage uses proprietary forms instead of CREC-approved versions, those forms must contain equivalent disclosures. CREC Rule F-4 requires that any alternative forms be at least as protective of the consumer as Commission-approved versions. Agents working under brokerages that use non-standard forms should verify this with their managing broker immediately.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Non-compliance is not an abstract risk in Colorado. The Division of Real Estate has enforcement authority under C.R.S. 12-10-217, which allows disciplinary action for violations of Commission rules, including failure to disclose compensation terms and performing brokering services without proper written agreements.

**Potential consequences range from administrative citations to license revocation.** CREC can impose fines up to $2,500 per violation under certain circumstances, require additional education, place a license on probation, or suspend/revoke the license entirely. For settlement-related violations, the most common enforcement triggers are performing showing services without a signed buyer agreement, misrepresenting compensation availability in marketing materials, and failing to specify compensation terms in the CBS1 form.

Beyond regulatory action, non-compliance creates civil liability. A buyer who discovers their agent earned undisclosed compensation from a listing broker could bring a claim for breach of fiduciary duty under Colorado common law. Similarly, a deal that closes with ambiguous commission authorization can result in post-closing disputes between brokerages—disputes that now require formal commission disbursement agreements rather than MLS-based assumptions.

Transaction cancellation is also on the table. If a buyer's agent cannot demonstrate a valid buyer representation agreement at the time services were rendered, the agent's claim to compensation may be unenforceable. This is true regardless of whether the deal ultimately closes. Managing your [transaction coordination paperwork](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) is no longer just about efficiency—it is about protecting your income.

## Common Mistakes Colorado Agents Are Making Right Now

After monitoring compliance patterns since the settlement took effect, five errors appear repeatedly among Colorado licensees.

**First, agents are presenting the Exclusive Right to Buy Contract at the wrong time.** CREC Rule E-35 requires the agreement before substantive brokering services. Showing a property is substantive. Pulling comps for a specific property is substantive. Agents who wait until a buyer wants to write an offer are already in violation—and their compensation claim for prior work is legally questionable.

Second, agents are using verbal commission agreements instead of written ones. Colorado's Statute of Frauds (C.R.S. 38-10-108) requires that agreements authorizing the payment of real estate commissions be in writing. A text message or email thread may satisfy the writing requirement in some circumstances, but CREC-approved forms are the safest path. Agents relying on "we discussed it on the phone" are building their compensation on sand.

Third, listing agents are including commission offers in broker-to-broker remarks within REcolorado. Even coded language like "seller is motivated to contribute" or "contact LA for compensation details" has triggered MLS compliance notices. REcolorado's rules are explicit: no compensation offers in any MLS field, including private remarks visible only to cooperating brokers.

Fourth, agents are failing to update compensation terms when deal conditions change. If a buyer negotiates a seller concession that was originally earmarked for agent compensation, the CBS1 form and any related addenda must be amended using form AE44. Agents who silently absorb the reduction or shift it without documentation risk audit findings.

Fifth, some agents are treating the buyer agreement as a one-size-fits-all document and not tailoring the compensation clause to the specific transaction. CREC expects that the amount or method stated in the CBS1 reflects the actual agreement between broker and client—not a template default the agent never adjusted.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry direct supervisory liability under C.R.S. 12-10-201 and CREC Rule D-1. If an employed or associated licensee commits a settlement-related violation, the managing broker can face independent disciplinary action for inadequate supervision.

**Every Colorado brokerage should be auditing five compliance points on a rolling basis.** First, verify that every active buyer relationship has a signed CBS1 or equivalent agreement on file before showing activity began. Second, review all active listings to confirm no compensation language exists in MLS remarks—private or public. Third, confirm that commission disbursement instructions match the terms in both the buyer agreement and any separate seller-funded compensation addenda. Fourth, ensure all team members—including unlicensed staff handling scheduling—understand that property access triggers the agreement requirement. Fifth, audit closed files quarterly to confirm AE44 amendments exist wherever compensation terms shifted during the transaction.

| Audit Point | Frequency | Who Reviews | Red Flag |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Signed CBS1 before first showing | Per transaction | TC or compliance officer | Showing date precedes agreement date |
| MLS listing compliance | Weekly | Listing manager | Any compensation language in remarks |
| Disbursement vs. agreement match | At closing | Managing broker or TC | Dollar amounts do not reconcile |
| AE44 amendments for changes | Per transaction | Assigned agent + TC | Compensation shifted without documented amendment |
| BD68 delivery timing | Per new client | Managing broker | No signed BD68 or date is after first substantive contact |

Brokers who rely on [checklists rather than memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) for these audits will catch problems before they become regulatory complaints. The volume of form-timing issues alone justifies investing in systematic file review rather than spot-checking.

## Compensation Negotiation Tactics That Stay Within the Lines

The settlement did not eliminate commission negotiation—it formalized it. Colorado agents can still negotiate any compensation amount, funded by any party, as long as the terms are documented, disclosed, and agreed upon before substantive services begin.

Seller-funded buyer-agent compensation remains the most common structure in Colorado residential transactions. The mechanism changed, not the economics. A listing agent who wants to market seller-funded compensation simply does so outside MLS channels—through brokerage websites, direct outreach, open house materials, or written offers communicated broker-to-broker.

Buyer-funded compensation is also gaining traction, particularly in competitive markets along the Front Range where sellers resist concessions. In these cases, the CBS1 form governs: the buyer agrees to pay their agent directly, and the compensation amount is typically factored into the buyer's total cost analysis. Some buyers are folding agent compensation into their loan if the lender permits it, though this adds appraisal and underwriting complexity.

The critical compliance rule: whatever structure the parties choose, the buyer must see and agree to the specific compensation their agent will receive before that agent performs substantive work. Ambiguity here is the fastest path to a CREC complaint.
