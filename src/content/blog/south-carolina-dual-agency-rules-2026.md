## Understanding South Carolina Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

South Carolina treats agency relationships with more statutory specificity than many neighboring states, and agents who treat disclosure as an afterthought face real consequences. The **South Carolina dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** framework is governed primarily by the SC Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act and Title 40, Chapter 57 of the South Carolina Code of Laws, particularly Sections 40-57-137 through 40-57-370. Every licensee operating in the state needs to understand how these statutes interact with SC Real Estate Commission forms and brokerage-level policies.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) sits under the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation (LLR) and holds enforcement authority over agency violations. Getting this wrong is not a paperwork nuisance—it is a license-threatening event.

## How South Carolina Defines Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency

**Dual agency exists when a single licensee—or a brokerage acting through one agent—represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction.** South Carolina Code Section 40-57-350 explicitly permits dual agency, but only with the informed written consent of all parties. The statute frames consent as a precondition, not a formality to be handled at closing.

Designated agency is a distinct arrangement. Under Section 40-57-350(B), a broker-in-charge may designate individual licensees within the firm to represent the buyer and the seller separately. Each designated agent owes full fiduciary duties—loyalty, confidentiality, obedience, disclosure, accounting, and reasonable care—to their respective client.

The distinction matters because designated agency avoids many of the fiduciary conflicts inherent in true dual agency. The broker-in-charge who makes the designation, however, becomes a dual agent by default and must remain neutral unless the brokerage's written policy states otherwise.

| | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Who represents both parties? | One licensee or the brokerage as a whole | Two separate licensees within the same brokerage |
| Fiduciary duties | Limited—agent cannot advocate for either side against the other | Full fiduciary duties to each respective client |
| Consent required | Written consent from both buyer and seller | Written consent from both parties plus broker designation |
| Broker-in-charge role | Acts as the dual agent or supervises the dual agent | Becomes a statutory dual agent with neutrality obligations |
| Primary risk | Inadvertent breach of confidentiality | Information leakage between designated agents |

## Required Forms and Timing of Disclosure

South Carolina mandates disclosure at "first substantive contact," which the SCREC interprets as the point at which a licensee begins discussing a party's specific needs, financial situation, or motivation. This means disclosure must happen before you show a property, not after.

**The primary form is [Form 520—Disclosure of Agency Relationship](https://llr.sc.gov/re/POL/forms.aspx), published by the SC Real Estate Commission.** Form 520 identifies the types of agency available and requires the consumer's signature acknowledging the relationship. It is not a contract creating agency; it is a disclosure confirming that the consumer understands the role the licensee will play.

When dual agency arises mid-transaction (the most common scenario—one agent's listing attracts the same agent's buyer client), both parties must sign Form 520 again or a supplemental written consent document that specifically addresses the dual agency arrangement. Some brokerages use a custom dual agency addendum alongside Form 520 to satisfy the statutory "informed" standard. The SCREC has made clear in guidance letters that a single signature at initial engagement does not constitute informed consent to dual agency that materializes later.

If a broker-in-charge designates agents, the designation must be documented in writing. While the Commission does not mandate a specific numbered form for the designation itself, best practice is to record it in the brokerage transaction file with the names, dates, and roles of each designated licensee—ideally referencing the property address and the parties involved.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The penalties for agency disclosure violations in South Carolina are not theoretical. The SCREC has the statutory authority under Section 40-57-145 to impose fines up to $1,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, and require additional education. The Commission reviews complaints through its investigative division, and cases involving undisclosed dual agency consistently appear in published disciplinary orders.

Beyond administrative action, civil liability is the larger financial threat. A party who was not properly informed of dual agency can seek rescission of the contract. South Carolina courts have held that a material breach of fiduciary duty—such as failing to disclose dual agency—can void the transaction and expose the agent and brokerage to damages including lost equity, relocation costs, and attorney fees.

Errors and omissions insurance policies commonly contain exclusions for knowing violations of agency law. If you knowingly failed to disclose, your E&O carrier may deny the claim. The brokerage's liability insurance may similarly exclude coverage when the violation stems from a policy the broker failed to enforce.

## Common Mistakes South Carolina Agents Make

**The following errors appear repeatedly in SCREC disciplinary files and malpractice claims.** Each one is avoidable with proper systems.

First, agents wait until the offer stage to address dual agency. The statute requires consent before the licensee acts in a dual capacity—not when it becomes convenient. By the time you are writing an offer for your buyer on your own listing, both parties must already have signed dual agency consent. Presenting the form alongside the purchase agreement creates the appearance of coercion and fails the "informed" standard.

Second, agents confuse transaction brokerage with dual agency. South Carolina recognizes transaction brokerage as a lawful relationship type, but it carries different duties than dual agency. An agent who checks the wrong box on Form 520 or verbally tells a client they are "just handling the paperwork" while actually performing dual agency functions faces both disciplinary and civil exposure.

Third, designated agents within the same brokerage share confidential client information—sometimes inadvertently during office meetings, sometimes through shared CRM notes. Section 40-57-350(B) requires each designated agent to maintain confidentiality as if the other party were represented by an outside firm. Shared transaction management systems without proper access controls create discoverable evidence of information leakage.

Fourth, brokers-in-charge fail to document the designation in writing. The decision to designate often happens verbally in the hallway. Without a dated, signed record, the brokerage cannot prove it established designated agency rather than default dual agency—a distinction that determines whether each agent owes full or limited fiduciary duties.

Fifth, agents neglect to re-disclose when circumstances change. A buyer initially working with Agent A decides to purchase a property listed by Agent B at the same firm. The agency relationship has shifted, and new written consent is required. Many agents treat the original Form 520 as a blanket authorization for any agency configuration that might arise.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Broker compliance obligations extend well beyond hiring good agents and hoping they follow the rules. Under Section 40-57-137, the broker-in-charge bears supervisory responsibility for all licensees operating under their license. A failure to supervise is independently actionable.

**Brokers should audit three elements in every transaction file: timing of disclosure, specificity of consent, and documentation of designation.** Timing means confirming that Form 520 was signed before substantive contact—look at the date on the form versus the date of the first showing or the first CRM note about the client's needs. Specificity means confirming that any dual agency consent explicitly names the property and both parties, not just a generic acknowledgment. Documentation of designation means a written record identifying which licensee represents which party, signed by the broker-in-charge.

| Audit Element | What to Look For | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of Form 520 | Date on form precedes first showing or substantive conversation logged in CRM | Form 520 dated same day as offer or later |
| Dual agency consent | Separate written consent naming both parties and the specific property | Only the initial Form 520 on file with no supplemental consent |
| Designated agency documentation | Broker-signed designation naming both agents, clients, and property address | No written designation; only verbal confirmation |
| Confidentiality controls | Separate CRM access, separate file notes, no shared showing feedback | Both agents accessing the same client notes or transaction thread |
| Policy manual reference | Brokerage policy manual addresses dual and designated agency procedures | No written policy or policy has not been updated since statutory amendments |

Brokers who operate teams face additional scrutiny. If a team leader lists a property and a team member brings the buyer, designated agency within the team requires the same formalities as across the brokerage. The SCREC does not carve out exceptions for teams operating under a single team leader's supervision. Quarterly file audits—not annual—catch problems before they become complaints. Smart brokerages run a checklist-based review within 48 hours of mutual acceptance, confirming all agency forms are present, properly dated, and signed by the correct parties. ## Practical Scenarios That Trip Up Experienced Agents

Consider a listing agent who holds an open house. A walk-in buyer without representation asks the listing agent to write an offer that afternoon. The listing agent now faces a dual agency situation. If the agent proceeds without obtaining written dual agency consent from both the seller and the unrepresented buyer—and without presenting Form 520 disclosing the dual agency relationship—the transaction is vulnerable to rescission.

Another scenario: a brokerage with fifteen agents designates Agent X to represent the seller and Agent Y to represent the buyer. During a price negotiation, the broker-in-charge mentions to Agent X that the buyer has been pre-approved for $50,000 above the offer price. That single comment constitutes a breach of the broker's neutrality obligation and the buyer's confidentiality. It can unwind the deal and generate a complaint.

These situations arise because [deals break down at predictable points](/blog/where-deals-break-down), and agency confusion amplifies every other pressure in the transaction.
