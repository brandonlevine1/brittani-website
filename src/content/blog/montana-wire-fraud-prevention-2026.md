## Understanding Montana Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

Wire fraud targeting real estate closings has surged by over 50 percent nationally since 2022, and Montana is no longer a low-risk outlier. The Montana wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 framework places direct obligations on licensed agents, their brokers, and the title companies they work with. Failing to meet these obligations can cost your client hundreds of thousands of dollars and cost you your license.

Montana's Board of Realty Regulation, operating under the Montana Department of Labor and Industry, enforces licensee conduct standards codified in the Administrative Rules of Montana (ARM), Title 24, Chapter 210. While Montana does not yet have a standalone wire fraud statute like some states, the existing duty-of-care rules under ARM 24.210.641 (competency and supervision) and ARM 24.210.625 (disclosure obligations) effectively require agents to implement wire fraud prevention measures in every transaction involving electronic fund transfers.

The Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) reinforced this reality in its 2025 forms update by releasing Form 610-WF, a standalone wire fraud advisory designed for use alongside the existing purchase agreement. If your brokerage has not integrated this form into its standard closing packet, you are already behind.

## The Regulatory Framework Behind the Requirement

**Montana does not have a single "wire fraud prevention" statute, which paradoxically makes compliance more dangerous to ignore.** The obligation is derived from multiple overlapping sources: ARM 24.210.641 (requiring licensees to perform services competently), ARM 24.210.625 (requiring disclosure of material risks), and the Montana Consumer Protection Act under MCA 30-14-103, which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade.

The Board of Realty Regulation has issued informal guidance through its quarterly newsletter and continuing education mandates indicating that failure to warn clients about wire fraud constitutes a potential violation of competency standards. This means an agent who closes a deal without providing a wire fraud advisory could face disciplinary review even if no actual fraud occurred.

Title companies operating in Montana must also comply with the Montana Title Insurance Act (MCA 33-25-101 et seq.), which governs their handling of escrow funds. When a title company verifies wire instructions through callback protocols, that protects them — but it does not protect the agent from a separate negligence claim if the agent forwarded compromised wiring details to a client via email.

## Key Forms and Documentation for Montana Agents

The primary form agents should use is MAR Form 610-WF (Wire Fraud Advisory and Acknowledgment). This one-page document warns buyers and sellers that wire instructions can be intercepted, advises them never to trust wiring details received solely via email, and requires their signature acknowledging the risk. It should be presented at the time of contract execution or no later than the point at which the title company is identified.

| Form/Document | Purpose | When to Present | Who Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAR Form 610-WF | Wire fraud advisory and acknowledgment | At contract execution or title company selection | Buyer and seller |
| MAR Form 100 (Purchase Agreement) | References wire fraud advisory in closing provisions | Contract execution | Both parties |
| Brokerage Wire Policy Addendum | Internal compliance documentation | Agent onboarding or annual renewal | Agent and broker |
| Title Company Callback Confirmation | Verifies wire instructions via phone | Before any wire transfer | Title officer, buyer/seller |

Some brokerages have developed their own proprietary wire fraud advisory forms. These are acceptable provided they cover the same substantive warnings as Form 610-WF. However, if your brokerage form omits the callback verification instruction or fails to disclaim agent liability for intercepted communications, it may expose you to greater risk than the MAR standard form.

**Document retention matters here as much as delivery.** ARM 24.210.641 requires licensees to maintain transaction records for a minimum of five years. Your signed wire fraud advisory must be part of that retained file, whether digital or physical.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences fall into three buckets: regulatory discipline, civil liability, and insurance coverage gaps.

On the regulatory side, the Montana Board of Realty Regulation can issue sanctions ranging from a formal reprimand to license suspension under ARM 24.210.601. A pattern of omitting wire fraud advisories — even without actual fraud losses — demonstrates a systemic competency failure that the Board has flagged as a priority enforcement area for 2026.

Civil liability is the more financially devastating risk. If a buyer wires $300,000 to a fraudulent account after receiving spoofed instructions, and the agent never provided a written warning about this exact scenario, the agent becomes a target in the subsequent lawsuit. Montana courts apply a comparative negligence standard (MCA 27-1-702), meaning the agent does not need to be the primary wrongdoer to bear a percentage of the loss.

| Compliance Failure | Potential Consequence | Typical Severity |
|---|---|---|
| No wire fraud advisory delivered | Board reprimand, possible suspension | Moderate to severe |
| Wiring instructions sent via unencrypted email | Civil liability, E&O denial | Severe |
| No callback verification performed | Shared liability with title company | Moderate |
| Failure to retain signed advisory in file | Audit finding, potential fine up to $1,000 | Low to moderate |
| Agent impersonates title company instructions | Criminal referral under MCA 45-6-317 | Severe — felony territory |

Errors and Omissions insurance carriers have increasingly added exclusion clauses for wire fraud losses where the insured agent failed to follow documented best practices. If your E&O policy contains such an exclusion and you did not deliver the advisory, your carrier may deny coverage entirely — leaving you personally responsible for six-figure judgments.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make

The first and most frequent error is treating the wire fraud advisory as optional supplemental paperwork. It is not optional. Agents who skip it because "the title company handles that" misunderstand the layered nature of liability in Montana. The title company's callback protocol protects the title company. Your advisory protects you.

The second mistake is emailing wire instructions directly to clients. Even when agents receive legitimate instructions from the title company, forwarding those details via standard email creates an interception point. ARM 24.210.641's competency standard requires agents to understand this risk. The correct protocol is to direct clients to call the title company's verified phone number — one the client looked up independently, not one embedded in an email.

**Third, agents routinely present the wire fraud advisory too late in the transaction.** Delivering it at the closing table, after the wire has already been sent, provides zero protection. Best practice — and what the Board expects — is delivery at or before the point when the buyer first receives title company contact information.

Fourth, agents in dual agency situations sometimes deliver the advisory to only one party. Both the buyer and the seller must receive and sign the form, because sellers receiving net proceeds via wire are equally vulnerable to interception schemes targeting their bank account details.

Fifth, some agents rely on verbal warnings without any written documentation. A verbal warning has zero evidentiary value if the Board investigates or if a civil suit is filed. ## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under ARM 24.210.641 and MCA 37-51-321. If an agent in your office fails to deliver wire fraud advisories and a client suffers a loss, the Board will examine whether your brokerage had written policies, training protocols, and audit mechanisms in place.

**At minimum, Montana brokers should audit for the presence of a signed wire fraud advisory in every closed transaction file.** This is not a quarterly spot-check item — it should be part of your standard file review before commission disbursement. If the advisory is missing, the file is incomplete and should not close your internal review process.

Brokers should also maintain a written Wire Fraud Prevention Policy as part of their office policy manual. This document should specify that agents must never email wire instructions, must always direct clients to perform independent callback verification, and must present MAR Form 610-WF (or approved equivalent) before the buyer initiates any wire transfer. The policy should be signed annually by every agent in the office, and that signature should be retained in the agent's personnel file.

Training is the third enforcement pillar. Montana's continuing education requirements under ARM 24.210.412 mandate specific coursework hours, and several approved CE providers now offer wire fraud prevention modules. Brokers should verify that agents have completed at least one such module within the current licensing cycle. ## Callback Verification: The Non-Negotiable Step

The single most effective fraud prevention measure is callback verification — the practice of confirming wire instructions by calling a known phone number that was not provided in the email containing the instructions. This sounds simple, but the failure rate is staggering.

> "No wire transfer should be initiated based solely on instructions received via email. The recipient must independently verify the instructions by calling the sending party at a phone number obtained from a source other than the email in question." — Montana Association of Realtors, 2025 Wire Fraud Prevention Guidance

Agents should instruct their clients on this protocol explicitly, both verbally and in writing. The MAR Form 610-WF includes a section where the client initials acknowledgment of the callback requirement. If a client proceeds to wire funds without performing the callback, that initialed section becomes evidence that the agent fulfilled their duty to warn.

Title companies in Montana have largely adopted their own callback protocols internally, but agents cannot rely on that alone. The agent's independent duty exists regardless of what the title company does.
