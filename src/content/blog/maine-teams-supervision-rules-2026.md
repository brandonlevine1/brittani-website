The shift toward team-based real estate models has changed how agents in Maine structure their businesses, split commissions, and market themselves to the public. But the Maine Real Estate Commission (MREC) has not relaxed its expectations about supervision, advertising, and accountability just because agents now operate under team banners. Understanding **Maine teams & supervision rules 2026** is not optional—it is the difference between a productive year and a disciplinary hearing.

Maine's regulatory framework for teams sits primarily within Title 32, Chapter 114 of the Maine Revised Statutes and the administrative rules promulgated by MREC under Chapter 420 (Advertising) and Chapter 450 (Brokerage Operations). These rules apply uniformly whether you run a two-person partnership or a 15-agent mega-team operating across multiple offices.

## How Maine Defines a Real Estate Team

Maine does not have a standalone "team license." Every agent on a team holds an individual license—either as a sales agent or associate broker—and operates under the umbrella of a designated broker. The team itself has no legal identity separate from the brokerage. This distinction matters because it dictates where liability falls and how advertising must be structured.

Under 32 M.R.S. § 13173, a designated broker bears full supervisory responsibility for all licensees affiliated with their brokerage, including those organized into teams. MREC has clarified through informal guidance letters and formal rule interpretation that a team leader does not assume the supervisory obligations of the designated broker unless that leader also holds a broker's license and has been formally designated in writing.

**Team leaders who believe they "run" their team independently of their broker are operating under a dangerous misconception.** The designated broker's name must appear on all contracts, and the broker retains final authority over transaction management, trust account deposits, and dispute resolution. This is non-negotiable under Maine law.

## Advertising and Team Name Requirements

MREC Rule Chapter 420 governs all advertising by licensees, and its application to teams is strict. The rule requires that any advertisement—digital, print, signage, social media, or otherwise—include the licensed brokerage name displayed at least as prominently as the team name. "At least as prominent" means equal or larger font size, equal or greater visual weight, and placement that a reasonable consumer would notice.

A team cannot use a name that implies it is a standalone brokerage. For example, a team calling itself "Coastal Maine Realty Group" without a clear brokerage affiliation would violate Chapter 420 because a consumer could reasonably believe the team is a separate brokerage entity.

| Advertising Element | Compliant Example | Non-Compliant Example |
|---|---|---|
| Yard signs | "Smith Team — ABC Realty" (equal font) | "Smith Team" (no brokerage name) |
| Social media profiles | Bio reads "Smith Team at ABC Realty, Portland" | Bio reads only "Smith Team, Portland ME" |
| Email signatures | Includes team name + full brokerage name | Uses team logo only with no brokerage reference |
| Website homepage | Brokerage name in header or equally visible location | Brokerage buried in footer small print |
| Business cards | Both names printed, brokerage at least as large | Team name dominates, brokerage in 6pt font |

MREC has issued multiple consent agreements in recent years involving advertising violations by teams. Fines typically range from $500 to $2,500 per violation, with repeat offenders facing license suspension.

## Broker Supervision Duties Under Maine Law

**The designated broker's supervisory obligation is not a passive one.** Under 32 M.R.S. § 13173 and MREC Rule Chapter 450, the broker must maintain active oversight of every transaction handled by team members. This includes reviewing contracts before execution when feasible, ensuring proper disclosure forms are delivered, and confirming that trust account procedures are followed.

Maine requires the use of specific disclosure forms in every residential transaction. The [Maine Real Estate Commission's Brokerage Relationships Disclosure Form](https://www.maine.gov/pfr/professionallicensing/professions/real-estate/forms) must be presented to all parties at first substantive contact. Team members often hand this form off without confirming the client understood it, which creates liability for the broker if a complaint is later filed.

The broker must also ensure that the Dual/Appointed Agency Consent Agreement is properly executed when a team represents both sides of a transaction—a situation that arises frequently within large teams where one member works with the buyer and another with the seller. Under 32 M.R.S. § 13175, appointed agency requires written informed consent from both parties, and the broker must personally approve the arrangement.

## Common Mistakes Maine Teams Make

The following errors appear repeatedly in MREC disciplinary actions and consent agreements. Each one is avoidable with proper training and oversight.

**First, team members acting outside their license level.** Associate brokers on a team sometimes delegate tasks to unlicensed assistants that constitute licensed activity under 32 M.R.S. § 13002. Answering client questions about pricing strategy, showing property independently, or presenting offers all require a license. When unlicensed staff perform these acts, both the individual and the supervising broker face disciplinary action.

Second, failing to deposit earnest money within the statutory timeframe. MREC Rule Chapter 450 requires that all trust funds be deposited into the brokerage trust account within a reasonable time, interpreted as no more than three business days after receipt. Team members who hold checks "until the broker is back in the office" or who deposit into personal accounts are violating trust account rules—one of the fastest paths to license revocation in Maine.

Third, operating under a team name that has not been registered with MREC. While Maine does not require a separate team license, the designated broker must notify MREC of all trade names and team names used in advertising. Failure to register creates an immediate advertising violation and can complicate E&O insurance claims if a transaction later goes sideways. Many agents overlook this step, assuming their team name is simply a marketing choice rather than a regulatory filing requirement.

Fourth, inconsistent disclosure practices across team members. One agent on the team might present the Brokerage Relationships Disclosure Form at the listing appointment while another waits until the offer stage. MREC expects consistency, and inconsistent timing creates grounds for consumer complaints alleging that informed consent was not properly obtained.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers who supervise teams in Maine should build audit protocols around the following categories. Random file reviews are insufficient—systematic checks produce defensible records if MREC ever opens an investigation.

| Audit Category | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | All active ads, social media, signs for proper brokerage name display | Monthly |
| Disclosure forms | Brokerage Relationships form signed and dated at first substantive contact | Every transaction |
| Trust account deposits | Earnest money deposited within 3 business days | Every transaction |
| Unlicensed activity | Task logs for assistants confirming no licensed activities performed | Quarterly |
| Team name registration | MREC notification on file for every team name in use | Annually |
| Appointed agency consent | Written consent from both parties when team handles both sides | Every applicable transaction |

**Brokers who fail to maintain these audits cannot claim ignorance as a defense.** MREC's disciplinary framework under 32 M.R.S. § 13195 allows the Commission to sanction a broker for "failure to supervise" even if the broker was unaware of the violation. The standard is whether the broker had reasonable systems in place to detect and prevent misconduct—not whether the broker had actual knowledge.

## Penalties and Consequences for Non-Compliance

MREC has broad enforcement authority under 32 M.R.S. § 13195. Penalties for team-related violations include monetary fines up to $5,000 per violation, mandatory continuing education beyond the standard 21-hour biennial requirement, license suspension for up to one year, and license revocation in cases of repeated or egregious conduct.

Beyond MREC action, agents face civil liability. A buyer who can demonstrate that a team's failure to properly disclose agency relationships caused them financial harm can pursue damages in Superior Court. The standard Purchase and Sale Agreement used in Maine (often based on the Maine Association of Realtors Form 102) includes representations about brokerage relationships—misrepresentation on that form opens the door to rescission of the entire contract.

Deal cancellation is also a real risk. If a seller or buyer discovers mid-transaction that the team violated advertising rules or failed to properly handle appointed agency disclosures, the aggrieved party may have grounds to void the agreement. This puts the team's commission at risk and exposes the brokerage to potential E&O claims.
