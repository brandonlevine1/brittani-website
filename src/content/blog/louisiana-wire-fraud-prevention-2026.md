## Louisiana Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules Have Teeth Now

Wire fraud losses in real estate topped $446 million nationally in 2024, and Louisiana was not spared. The Louisiana Real Estate Commission (LREC) has responded by tightening expectations around agent and broker conduct, making the Louisiana wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 something you cannot treat as optional paperwork. If you hold a license in this state, the obligation to warn, document, and verify is now baked into your fiduciary duties—and the consequences for skipping steps are severe.

This is not simply about handing someone a flyer. The requirements touch your purchase agreements, your communication protocols, and your broker's supervisory obligations. Getting this wrong does not just risk a fine. It risks your license, your client's life savings, and potentially your freedom under federal wire fraud statutes.

## The Statutory Framework Behind the Mandate

Louisiana's real estate licensing law, codified under [La. R.S. 37:1430 et seq.](https://www.legis.la.gov/legis/Law.aspx?d=95081), grants the LREC broad authority to define standards of practice and impose discipline for violations. The Commission's Rules and Regulations—particularly Chapter 31, Section 3101 through 3109—establish record-keeping and disclosure obligations that now explicitly include wire fraud prevention materials.

**The LREC treats failure to disclose wire fraud risks as a potential violation of La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(1), which prohibits conduct that demonstrates incompetency or dishonesty.** This is the same statute used for misrepresentation claims, so the disciplinary exposure is not trivial. Penalties range from formal reprimand to license revocation, plus fines of up to $5,000 per violation.

At the federal level, agents also face exposure under 18 U.S.C. Section 1343 if their negligence materially facilitates a fraudulent wire transfer. While criminal prosecution of agents remains rare, civil liability claims are climbing every year. Your errors and omissions policy likely contains a carve-out for failures to follow required disclosure protocols.

## What Forms Louisiana Agents Must Use

The LREC's prescribed Wire Fraud Advisory form is designated as Form WFA-1. This one-page document must be provided to buyers, sellers, and any party who may send or receive wired funds during the transaction. The form explains how business email compromise schemes operate, warns against trusting emailed wiring instructions without phone verification, and requires the party's signature and date.

Form WFA-1 should be presented no later than the signing of the purchase agreement. Many brokerages now include it in the initial disclosure packet alongside the Property Disclosure Document (Form PDD) and the Dual Agency/Designated Agency Disclosure (Form DAD). Timing matters—presenting it at closing is too late if a fraudulent wire has already been sent.

| Document | Form Number | When to Present | Who Signs |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Wire Fraud Advisory | WFA-1 | At or before purchase agreement | All parties |
| Property Disclosure | PDD | Before or at offer | Seller |
| Dual Agency Disclosure | DAD | Before representation begins | All parties |
| Lead Paint Disclosure | LBPD | Before offer on pre-1978 homes | Buyer and Seller |

Beyond WFA-1, some Louisiana brokerages have adopted supplemental cyber-security advisories. These are not LREC-mandated, but they can provide additional liability protection. If your brokerage uses a custom supplement, make sure it does not contradict or dilute the language in the official form.

## Louisiana Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Liability When Things Go Wrong

The worst-case scenario is not hypothetical. A Baton Rouge agent faced a civil lawsuit in 2024 after a buyer wired $387,000 to a spoofed account. The agent had emailed closing instructions without any verbal verification and had never provided the WFA-1 form. The buyer's attorney argued the agent breached fiduciary duty and violated LREC disclosure standards. The case settled for six figures, and the agent's license was suspended for twelve months.

**If you fail to provide Form WFA-1, the LREC can impose discipline even if no actual fraud occurred.** The form is a prophylactic requirement—its purpose is prevention, and the Commission does not need to wait for a loss to act. This mirrors how property condition disclosure violations work: the failure itself is the infraction, regardless of harm.

Your broker's E&O coverage may also be affected. Most policies now require adherence to state-mandated disclosure protocols as a condition of coverage. If your insurer can prove you skipped the wire fraud advisory, they may deny the claim entirely under the policy's "failure to comply with regulatory requirements" exclusion. This leaves you personally exposed to the full amount of the loss.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

The first and most frequent error is presenting WFA-1 at closing instead of at the purchase agreement stage. By the time parties reach the closing table, wiring instructions have typically already been sent and acted upon. The disclosure serves no protective function at that point—it becomes a checkbox exercise rather than a genuine warning.

The second mistake is emailing wiring instructions directly to clients. Even if you received the instructions from a legitimate title company, forwarding them via email creates another attack vector. Agents should direct clients to contact the title company by phone using a number they independently verify—never a number contained in an email.

**Third, agents routinely fail to document the verbal verification step.** Louisiana law does not explicitly require you to confirm that your client called the title company, but the LREC's investigative process gives weight to agents who can show they went beyond the minimum. A simple note in your transaction file—"Client confirmed wire instructions via phone call to ABC Title at [number] on [date]"—can be the difference between a clean file review and a disciplinary finding.

Fourth, some agents assume the title company handles everything and that their role ends with the disclosure form. The LREC's position is that agents have an ongoing duty to advise their clients of known risks throughout the transaction. If you become aware of suspicious communications or phishing attempts, you must immediately warn your client and document that warning.

Fifth, agents working with out-of-state buyers sometimes skip the form entirely, assuming it only applies to Louisiana residents. The form is transaction-based, not residency-based. If the property is in Louisiana, WFA-1 is required regardless of where the buyer lives.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under La. R.S. 37:1455(A)(24), which makes a broker responsible for the acts of their licensees when those acts result from inadequate supervision. This means if your agent skips the wire fraud advisory and a client loses money, the LREC can—and likely will—come after both the agent and the broker.

| Broker Obligation | Frequency | Documentation Required |
|-------------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Verify WFA-1 in every file | Per transaction | Signed, dated form in file |
| Audit agent email practices | Quarterly minimum | Policy acknowledgment on file |
| Train agents on wire fraud protocols | Annually (at minimum) | Attendance records, training materials |
| Review client communication logs | Per transaction or spot-check | Notes showing verification advisement |
| Retain all wire-related documents | 5 years post-closing | Physical or digital archive per Rule 3101 |

**Brokers should build wire fraud compliance into their transaction checklist as a non-negotiable milestone, not an optional add-on.** The file should not move to "clear to close" status without a signed WFA-1, and agents who repeatedly fail to include it should face internal corrective action before the LREC intervenes.

Quarterly audits are the minimum standard the Commission expects. During a random audit or complaint investigation, the LREC examiner will look for WFA-1 in the file. If it is missing, the burden shifts to the broker to explain why their supervisory systems failed to catch it. ## Practical Steps to Stay Compliant Every Transaction

Start by building the WFA-1 into your listing and buyer intake workflow. The moment a client signs a representation agreement, the wire fraud advisory should follow. Do not wait for an accepted offer.

Train yourself to never—under any circumstances—send wiring instructions via email, text, or social media message. If a client asks you to forward them, decline and explain why. Direct them to call the title company or closing attorney using a number from the company's official website or a business card obtained in person.

Keep a dedicated section in your transaction file for wire fraud documentation. This should contain the signed WFA-1, any notes about verbal verification conversations, and copies of any warnings you issued about suspicious communications. If your brokerage uses a digital transaction management platform, flag the wire fraud advisory as a required document that blocks file completion until uploaded.
