## Understanding New Hampshire Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

Real estate wire fraud cost American buyers and sellers over $446 million in 2023 according to FBI IC3 data, and New Hampshire has not been spared. The **New Hampshire wire fraud prevention requirements 2026** framework places specific obligations on licensed agents, their supervising brokers, and the closing attorneys who facilitate fund transfers. Failing to meet these obligations can result in license discipline, civil liability, and deals that collapse days before settlement.

New Hampshire operates as an attorney-closing state, which means the final wire verification responsibility technically rests with the closing attorney. That does not absolve agents. The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC), operating under RSA 331-A, holds licensees accountable for conduct that causes financial harm to consumers—including the failure to warn buyers about wire fraud schemes before those buyers ever receive closing instructions.

The 2026 environment is stricter than even two years ago. NHREC's updated guidance memo issued in January 2026 clarified that agents who transmit wire instructions via standard email—without encryption and without a prior written advisory—may be deemed in violation of RSA 331-A:26(II)(n), the catch-all provision covering dishonest or negligent conduct.

## The Legal Framework: RSA 331-A and NHREC Guidance

**New Hampshire does not have a single standalone wire fraud prevention statute like some states.** Instead, the duty arises from a combination of RSA 331-A (the real estate practice act), NHREC administrative rules (specifically Rea 700 governing trust accounts and consumer protection), and common law negligence principles applied by New Hampshire courts.

RSA 331-A:26 enumerates grounds for disciplinary action against licensees. Subsection II(n) addresses "any conduct which constitutes dishonest dealing" and subsection II(o) covers failure to exercise reasonable skill and care. NHREC has cited both provisions in informal guidance letters when addressing agent negligence related to wire fraud.

> "A licensee who transmits wiring instructions to a consumer without reasonable verification or who fails to provide written advisories regarding wire fraud risks may be subject to disciplinary proceedings under RSA 331-A:26." — NHREC Guidance Memo 2026-02

The [New Hampshire Real Estate Commission](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) published this memo alongside its 2026 license renewal cycle materials, signaling that enforcement attention is increasing.

## Required Forms and Disclosures for New Hampshire Agents

Most New Hampshire brokerages have adopted form NHREC-WFA (Wire Fraud Advisory) or a substantially similar proprietary disclosure drafted by their legal counsel. While NHREC has not mandated a single statewide form by rule number, the Commission's guidance makes clear that a written advisory must be delivered to all parties—buyers and sellers—prior to the point at which wire instructions are exchanged.

The typical New Hampshire wire fraud advisory includes three elements: a description of business email compromise schemes, explicit instructions to verify wire details by phone using a known number (not one from an email), and a statement that the brokerage will never change wire instructions via email. Agents should retain a signed copy in the transaction file.

**Beyond the advisory, agents should also be familiar with NHREC Form 2A (Agency Disclosure) and how it intersects with wire fraud duties.** Form 2A establishes the scope of an agent's obligations to their client. If your Form 2A promises to "protect the client's interests throughout the transaction," a court may interpret that promise to include warning the client about foreseeable wire fraud risks.

| Document | Purpose | When Delivered | Retained By |
|----------|---------|---------------|-------------|
| NHREC-WFA or equivalent | Written wire fraud advisory | At contract signing or before closing instructions sent | Agent file + closing attorney |
| NHREC Form 2A | Agency disclosure establishing duties | First substantive contact | Broker file |
| Closing attorney wire verification | Confirms account details by callback | 24-48 hours before closing | Closing attorney |
| Brokerage wire policy acknowledgment | Agent confirms training on internal procedures | Annually at policy review | Broker file |

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Liability, Fines, and Lost Deals

An agent who skips the wire fraud advisory or recklessly forwards unverified wire instructions faces consequences on multiple fronts. NHREC can impose discipline ranging from a formal reprimand to license suspension or revocation under RSA 331-A:28. Fines of up to $3,000 per violation are authorized under the statute.

Civil liability is the bigger financial exposure. New Hampshire courts apply a negligence standard: if a reasonably competent agent would have warned the client about wire fraud and you did not, you can be held liable for the client's full loss. Wire fraud losses in residential transactions typically range from $50,000 to $400,000—often the buyer's entire down payment.

**Your errors and omissions insurance may not cover you if you deviated from your brokerage's written policies.** Most E&O carriers include policy language requiring adherence to "established industry standards and company procedures." If your broker mandated the wire fraud advisory and you skipped it, the carrier has grounds to deny the claim. That leaves you personally exposed.

Deals also die. When a buyer's funds are diverted, the closing cannot proceed. Sellers may terminate the contract if the buyer cannot fund by the agreed date. In a market where inventory is tight and sellers have backup offers, one missed wire fraud disclosure can cost your client both their money and the property.

## Common Mistakes New Hampshire Agents Make

The first mistake is treating the wire fraud advisory as optional. Some agents assume that because the closing attorney handles funds, the advisory is the attorney's job. NHREC disagrees. The duty to warn attaches to the agent-client relationship, not the attorney-client relationship.

The second mistake is emailing wire instructions directly. Even when an agent receives legitimate instructions from the closing attorney, forwarding them via unencrypted email creates an interception risk. If those instructions are compromised after you forwarded them, the liability chain includes you. The correct practice is to direct clients to contact the closing attorney's office by phone to receive wire details verbally.

**The third mistake is failing to document delivery of the advisory.** An unsigned advisory sitting in your email outbox proves nothing. NHREC expects a signed acknowledgment, preferably with a date stamp, retained in the transaction file. Digital signatures through your transaction management platform satisfy this requirement.

The fourth mistake is using outdated advisory language. Wire fraud schemes evolve constantly. An advisory written in 2021 that only mentions email hacking may not cover newer tactics like deepfake voice calls or spoofed text messages. Review your form annually with your broker or legal counsel.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the seller side. Agents focus wire fraud warnings on buyers because buyers are wiring funds. But sellers also receive wire proceeds and can be targeted with fraudulent "updated deposit account" instructions. Both parties need the advisory. Understanding [where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) helps agents anticipate these gaps before they become liability events.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in New Hampshire carry independent liability under RSA 331-A:25-c for the acts of their affiliated licensees. A broker who fails to implement and enforce wire fraud policies can face discipline even if the broker personally did nothing wrong in a specific transaction.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Evidence Required |
|-------------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Wire fraud advisory in every closed file | Per-transaction review | Signed NHREC-WFA or equivalent |
| Annual agent training on wire fraud protocols | Annually | Attendance log with signatures |
| Policy manual includes wire fraud procedures | Annual update | Dated policy document |
| Email encryption or secure portal requirement | Ongoing | IT configuration records |
| Incident response plan for suspected fraud | Annual review | Written plan with contact list |

Brokers should conduct random file audits at least quarterly. Pull ten closed files and check for the signed advisory. If even one is missing, that represents a systemic gap—not an isolated oversight. The agent likely skipped it on multiple files.

**Training must go beyond a PowerPoint once a year.** Role-play scenarios where an agent receives a spoofed email with changed wire instructions. Test whether agents know to call the closing attorney on a verified number rather than replying to the email. Document everything. NHREC will ask for training records if a complaint is filed.

Brokers should also establish a clear escalation protocol. If an agent suspects a client has received fraudulent wire instructions, the brokerage needs a step-by-step response: contact the client immediately, notify the closing attorney, report to the FBI's IC3, and file a SAR if applicable. Hesitation costs money—banks can sometimes recover wired funds within 24-48 hours, but rarely after 72.

NHREC has signaled through its 2026 guidance memo that wire fraud prevention will be a focus area in upcoming audit cycles. The Commission is also considering whether to adopt a mandatory statewide form rather than relying on brokerage-specific versions—a move that would standardize expectations and simplify enforcement.
