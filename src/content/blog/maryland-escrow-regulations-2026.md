## Understanding Maryland Escrow Regulations 2026

Maryland escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for licensed agents and their supervising brokers. The state treats mishandling of escrow funds as one of the most serious violations a licensee can commit, and the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) has not softened its enforcement posture heading into this year. If you hold a Maryland license, the rules below are not optional reading—they are the scaffolding that keeps your license intact and your transactions from imploding.

The governing authority for escrow conduct sits in the Maryland Business Occupations and Professions Article, Title 17, and the administrative regulations codified under COMAR 09.11.02. Together, these statutes and regulations define who may hold deposits, when funds must be deposited, what records must be kept, and what penalties apply when agents get it wrong. MREC is the regulatory body responsible for enforcement, and it reports to the Maryland Department of Labor.

## Deposit Timing and Trust Account Rules

**The single most time-sensitive obligation an agent has after contract ratification is getting the earnest money deposit into the correct trust account.** Under COMAR 09.11.02.06, all deposit money received by a licensee in connection with a real estate transaction must be placed in a trust account within seven business days of the date a contract is ratified. "Ratification" in Maryland means the moment the last party signs the contract and that acceptance is communicated.

The trust account must be maintained in a federally insured financial institution located in Maryland. Brokers may not hold escrow funds in an out-of-state bank, and the account must be designated as a trust or escrow account that clearly separates client funds from the broker's operating funds. Interest-bearing accounts are permitted only with the written consent of all parties, and any interest earned belongs to the party or parties specified in the contract, not the brokerage.

Maryland uses the Residential Contract of Sale (sometimes referred to as the MAR Form 100 series by the Maryland Association of Realtors) to identify the escrow holder. Paragraph 6 of the standard MAR contract specifies who holds the deposit and under what conditions it will be disbursed. Agents must ensure this section is completed accurately—leaving it blank or ambiguous invites disputes that MREC may treat as a compliance failure.

## Key Forms and Contractual Provisions

The Maryland contract ecosystem has specific forms that intersect with escrow obligations. MAR Form 100 (Contract of Sale—Residential) and MAR Form 104 (Counter Offer) both contain provisions governing deposit amounts, holders, and release conditions. The Release of Contract form (MAR Form 180) is the document agents use when both parties agree to terminate and return the deposit.

| Form | Purpose | Escrow-Related Section |
|------|---------|----------------------|
| MAR Form 100 | Residential Contract of Sale | Paragraph 6 — Deposit |
| MAR Form 104 | Counter Offer | May modify deposit terms |
| MAR Form 180 | Release of Contract | Directs disposition of deposit funds |
| MAR Form 116 | Contract Addendum | Can add escrow-specific conditions |
| MREC Trust Account Record | Broker ledger requirement | Full transaction deposit history |

When a transaction goes sideways—buyer default, failed contingency, or mutual termination—agents need the Release of Contract signed by both parties before the escrow holder can disburse funds. Without it, the deposit sits frozen. Maryland does not have a statutory automatic-release mechanism; the escrow holder must either obtain mutual written consent or file an interpleader action in circuit court if the parties cannot agree.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of escrow violations in Maryland range from administrative fines to criminal prosecution, depending on severity. MREC has statutory authority under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-322 to impose penalties including license suspension, license revocation, and fines of up to $5,000 per violation. In cases involving conversion—using escrow funds for personal or business expenses—the violation may rise to criminal theft charges under Maryland Criminal Law Article Section 7-104.

**A single late deposit can trigger a formal complaint, an audit, and a consent order that stays on your license record permanently.** Even if no client was harmed, the commission views procedural violations as evidence of systemic compliance failures within the brokerage. The broker of record bears supervisory liability under COMAR 09.11.01.10, meaning a single agent's mistake can subject the entire brokerage to audit and discipline.

Deal cancellation is another practical consequence. When deposit disputes arise, buyers frequently exercise their right to cancel under contingency protections, and sellers may refuse to proceed if they learn funds were mishandled. Title companies and settlement attorneys in Maryland routinely refuse to close transactions where escrow irregularities appear in the file. The reputational damage alone can cost an agent referral business for years.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Maryland Escrow

Five errors appear repeatedly in MREC disciplinary actions and trust account audits. Each one is avoidable with basic systems and awareness.

**First: Holding checks instead of delivering them to the escrow holder.** Some agents keep a buyer's earnest money check in their desk drawer or car for days after ratification, intending to deliver it "when convenient." This violates the seven-business-day rule and, if the check is lost or damaged, creates potential liability for the agent personally.

Second: Failing to identify the escrow holder in the contract. Paragraph 6 of MAR Form 100 must be completed at the time of offer. Leaving it blank or writing "TBD" does not satisfy the requirement, and MREC auditors flag incomplete contracts during brokerage reviews. The escrow holder must be a specific licensed broker, title company, or attorney.

Third: Disbursing deposits without written authorization from all parties. Agents sometimes instruct their broker to release funds based on a verbal agreement or a single party's request. Maryland law requires mutual written consent via the Release of Contract form (MAR Form 180) or a court order. Unilateral release exposes the broker to liability for conversion.

Fourth: Commingling broker commissions with escrow funds before closing. Some brokerages deduct their commission from the escrow account before settlement, which is a direct violation of trust account regulations. All disbursements must occur through proper settlement procedures.

Fifth: Failing to disclose dual deposits. In transactions where both an initial deposit and an additional deposit are required, agents occasionally forget to document the second deposit's receipt and trust account entry, creating a gap in the ledger that triggers audit findings.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Maryland carry primary responsibility for trust account compliance under COMAR 09.11.02. The regulations require monthly reconciliation of all trust accounts, and MREC can demand records at any time without prior notice. A broker who cannot produce a complete ledger on demand faces immediate disciplinary risk.

| Audit Item | Frequency Required | Regulatory Basis |
|------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | COMAR 09.11.02.04 |
| Individual transaction ledger review | Per transaction, within 7 days of activity | COMAR 09.11.02.06 |
| Agent deposit delivery verification | Per transaction | COMAR 09.11.02.06 |
| Annual trust account report to MREC | Annually (if required by Commission) | Bus. Occ. & Prof. Art. 17-322 |

Brokers should implement a system that verifies deposit receipt within 48 hours of contract ratification—well inside the seven-day window. Waiting until day six to confirm whether an agent delivered the check is a recipe for violations. The verification system should document who received the check, when it was deposited, and the bank confirmation number.

Supervisory brokers also need to audit the completeness of Paragraph 6 on every contract their agents submit. A [strong checklist-based approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) eliminates the most common documentation gaps before they become compliance issues. The broker's office policy manual should include explicit procedures for escrow handling, and every agent should sign an acknowledgment annually confirming they understand the trust account rules.

MREC has increased its focus on proactive audits in recent years, particularly for brokerages with high transaction volumes. The [Maryland Real Estate Commission's official guidance](https://www.dllr.state.md.us/license/mrec/) provides additional resources on trust account requirements and complaint procedures.

## Dispute Resolution and Interpleader

When buyer and seller disagree about who gets the deposit after a failed transaction, Maryland agents face a procedural trap. The escrow holder cannot simply pick a side. Without a signed MAR Form 180 from both parties, the deposit remains frozen in the trust account indefinitely—unless the holder files an interpleader action in circuit court.

Interpleader allows the escrow holder to deposit the disputed funds with the court and ask a judge to decide who receives them. The cost of filing is borne initially by the escrow holder but is typically recoverable from the losing party. Brokers who fail to interplead disputed funds within a reasonable time (Maryland courts have not defined a specific deadline, but 30-60 days after demand from both parties is the practical standard) risk complaints from both sides.

Agents should understand that their role in a dispute is limited. Once a transaction fails and the parties disagree, the agent's job is to direct both parties to communicate in writing with the escrow holder and to avoid making promises about fund release. Agents who tell a buyer "don't worry, you'll get your deposit back" without a signed release are creating liability for themselves and their broker.
