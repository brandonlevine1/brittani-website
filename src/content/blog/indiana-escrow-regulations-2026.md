## Understanding Indiana Escrow Regulations 2026

The rules governing escrow and trust accounts in Indiana are not optional guidelines—they are enforceable regulations backed by fines, license revocation, and civil liability. Indiana escrow regulations 2026 remain rooted in Indiana Code Title 25, Article 34.1, and the administrative rules found in 876 IAC 8, but recent enforcement patterns from the Indiana Real Estate Commission (IREC) have tightened scrutiny on deposit timing, record-keeping, and disbursement disputes. Agents and brokers who ignore the details risk more than a slap on the wrist.

**Every dollar of earnest money that touches a broker's trust account is governed by specific statutory deadlines and documentation requirements.** The IREC has made clear in its 2025 and 2026 disciplinary actions that ignorance of trust account rules is not a defense. This post breaks down the statutes, the forms, the mistakes, and the broker-level auditing obligations that define compliant escrow handling in the state.

## The Statutory Framework: IC 25-34.1 and 876 IAC 8

Indiana's escrow obligations originate in [Indiana Code 25-34.1-10](https://iga.in.gov/laws/2024/ic/titles/25#25-34.1-10), which establishes the duties of brokers regarding trust funds. Section 10-10 specifically prohibits commingling personal funds with client trust funds, with a narrow exception allowing brokers to keep up to $500 of their own money in the account to cover bank fees. Section 10-11 addresses the improper conversion of trust funds and classifies it as grounds for license discipline.

The administrative rules in 876 IAC 8-3 add operational specifics. Rule 876 IAC 8-3-2 mandates that all earnest money received by a licensee must be deposited into the broker's trust account no later than the close of the next banking day following mutual acceptance of the purchase agreement. Rule 876 IAC 8-3-4 requires that every trust account be maintained at an Indiana-chartered bank or federally insured institution located within the state.

These are not soft deadlines. The IREC's enforcement arm treats a single missed deposit deadline as a standalone violation, regardless of whether the funds were eventually deposited or whether the transaction closed successfully.

## Key Forms and Documentation Indiana Agents Must Use

Indiana does not mandate a single statewide purchase agreement form the way some states do, but the Indiana Association of REALTORS (IAR) publishes standardized forms that most brokerages adopt. The IAR Purchase Agreement (Form 10.1) contains specific escrow-related provisions in Section 4, including the earnest money amount, the entity holding funds, and the timeline for deposit. Agents should also be familiar with the IAR Earnest Money Dispute and Release Form (Form 50.1), which is used when buyer and seller disagree on disbursement.

| Form | Purpose | When Used |
|------|---------|-----------|
| IAR Form 10.1 | Purchase Agreement (escrow terms in Section 4) | At offer |
| IAR Form 50.1 | Earnest Money Dispute and Release | When disbursement is contested |
| IAR Form 13.1 | Amendment to Purchase Agreement | Changing escrow holder or amount |
| Broker Trust Account Ledger | Individual transaction accounting | Throughout the transaction |
| IREC Trust Account Reconciliation | Monthly balance verification | Monthly by broker |

**The trust account ledger is not optional—it is an auditable document that the IREC can demand at any time without prior notice.** Brokers must maintain individual transaction ledgers showing dates of deposit, the source of funds, the amount, and the date and payee of any disbursement. A running balance must be reconcilable to the bank statement at all times.

Agents should also note that Indiana's interpleader process (governed by Indiana Trial Rule 22) applies when escrow disputes cannot be resolved through Form 50.1. The broker may file an interpleader action in circuit or superior court, depositing the contested funds with the court and requesting discharge from liability.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, Suspension, and Civil Exposure

The IREC has statutory authority under IC 25-34.1-6-11 to impose discipline ranging from a formal reprimand to permanent license revocation. The commission can also assess civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation. In practice, escrow violations appear in roughly one-third of all IREC disciplinary orders published since 2024.

Here is what a compliance failure actually looks like in dollars and career damage:

| Violation | Typical IREC Penalty | Additional Exposure |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Late deposit (1-3 days) | Letter of reprimand, $1,000-$2,500 fine | Buyer's breach claim |
| Commingling funds | Suspension (30-180 days), $5,000+ fine | Civil conversion claim |
| Unauthorized disbursement | Revocation proceedings, $10,000 fine | Lawsuit from aggrieved party |
| Failure to maintain ledger | Probation, mandatory education | Audit triggering further findings |
| Conversion of trust funds | Revocation, criminal referral | Felony theft charges (IC 35-43-4) |

Beyond IREC discipline, an agent who mishandles escrow funds exposes their brokerage to civil liability. Buyers and sellers can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, and courts routinely award consequential damages when a botched escrow causes a deal to collapse—think lost inspection costs, appraisal fees, or the price difference when a buyer must purchase a more expensive property after the original deal falls apart.

The distinction between a late deposit and outright conversion matters enormously. Late deposits typically result in fines and education requirements. Conversion—using trust funds for personal purposes—triggers criminal referral to the county prosecutor under Indiana's theft statute, IC 35-43-4, which classifies theft of $750 or more as a Level 6 felony.

## Common Mistakes Indiana Agents Make With Escrow

Five recurring errors show up in IREC disciplinary actions and brokerage audits year after year. None of them require bad intent—just inattention.

**First, agents hold checks "pending inspection" instead of depositing them immediately.** The statute is unambiguous: deposit by end of next banking day after mutual acceptance. There is no exception for pending inspections, upcoming contingency deadlines, or verbal agreements with the other side to "hold off." If the purchase agreement is signed by both parties, the clock starts.

Second, agents fail to specify the escrow holder in the purchase agreement. Section 4 of IAR Form 10.1 requires identification of the party holding funds. When this field is left blank or ambiguous, disputes arise about whether the listing broker, selling broker, or title company is responsible. The IREC has disciplined agents for this omission alone, reasoning that it creates confusion about fiduciary accountability.

Third, agents disburse earnest money without written authorization from all parties. Indiana law requires mutual written consent for release of trust funds unless a court orders otherwise. An agent who releases funds based on a phone call from one party—even when that party appears to have the contractual right—faces discipline if the other party later objects. The proper path is Form 50.1 signed by both parties, or interpleader.

Fourth, brokers fail to reconcile trust accounts monthly. The IREC expects a bank-statement-to-ledger reconciliation performed within 10 days of the statement date. Many smaller brokerages treat this as a quarterly task or delegate it to unlicensed staff without oversight. When an audit reveals a discrepancy—even a small one—the IREC treats it as a systemic compliance failure.

Fifth, agents accept earnest money made payable to themselves rather than the brokerage trust account. Checks must be made payable to the broker's trust account or the designated escrow holder. A check payable to an individual agent cannot be properly deposited into the trust account without endorsement irregularities, and the practice itself signals potential commingling.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry the ultimate liability for trust account compliance in Indiana. IC 25-34.1-10-10.5 assigns supervisory responsibility to the broker, meaning that an agent's escrow violation is simultaneously a broker's supervision failure. The IREC regularly disciplines brokers and agents in tandem for the same underlying conduct.

**Brokers should implement a monthly trust account audit that goes beyond simple reconciliation.** The audit should verify that every open escrow entry corresponds to an active transaction file, that no deposit sat unreconciled for more than one statement cycle, and that every disbursement has a signed authorization (Form 50.1 or closing statement) in the file.

A functional broker audit program includes three layers. First, same-day verification that earnest money received by any agent was deposited within the statutory window. Many brokerages now require agents to photograph deposit slips and upload them to [transaction management systems](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) within 24 hours. Second, monthly reconciliation of the trust account ledger against the bank statement, documented with the broker's signature and date. Third, quarterly review of all pending escrow entries to confirm that no stale funds remain in the account without an active contract or court order.

Brokers who rely on agents to self-report deposits are setting themselves up for disciplinary action. The IREC's position is that delegation of tasks is permissible, but delegation of responsibility is not. ## Indiana Escrow Regulations 2026: Dispute Resolution and Interpleader

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the earnest money, Indiana brokers face a specific procedural obligation. The broker cannot simply pick a side. Under 876 IAC 8-3-5, the broker must continue to hold the disputed funds until receiving mutual written release (Form 50.1), a final court order, or a reasonable time has passed for the broker to initiate interpleader.

The interpleader process under Indiana Trial Rule 22 allows the broker to deposit funds with the court, name both buyer and seller as defendants, and petition for discharge from further liability. Filing fees are modest—typically under $200 in Indiana circuit court—and the broker can deduct reasonable attorney fees from the deposit if the purchase agreement authorizes it. Many agents are surprised to learn that IAR Form 10.1, Section 4(E), does contain language authorizing the escrow holder to recover costs of interpleader from the deposited funds.

Brokers should not wait indefinitely before filing interpleader. The IREC has issued guidance suggesting that holding disputed funds beyond 90 days without resolution or court filing may itself constitute a trust account violation. The rationale is that indefinite retention creates risk of loss, bank errors, or confusion when the broker changes institutions.

For agents who want to reduce the odds of a dispute reaching interpleader, clear contract drafting is the best prevention. Specify exactly which contingency failures trigger automatic return of earnest money, reference specific paragraph numbers, and avoid handwritten addenda that contradict printed form language. If you are managing [deadlines and contingencies](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) across multiple transactions simultaneously, systematized tracking prevents the kind of missed communication that escalates into formal disputes.

## Staying Ahead of IREC Enforcement Trends

The IREC publishes disciplinary orders on its website and in its quarterly newsletter. Reviewing these orders is the fastest way to understand what the commission is actually enforcing versus what sits dormant in the code. In 2025, the commission increased enforcement activity around two specific areas: late deposits by agents working remotely (where physical check handling introduced delays) and inadequate broker supervision of team leaders who handled escrow on behalf of multiple agents.

Remote transactions have created a timing problem that did not exist when agents physically carried checks to the office. If a buyer's agent receives a check at a showing on Friday afternoon and does not reach the brokerage until Monday, the statutory deadline has already passed. The solution—and the one the IREC expects—is either immediate delivery to the broker or use of electronic payment methods that eliminate the physical custody gap. Some brokerages now require wire or ACH for all earnest money deposits exceeding $5,000, specifically to avoid the [paperwork and timing failures](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) that trigger violations.

**Indiana escrow regulations 2026 demand precision, not perfection—but the margin for error is thinner than most agents realize.** The difference between a clean file and a disciplinary complaint often comes down to one missed day, one unsigned form, or one broker who assumed their agents were handling deposits correctly without verification. Agents who build deposit-day documentation habits and brokers who audit aggressively will avoid the fines, lawsuits, and license actions that continue to fill the IREC's published orders.
