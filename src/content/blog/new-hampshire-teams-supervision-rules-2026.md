The regulatory framework behind **New Hampshire teams & supervision rules 2026** has sharpened considerably over the past two years. The NH Real Estate Commission, operating under RSA 331-A and the administrative rules in Chapter Rea 300, now expects brokers and team leaders to demonstrate clear supervisory structures, proper advertising disclosure, and documented oversight of every licensee operating under a team umbrella. If you run a team—or plan to join one—ignorance of these requirements is expensive.

## How New Hampshire Defines a Real Estate Team

New Hampshire does not issue a separate "team license." A team is simply a marketing arrangement within a brokerage where two or more licensees collaborate and advertise under a shared name. The legal entity remains the brokerage itself, not the team.

RSA 331-A:2 defines "broker" and "salesperson" but is silent on "team" as a distinct licensure category. That silence is the trap. Because no separate license exists, every obligation that attaches to the brokerage attaches equally to any sub-group operating within it. The Commission's administrative rules in Rea 303 fill the gap by imposing advertising and supervision requirements that functionally regulate team behavior.

**The designated broker—not the team leader—is the legally responsible party for all team activities.** This is the single most misunderstood point among New Hampshire agents forming teams. A team leader can coordinate workflows, but the supervisory duty never transfers away from the broker.

## Advertising and Team Name Requirements Under Rea 303

Rea 303.02 governs advertising by licensees and demands that every advertisement include the name of the brokerage office "in a clear and conspicuous manner." A team name cannot appear more prominently than the brokerage name in any marketing material—print, digital, signage, or social media.

| Requirement | What Rea 303.02 Mandates | Common Violation |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name prominence | Must be equal to or larger than team name | Team logo dominates; brokerage in fine print |
| Licensed name usage | Agents must use their name as it appears on their license | Nicknames or abbreviations without Commission approval |
| Contact information | Brokerage phone or address must appear | Only team cell number listed |
| Digital ads | Same rules apply online as in print | Social media profiles omit brokerage entirely |
| Team name wording | Cannot imply the team is an independent firm | "Smith Realty Group" without brokerage context |

Violations under this section trigger RSA 331-A:26, which grants the Commission authority to impose administrative fines up to $2,000 per occurrence and to initiate license suspension proceedings. In 2025, the Commission publicly disciplined four licensees for team-advertising violations—each case involved a team name that implied independent brokerage status.

## Supervision Duties: What RSA 331-A Demands from Brokers

RSA 331-A:16 establishes the broker's supervisory obligation. The statute requires that a designated broker "directly supervise the activities" of every salesperson and associate broker affiliated with the office. When a team operates within that office, the broker's duty doesn't dilute—it multiplies.

The Commission's interpretive guidance, updated in early 2026, specifies that "direct supervision" means the broker must have systems in place to review transactions in progress, not merely audit closed files after the fact. Retrospective review alone is insufficient. Brokers must demonstrate contemporaneous awareness of team transactions, which usually means access to transaction management systems, regular file reviews, and documented check-ins.

**Delegation of day-to-day coordination to a team leader is permitted, but delegation of supervisory responsibility is not.** The broker may allow a team leader to assign tasks, run meetings, and manage client intake. However, the broker must still personally review or have a documented protocol for reviewing all contracts, disclosures, and client communications generated by team members.

This is where many brokerages operating in [states with similar structures](/blog/wisconsin-teams-supervision-rules-2026) differ from New Hampshire. Wisconsin, for example, has explicit statutory recognition of teams. New Hampshire forces teams into the existing broker-salesperson supervision framework without separate accommodation.

## Forms and Documentation Teams Must Use

New Hampshire's required disclosure forms carry specific obligations that teams need to handle carefully. The primary form is the **Agency Disclosure Form (NH-AD)**, which must be presented to buyers and sellers at first substantive contact. When a team is involved, the form must clearly identify which licensee within the team is the designated agent for that client.

The **Dual Agency Consent Form (NH-DAC)** becomes a frequent compliance flashpoint for teams. When two members of the same team represent opposite sides of a transaction, dual agency arises at the brokerage level even if the agents themselves believe they are acting independently. RSA 331-A:25-c requires informed written consent before dual agency can proceed.

> "A licensee shall not act as a dual agent without the informed written consent of all parties to the transaction." — RSA 331-A:25-c

Teams must also maintain proper records under Rea 304.01, which requires that transaction files be retained for three years and be available for Commission inspection. Each team member's role in the transaction—who sourced the lead, who conducted showings, who drafted the offer—should be documented in the file.

For teams handling trust account deposits, the brokerage's escrow account is the only permissible repository. Teams cannot maintain separate trust accounts. All earnest money must flow through the brokerage's account as governed by [standard escrow regulations](/blog/wisconsin-escrow-regulations-2026) and RSA 331-A:18.

## Common Mistakes That Trigger Commission Action

**First: Allowing unlicensed assistants to perform licensed activities.** Team assistants frequently cross the line from administrative support into substantive real estate activity—discussing pricing with clients, explaining contract terms, or conducting showings solo. RSA 331-A:1 defines licensed activity broadly, and the Commission has consistently interpreted client-facing discussions about property value or contract terms as requiring licensure.

Second: Failing to disclose the supervisory structure to clients. Buyers and sellers are entitled to know who their designated agent is and who supervises that agent. When a team obscures this relationship behind a brand name, the client cannot exercise informed consent regarding agency. This violates RSA 331-A:25-a's disclosure requirements.

Third: Team leaders signing documents without proper authority. Unless a team leader holds a broker's license, they cannot sign listing agreements or buyer agency agreements on behalf of the brokerage. Only the designated broker or a properly authorized associate broker can bind the firm. Team leaders who are licensed as salespersons exceed their authority when they execute agreements in the brokerage's name.

Fourth: Inconsistent commission split documentation. The Commission has investigated complaints where team members disputed their compensation, only to find no written agreement existed between the team, the members, and the brokerage. While the Commission does not regulate compensation amounts, the absence of written agreements creates liability and often reveals that the broker lacked awareness of the team's internal structure.

Fifth: Operating across multiple offices without proper notification. Some NH teams have members physically located in different branch offices. Rea 302.04 requires that each office be separately registered and that agents be assigned to a specific office. A team member cannot float between offices without the Commission being notified of their primary office of affiliation.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers who sponsor teams should build audit protocols around five categories. Transaction file completeness is the baseline—every file should contain the signed agency disclosure, all contracts, addenda, and communication logs. The Commission's investigators check these files during routine audits, and gaps create liability.

| Audit Category | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | All team marketing materials include brokerage name prominently | Monthly |
| Agency disclosures | NH-AD and NH-DAC forms properly completed in every file | Per transaction |
| Unlicensed activity | Assistants are not performing licensed activities | Quarterly review |
| Trust account handling | All earnest money deposited in brokerage escrow account | Per deposit |
| Written team agreements | Compensation splits and roles documented | Annually or at team formation |
| Office affiliation records | Each team member assigned to correct registered office | At onboarding |

**Brokers should also audit their team's online presence at least monthly.** Social media profiles, Zillow accounts, and personal websites are the most common sources of advertising violations. The Commission has stated publicly that it monitors online advertising and accepts anonymous complaints.

The [NH Real Estate Commission](https://www.nh.gov/nhrec/) posts disciplinary actions on its website, and a review of recent orders reveals that team-related violations have increased steadily since 2024. Brokers who cannot demonstrate proactive supervision face not only regulatory penalties but also vicarious liability in civil lawsuits arising from their agents' conduct.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance: Fines, Suspension, and Deal Risk

The penalty structure under RSA 331-A:26 gives the Commission broad discretion. Fines can reach $2,000 per violation, and in cases involving pattern conduct, the Commission may stack penalties across multiple transactions. License suspension is on the table for repeat offenders or cases involving consumer harm.

Beyond regulatory penalties, non-compliant team structures create civil exposure. If a buyer discovers that their "agent" was actually an unlicensed assistant, or that dual agency was never properly disclosed, the affected party can seek rescission of the contract. Courts in New Hampshire have recognized failure to disclose material agency relationships as grounds for voiding a purchase agreement.

Errors and omissions insurance may also decline coverage when the underlying conduct involves a regulatory violation. Carriers frequently include exclusions for intentional acts or knowing violations of licensing law. A team that knowingly deploys unlicensed personnel or advertises deceptively may find itself without insurance protection precisely when it is most needed.

## Building Compliant Team Structures Moving Forward

The path to compliance is not ambiguous—it just requires discipline. Written team agreements should define roles, compensation, supervisory protocols, and advertising standards before the team goes to market. The designated broker should sign off on the agreement and retain a copy in the office files.
