## Vermont's Unusual Closing Framework — And Why It Matters

The question of Vermont attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up even experienced agents who have closed dozens of deals in the state. Vermont occupies a gray zone that creates real liability exposure for agents who assume the rules work the same way they do in neighboring New Hampshire or New York. Understanding the distinction between what a title company can do and what only a Vermont-licensed attorney may do is the difference between a clean closing and a transaction that blows up weeks after recording.

Vermont is considered an "attorney closing" state by industry convention, though no single statute uses that exact phrase. The practical effect is the same: certain closing functions are reserved exclusively for attorneys, and agents who facilitate workarounds risk disciplinary action from the [Vermont Real Estate Commission](https://sos.vermont.gov/real-estate-commission/) and potential civil liability.

This post breaks down the specific rules, the forms you are already using, and the mistakes that get agents and brokers into trouble.


## What Vermont Law Actually Says About Closing Authority

Vermont does not have a statute that reads "an attorney must be present at every real estate closing." Instead, the restriction comes from the Vermont Supreme Court's authority over the practice of law, codified through Administrative Order 42 and enforced by the Professional Responsibility Board. Title examination, deed preparation, mortgage document drafting, and the disbursement of escrow funds have all been treated as legal practice under Vermont case law.

The key regulatory body here is the Vermont Board of Bar Examiners, which has issued advisory opinions clarifying that non-attorneys — including title company employees and real estate agents — may not prepare legal instruments for real estate transactions. This includes warranty deeds, quitclaim deeds, and settlement statements when those documents involve legal judgment rather than mere data entry.

Title 27, Chapter 5 of the Vermont Statutes governs conveyances and requires that deeds be properly executed and recorded in the town clerk's office. While the statute does not specify who drafts the deed, the unauthorized practice of law framework fills that gap. Agents who refer clients to a title-only closing without attorney involvement are walking into a compliance minefield.


## Title Companies vs. Attorneys: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

**The confusion for agents usually starts with not knowing where the title company's role ends and the attorney's role begins.** The following table clarifies the division of responsibilities as practiced in Vermont.

| Closing Function | Title Company | Vermont Attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Title search and examination | May order search; cannot render title opinion | Examines title and issues opinion of title |
| Title insurance issuance | Yes — underwriting and policy issuance | May also issue as title agent |
| Deed preparation | No — constitutes practice of law | Yes — exclusive authority |
| Mortgage document review | Limited to lender-prepared docs | Full review and preparation |
| Escrow/disbursement | Only under attorney supervision in most cases | Yes — full authority |
| Closing table facilitation | Not standard in Vermont | Typically conducts closing |

This division matters because Vermont's framework differs sharply from states like Wisconsin, where title companies routinely run closings without mandatory attorney involvement. If you have worked in a [title-company-centered closing state](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026), you need to reset your assumptions for Vermont transactions.

A title company in Vermont serves a vital role — issuing title insurance policies, coordinating with lenders, and processing the administrative side of the transaction. But the moment the work requires legal judgment, the attorney must step in. There is no workaround, and "we've always done it this way" is not a defense.


## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Vermont real estate transactions rely on forms published by the Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) and, for some transactions, attorney-drafted custom agreements. The standard Purchase and Sale Agreement form (VAR Form 200) includes provisions that presume attorney involvement, including an attorney review period that gives both buyer and seller the right to have their attorneys review and modify the contract within a specified timeframe.

**Agents should pay close attention to the attorney review clause in VAR Form 200 because it is not merely optional — waiving it exposes your client and you.** If a client declines attorney review, document that decision in writing. The Property Disclosure Form (VAR Form 224) is the agent's responsibility to ensure is completed, but any legal questions arising from disclosures should be directed to the client's attorney, not answered by the agent.

The Settlement Statement, whether using the ALTA form or a lender-specific version, must be reviewed by the closing attorney. Agents who attempt to "pre-fill" settlement figures or adjust line items without attorney input risk crossing into unauthorized practice territory. Your job is to provide the data — commission splits, agreed-upon credits, repair allowances — and let the attorney or the attorney-supervised title company build the final document.

For transactions involving land, new construction, or properties with complex title histories, attorneys may also prepare Affidavits of Title, boundary line agreements, and easement documents. None of these fall within an agent's scope.


## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Vermont Closings

The first and most frequent error is recommending a closing process that omits attorney involvement. This happens most often with agents who relocated from attorney-optional states or who are working with out-of-state buyers accustomed to title-company-only closings. When you tell a buyer "you don't really need an attorney," you are giving legal advice — which is itself a violation of your scope as a licensee.

The second mistake is preparing or modifying deed language. Even small changes to a quitclaim or warranty deed — correcting a name spelling, adding a trust reference, adjusting the legal description — constitute the practice of law. Agents who make these edits, even with good intentions, expose themselves to malpractice claims and Vermont Real Estate Commission discipline under 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41, which governs real estate licensing.

**The third mistake is mishandling earnest money deposits by routing them through non-compliant escrow arrangements.** Vermont requires that earnest money be held in a designated escrow account, and disbursement at closing must follow the terms of the purchase agreement as interpreted by the closing attorney. Agents who release funds early, redirect deposits, or commingle escrow with operating funds face license suspension and civil liability. This parallels [earnest money risks in other states](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026), but Vermont's attorney-centric framework adds an extra layer of oversight.

The fourth mistake is failing to calendar the attorney review deadline from VAR Form 200. If neither party's attorney objects within the review window, the contract becomes binding as written. Agents who miss this deadline — or who fail to inform clients about it — may find their clients locked into terms that an attorney would have modified. This is a [deadline management failure](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that costs agents referrals and repeat business even when it does not kill the deal outright.

The fifth mistake is treating the title company as a substitute for the closing attorney. Agents sometimes assume that because a title company is "handling the closing," attorney obligations have been satisfied. In Vermont, the title company's involvement does not eliminate the need for an attorney to perform the functions reserved to legal practitioners. Confirm — in writing — who is serving as the closing attorney before you reach the seven-day pre-closing window.


## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Vermont carry supervisory liability under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296, which requires that supervising brokers ensure their agents comply with all applicable laws and regulations. This is not a suggestion. If an agent under your supervision facilitates a closing without proper attorney involvement, the Commission can hold you jointly responsible.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Attorney review clause | VAR Form 200 includes clause; client signed acknowledgment | Every transaction |
| Closing attorney confirmation | Written confirmation of attorney name and contact | 10+ days before closing |
| Earnest money escrow | Deposit held in compliant account; disbursement authorized by attorney | At deposit and at closing |
| Deed preparation | Attorney prepared or reviewed all conveyance documents | Before recording |
| Settlement statement review | Attorney sign-off on final ALTA or lender statement | Day of closing |

**Brokers should build a quarterly file audit into their compliance calendar.** Pull five to ten random transaction files and verify that each one contains documented attorney involvement at the closing stage. If you find gaps, address them immediately — a pattern of non-compliance can trigger a Commission investigation that affects your entire brokerage, not just the agent who cut corners.

Brokers also need to enforce training on the distinction between administrative tasks agents may perform and legal functions they may not. Annual training sessions that cover Vermont's unauthorized practice of law standards are not optional overhead — they are liability insurance that costs you nothing but an afternoon.


## Liability, Fines, and Deal Cancellation Risks

When an agent facilitates a closing that bypasses attorney requirements, the consequences cascade. The Vermont Real Estate Commission has authority under 26 V.S.A. Section 2299 to impose fines, suspend licenses, or revoke licenses for violations of the licensing statute and its associated rules. A single violation can result in a fine of up to $1,000 per occurrence, with repeat violations escalating to suspension.

Beyond Commission discipline, agents face civil liability. If a buyer later discovers a title defect that an attorney would have caught — an unreleased lien, a boundary dispute, a defective deed — the agent who steered the client away from attorney involvement becomes a target in litigation. Vermont courts have not been sympathetic to agents who argue they were "just following the client's wishes" when the agent's own conduct enabled the non-compliant closing.

Deal cancellation is the third risk. Lenders increasingly require attorney closing letters before funding, and a transaction that reaches the closing table without one may simply not fund. The deal dies, your commission evaporates, and your client blames you — correctly — for the failure.


## How to Structure Every Vermont Closing for Compliance

Start every transaction by confirming attorney involvement in writing at the contract stage. When you present VAR Form 200, walk your client through the attorney review clause and explain why it exists. If the client is a first-time buyer from out of state, they may not understand that Vermont treats this differently from where they last purchased property.

Build your pre-closing checklist to include a line item for "closing attorney confirmed" no later than ten days before the scheduled closing date.
