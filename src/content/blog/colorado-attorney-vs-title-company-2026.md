## Colorado Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Definitive Answer

The question of Colorado attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 confuses agents every single year, and it continues to generate liability exposure for those who guess wrong. Colorado is not an attorney-state. The settlement table does not require a licensed lawyer to be seated at it for the transaction to be valid.

That does not mean attorneys are irrelevant, and it does not mean title companies operate without constraints. Understanding who does what — and who must be present — is the difference between a clean closing and a complaint filed with the Colorado Division of Real Estate (DORA).

## What Colorado Law Actually Requires at the Closing Table

**Colorado is classified as a "title company state," meaning a title insurance company or its authorized agent may conduct settlement without an attorney present.** The governing framework lives in the Colorado Revised Statutes at C.R.S. 10-11-101 through 10-11-123, which regulate title insurance entities, and C.R.S. 38-35-101 et seq., which governs recording and conveyance. Neither statute requires attorney attendance.

The Colorado Real Estate Commission (CREC) reinforces this in Commission Rule E-1, which prohibits licensees from engaging in the unauthorized practice of law but acknowledges that closings proceed through title companies routinely. The practical outcome: a title company closer — sometimes called a settlement agent or escrow officer — handles document execution, fund disbursement, and recording instructions.

An attorney becomes mandatory only in specific scenarios. If the transaction involves a complex estate, contested title, foreclosure-related deficiency, or court-ordered sale, the parties will typically need counsel. But for a standard residential purchase governed by the Commission-approved Contract to Buy and Sell Real Estate (CBS forms 1 through 5), no statute demands a lawyer in the room.

## The Role of the Title Company in Colorado Closings

Title companies in Colorado perform three distinct functions that agents sometimes blur together: title examination, title insurance underwriting, and settlement services. The closing itself falls under settlement services, and the company acts as a neutral third party holding funds in escrow until all conditions are satisfied.

Under [DORA's Title Insurance Division](https://doi.colorado.gov/insurance-products/title-insurance), title entities must be licensed and bonded. The closer who sits across from your buyer is typically a notary public employed by the title company, authorized to witness signatures and certify document execution. They are not acting as an advocate for either party.

**Agents must understand that the title closer cannot explain legal terms, advise on contract provisions, or interpret ambiguous language in the deed.** If your client asks the closer "what does this indemnification clause mean?" the proper answer is a referral to their own attorney. The closer facilitates; they do not counsel.

## When an Attorney Should Be Involved (Even Though It Is Not Required)

Colorado's lack of an attorney mandate does not eliminate the need for legal counsel in certain transactions. The Commission's CBS Form 1 (Residential), CBS Form 4 (Income/Residential), and CBS Form 5 (Commercial) all contemplate the possibility that parties may engage attorneys, and Section 29 of CBS Form 1 specifically provides an attorney-review termination option when the box is checked.

Situations that call for attorney involvement include title held in trust, transactions involving boundary disputes or easement negotiations, 1031 exchange structuring, and any deal where the seller's title commitment reveals exceptions that a standard endorsement cannot cure. Agents who try to resolve these issues themselves wander into unauthorized practice of law territory — a violation of C.R.S. 12-10-601.

A useful mental framework: if the issue requires interpreting law rather than filling in blanks on an approved Commission form, your client needs a lawyer, not your opinion.

Some Colorado attorneys also hold title insurance agent licenses, meaning they can simultaneously represent a client and issue title insurance through an underwriter. This dual role is legal but creates a disclosure obligation — the attorney must inform the client of any potential conflict under Colorado Rule of Professional Conduct 1.7.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Around Closing Representation

**The first and most damaging error is agents drafting addenda or legal documents outside of Commission-approved forms.** CREC Rule E-44 explicitly limits licensees to completing approved Commission forms and requires that any non-standard provision be drafted by an attorney. Agents who type up their own "hold harmless" language or "as-is" riders on blank paper expose themselves to DORA discipline and civil liability.

The second mistake is advising clients that they do not need an attorney when the transaction clearly demands one. If your buyer is purchasing from an estate and the personal representative has not been appointed by the court yet, telling them "the title company will handle it" is negligent. The title company will issue a requirement on the commitment, and if nobody engages counsel, the deal stalls or collapses at closing.

Third, agents routinely fail to disclose their affiliated business arrangements with title companies. RESPA Section 8 and Colorado's [C.R.S. 10-11-122](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/olls/crs2023-title-10.pdf) prohibit kickbacks and unearned fees. If your brokerage has an ownership interest in a title company, the Affiliated Business Arrangement (AfBA) disclosure must be provided at or before the time of referral — not at closing. Missing this step triggers potential federal and state penalties.

Fourth, agents sometimes schedule closings without confirming the title commitment has been delivered to the buyer within the deadline specified in CBS Form 1, Section 8. The standard deadline is calendar days from MEC (mutual execution of contract), and if the buyer's objection deadline passes without receipt, the contract may become voidable depending on the notice provisions.

Fifth, agents occasionally tell buyers they cannot choose their own title company. Under RESPA Section 9, a seller cannot mandate a buyer use a specific title insurer as a condition of the transaction. Agents who parrot listing instructions that require a specific title company are facilitating a potential RESPA violation.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers carry supervisory liability under CREC Rule E-35, which requires reasonable supervision of all transaction activities conducted by their licensees. When it comes to closing procedures, brokers should audit three specific areas quarterly.

First, review all addenda and additional provisions added to CBS contracts by agents. Any language that interprets or creates legal obligations beyond fill-in-the-blank completion of approved forms is a red flag. The broker must either require attorney involvement or reject the language. Similar to how [agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on inspection and title objection periods, non-standard addenda often slip through without review until DORA comes calling.

Second, verify that AfBA disclosures are delivered timely and filed in every transaction where the brokerage has a title company relationship. A single missing AfBA disclosure in an audit can trigger a RESPA investigation that examines every file in the office. The [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) should be paired with AfBA compliance checks as part of a unified closing-risk protocol.

Third, confirm that agents are not attending closings and answering legal questions on behalf of clients. This sounds obvious, but audio recordings at some Colorado closing offices have revealed agents explaining deed-of-trust provisions, interpreting title exceptions, and advising on property tax proration disputes — all of which constitute unauthorized practice of law.

## Liability Consequences for Non-Compliance

An agent who crosses into unauthorized legal practice faces a two-front attack. DORA can impose fines, suspend or revoke the license, and require additional education under C.R.S. 12-10-217. Separately, the injured party can file a civil suit for damages caused by the agent's erroneous legal guidance, and E&O insurance may deny coverage if the act falls outside the scope of licensed activity.

**If a deal cancels because an agent improperly handled a title objection that should have gone to counsel, the brokerage faces potential commission clawback and a complaint from both parties.** The brokerage's errors and omissions carrier will scrutinize whether the supervising broker had policies in place. Without documentation showing broker-level oversight — written policies, audit trails, training records — the carrier may subrogate against the employing broker personally.

RESPA violations carry penalties up to $10,000 per occurrence and criminal liability for willful violations involving kickbacks. Colorado's Division of Insurance can also revoke a title entity's license for paying unlawful referral fees, which cascades back to the referring agent's brokerage.
