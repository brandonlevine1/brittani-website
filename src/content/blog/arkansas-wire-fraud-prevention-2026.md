## Understanding Arkansas Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026

The Arkansas wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 represent a significant tightening of agent and broker obligations around fund transfers. After more than $40 million in reported real estate wire fraud losses nationally in 2024 alone, Arkansas joined a growing list of states mandating specific disclosures and verification procedures at the transaction level. The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) now expects every licensee involved in a residential closing to follow documented protocols—or face enforcement consequences.

Wire fraud in real estate typically involves a bad actor intercepting email communications between agents, title companies, and buyers, then sending fraudulent wiring instructions. The buyer sends their down payment or closing funds to a criminal's account, and the money disappears within hours. Arkansas statute Ark. Code Ann. 5-37-227, which addresses computer fraud and wire-based financial crimes, provides the criminal framework, but AREC's regulatory updates place a preventive duty squarely on licensees.

This is not optional guidance. AREC has made wire fraud prevention a supervision and compliance issue, meaning brokers who fail to enforce it face the same disciplinary exposure as agents who skip it.

## The Specific Forms and Disclosures Required

**AREC Form WFA-1 (Wire Fraud Advisory) became mandatory for all residential transactions involving wired funds starting January 2025, with enhanced enforcement language added for 2026.** This one-page disclosure must be signed by any party who will send or receive funds via wire transfer during the transaction. Agents are responsible for presenting it at or before the point when earnest money instructions are first communicated.

Form WFA-1 includes three critical elements: a plain-language explanation of how wire fraud schemes operate, a directive to independently verify all wiring instructions via telephone using a known and trusted number, and an acknowledgment that the brokerage is not responsible for losses resulting from the client's failure to verify. The form does not replace verbal education—AREC expects agents to walk clients through the risks, not simply hand over paper.

In addition to WFA-1, many Arkansas title companies now require their own separate wire fraud acknowledgment at closing. Agents should not assume the title company's form satisfies the AREC requirement. Both must be completed and retained in the transaction file.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of skipping wire fraud disclosures in Arkansas operate on three levels: regulatory, civil, and transactional. On the regulatory side, AREC treats a missing Form WFA-1 as a supervision violation under [AREC Rule 10.14](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/), which governs transaction record-keeping and agent duties. First-offense penalties typically include a formal reprimand and mandatory continuing education, but repeat violations can trigger license suspension.

Civil liability is where the real financial pain lives. If a buyer loses funds to wire fraud and the agent never provided the required advisory, the agent and their brokerage become targets in a negligence lawsuit. Arkansas courts have increasingly looked at whether the agent met the standard of care—and a missing WFA-1 is strong evidence they did not. Insurance carriers may deny E&O claims where the agent failed to follow a mandatory state disclosure requirement.

On the transactional level, wire fraud can delay or kill a deal entirely. If a buyer's funds are stolen and cannot be recovered, the purchase often falls apart. The seller may pursue damages for breach of contract, and the agent's commission evaporates along with the closing.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Wire Fraud Prevention

**The most frequent error is treating Form WFA-1 as a closing document rather than an early-transaction document.** AREC expects the advisory to be delivered before wiring instructions are first communicated—typically at contract execution or earnest money deposit, not at the closing table three weeks later.

Second, agents often send wiring instructions via unencrypted email without any verbal verification step. Even when Form WFA-1 has been signed, forwarding title company wire instructions in a standard email creates the exact vulnerability the form warns about. Agents should never be the relay point for wire instructions. Direct the client to call the title company independently.

Third, some agents use outdated or brokerage-modified versions of Form WFA-1 that omit required language. AREC's 2026 version includes updated references to AI-generated phishing and deepfake voice calls—older versions do not address these threats and may not satisfy the current standard.

Fourth, agents fail to document that the advisory was delivered. A signed copy must be retained in the transaction file per AREC Rule 10.14's record-keeping requirements. Verbal warnings alone, without the signed form, do not satisfy compliance.

Fifth, agents assume the buyer's lender or the title company has already handled the wire fraud warning. This assumption is dangerous because AREC holds the licensee independently responsible regardless of what other parties have done. Your obligation does not transfer because someone else also warned the client.

| Common Mistake | Why It Fails Compliance | Corrective Action |
|---|---|---|
| Delivering WFA-1 at closing | Too late—funds may already be at risk | Present at contract execution |
| Emailing wire instructions directly | Creates interception vulnerability | Direct client to call title company |
| Using outdated form versions | Missing 2026 AI/deepfake language | Download current form from AREC |
| No signed copy in file | Violates AREC Rule 10.14 | Retain executed copy immediately |
| Assuming title company handled it | Agent duty is independent | Always provide WFA-1 yourself |

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Arkansas carry heightened responsibility under AREC's supervision rules. AREC Rule 10.5 requires the principal broker or supervising broker to maintain policies and procedures that ensure agent compliance with all mandatory disclosures, including wire fraud prevention. A broker who has no written wire fraud policy is already out of compliance before a single transaction closes.

**Quarterly file audits should specifically check for the presence and timing of Form WFA-1 in every transaction file involving wired funds.** The audit should verify that the form was signed before or at the time of contract execution—not weeks later at closing. Brokers should also confirm their agents are using the current 2026 version of the form, not a prior edition.

Beyond form audits, brokers need to establish and enforce email security protocols. This means requiring agents to use encrypted email platforms for any communication involving financial details, prohibiting agents from forwarding wire instructions, and maintaining a written policy that agents can reference. The policy should address how agents respond if a client reports receiving conflicting wire instructions.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | AREC Rule Reference |
|---|---|---|
| WFA-1 presence in transaction files | Every closed file | Rule 10.14 |
| Form version verification (2026 current) | Quarterly | Rule 10.14 |
| Written wire fraud office policy | Annual review | Rule 10.5 |
| Agent email security compliance | Semi-annual spot check | Rule 10.5 |
| Training documentation for new agents | At onboarding | Rule 10.5 |

Brokers should also document their training efforts. When AREC investigates a wire fraud complaint, one of the first questions is whether the brokerage provided adequate training and supervision. Written training logs with agent signatures provide a defense that verbal reminders at a monthly meeting cannot.
