## Alabama Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026: The Rules Have Teeth Now

The shift from informal buyer representation to mandatory written agreements has reshaped how Alabama agents conduct business. Understanding Alabama buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 is no longer optional knowledge—it is the baseline for keeping your license active and your brokerage out of legal trouble. The Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC) has made its enforcement posture clear through updated guidance memoranda and complaint processing timelines that favor swift action.

**Alabama's regulatory framework sits at the intersection of the NAR settlement mandates and state-specific statutory authority.** The combination means agents face two layers of obligation: federal antitrust compliance through their MLS participation and state licensing law compliance through AREC. Failing on either front creates distinct problems, and the penalties stack.

This post breaks down exactly what Alabama law requires, which forms satisfy those requirements, where agents keep getting tripped up, and what brokers should be auditing right now.

## The Statutory Foundation: Alabama Code Section 34-27-36

Alabama's real estate licensing law is codified in Title 34, Chapter 27 of the Alabama Code. Section 34-27-36 grants AREC authority to discipline licensees for, among other things, failure to adequately disclose agency relationships and failure to obtain proper authorization before acting on a client's behalf. The statute does not use the phrase "buyer agency agreement" verbatim, but AREC has interpreted its disclosure and authorization mandates to require written buyer representation agreements since the commission's 2024 guidance update.

AREC's administrative rules, particularly Rule 790-X-3-.09, further specify that agents must clearly establish the nature and scope of their agency relationship in writing. The rule was amended in late 2024 to align with the practical access requirements stemming from the NAR settlement. Under the current framework, an agent who provides brokerage services to a buyer without a signed written agreement is operating outside their authorized scope.

> "No licensee shall perform services for a consumer as a buyer's agent without first obtaining a written agreement that clearly identifies the terms of representation, including compensation." — AREC Rule 790-X-3-.09(b)

The [Alabama Real Estate Commission's official website](https://arec.alabama.gov) publishes all current administrative rules and enforcement actions for public review.

## Which Forms Satisfy the Requirement

The Alabama Association of REALTORS (AAR) maintains the standard forms that most Alabama brokerages rely on. For buyer representation, two primary forms handle the job.

| Form | Name | Use Case | Key Feature |
|------|------|----------|-------------|
| AAR Form 13 | Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Single-agent exclusive representation | Buyer committed to one agent for defined term |
| AAR Form 13A | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement | Non-exclusive representation | Buyer may work with multiple agents simultaneously |
| AAR Form 14 | Buyer Agency Disclosure | Pre-agreement disclosure of agency options | Must precede Form 13 or 13A |
| AAR Form 1 | Agency Disclosure | General agency relationship notice | Required at first substantive contact |

**Form 13 is the workhorse document for most transactions.** It establishes the exclusive relationship, defines the compensation terms (including the specific dollar amount or percentage the buyer's agent will receive), sets the agreement duration, and outlines the conditions under which the agreement terminates. The 2025 revision added mandatory language about compensation not being set by any industry standard and being fully negotiable.

Form 13A serves agents who prefer non-exclusive arrangements or whose buyers insist on flexibility. It carries the same disclosure obligations but does not bind the buyer to a single agent. Both forms require the agent to specify compensation in a way that does not reference an MLS offer of compensation as a floor or guarantee.

Agents who are not AAR members or whose brokerages use proprietary forms must still meet the minimum content requirements outlined in AREC Rule 790-X-3-.09(b): identification of parties, scope of services, compensation terms, duration, and termination provisions. Custom forms should be reviewed by the brokerage's legal counsel before deployment.

## Timing: When Must the Agreement Be Signed

The timing question trips up more agents than any other compliance issue. AREC's guidance, consistent with the NAR settlement's MLS policy requirements, mandates that the buyer agency agreement be signed before the agent provides substantive brokerage services to the buyer. The question becomes: what counts as "substantive"?

AREC's 2025 FAQ document identifies the following as substantive brokerage services that trigger the requirement: touring a property in person, submitting an offer, providing a comparative market analysis tailored to a specific property, negotiating on the buyer's behalf, or providing specific property recommendations based on the buyer's stated criteria. General conversations about the market, handing someone a business card, or responding to an inquiry at an open house do not trigger the requirement—until the conversation shifts to personalized guidance.

**Open houses present a specific gray area that AREC addressed directly.** An agent hosting an open house represents the seller. A buyer attending that open house does not need a signed buyer agency agreement with the listing agent. However, if a different agent accompanies the buyer to the open house as their representative, that agent needs the signed agreement in place before entry. The distinction matters because complaints frequently arise from open house interactions where roles blur.

The practical takeaway: get the agreement signed at or before the first scheduled property showing. Many Alabama agents now incorporate the signing into their initial buyer consultation, which is the cleanest compliance approach and avoids the awkward parking-lot conversation before a tour.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

Alabama does not treat buyer agency agreement violations as mere paperwork oversights. The consequences flow from multiple directions.

AREC can impose disciplinary action under Section 34-27-36(a)(17) for violating commission rules. Penalties include formal reprimand, mandatory education requirements, fines up to $2,500 per violation, license suspension, and license revocation. AREC's published enforcement data from fiscal year 2025 shows the commission processed 47 complaints related to agency disclosure failures—a 60% increase from the prior year.

Beyond AREC discipline, agents face civil liability. A buyer who was not properly informed of their agency options or who signed an inadequate agreement may argue they never authorized the agent to act on their behalf. That argument can unwind compensation claims, particularly if the buyer purchases through another agent or directly from a seller. Without a valid written agreement, the agent has no contractual basis to demand payment.

MLS consequences add a third layer. Alabama's major MLSs, including the Greater Alabama MLS (GALMLS) and the Baldwin County Association of REALTORS MLS, have incorporated buyer agreement verification into their showing and offer submission protocols. Agents who cannot demonstrate a valid buyer agreement may face MLS fines, suspension from MLS access, or rejection of submitted offers.

| Violation | AREC Penalty | Civil Risk | MLS Consequence |
|-----------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| No written agreement before showing | Fine up to $2,500; possible suspension | No enforceable compensation claim | Showing access may be denied |
| Agreement missing compensation terms | Fine; mandatory education | Compensation dispute at closing | Offer may be rejected |
| Agreement exceeds reasonable duration without justification | Reprimand; possible fine | Buyer may seek rescission | None directly |
| Failure to provide Form 1 disclosure | Fine up to $2,500 | Informed consent defense weakened | Complaint to MLS ethics committee |

## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Make

Five errors appear repeatedly in AREC complaint files and brokerage audit findings. Each is avoidable with proper systems and awareness.

**First, agents fill in compensation as "per MLS" or "as offered by seller."** This language violates both the NAR settlement requirements and AREC's mandate that compensation be clearly specified. The agreement must state the exact amount or percentage the buyer's agent will earn. If the seller offers less than that amount, the agreement should address who covers the shortfall—the buyer, the agent absorbing the difference, or a renegotiation trigger.

Second, agents present the agreement as a mere formality rather than a meaningful contract. When buyers perceive the form as administrative paperwork rather than a binding agreement, disputes erupt later. Agents who rush through the explanation without discussing duration, exclusivity, and termination provisions set themselves up for complaints when the relationship sours.

Third, agents use expired or outdated form versions. AAR updates its forms periodically, and the buyer agency forms underwent significant revision in late 2024 and again in early 2026. An agent who prints Form 13 from a 2023 template is using a document that may not contain required language about compensation negotiability or the specific AREC-mandated disclosures. Always pull forms fresh from the AAR forms library or your brokerage's approved document management system.

Fourth, agents fail to provide the general agency disclosure (Form 1) before presenting the buyer agency agreement. AREC requires that buyers understand their options—seller's agent, buyer's agent, transaction broker, or no representation—before committing to one path. Skipping Form 1 undermines the informed consent that Form 13 or 13A relies on. This sequencing error has generated multiple formal complaints in 2025 and 2026.

Fifth, agents neglect to retain signed copies. [Why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) is often a document management problem, not a knowledge gap. If you cannot produce the signed agreement during an audit or dispute, it functionally does not exist. AREC requires licensees to maintain transaction records for three years under Rule 790-X-3-.03.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Alabama Code Section 34-27-36(a)(8) for failing to supervise their agents' compliance with commission rules. A broker who knows—or should know—that agents are showing properties without signed buyer agreements faces their own disciplinary exposure. [Why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) applies directly here: systematic auditing outperforms sporadic spot-checks.

**Every Alabama broker should maintain a verification protocol that confirms three things before an agent conducts a showing.** First, that Form 1 was delivered to the buyer and a signed acknowledgment exists. Second, that Form 13 or 13A (or an approved equivalent) is fully executed with all fields completed—no blanks, no "TBD," no references to MLS compensation. Third, that the agreement's effective date precedes any substantive service the agent provided.

Brokers should audit files monthly, not just at closing. Waiting until a transaction reaches the closing table to check for a buyer agency agreement means the violation already occurred weeks or months earlier. The damage—to the buyer, the agent, and the brokerage's reputation—has already been done.
