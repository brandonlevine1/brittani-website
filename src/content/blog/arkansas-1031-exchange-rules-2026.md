## Understanding Arkansas 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026

The federal 1031 exchange gets most of the attention, but Arkansas 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 present a distinct layer of risk that many agents either underestimate or ignore entirely. Arkansas imposes its own income tax on capital gains from real property sales, and the state's treatment of like-kind exchanges does not mirror the IRS playbook in every respect. Agents who assume federal deferral automatically means state deferral are setting their clients—and themselves—up for audit exposure.

Arkansas generally conforms to IRC Section 1031 for state income tax purposes under Ark. Code Ann. 26-51-401 et seq., which adopts the Internal Revenue Code as the starting point for calculating state taxable income. However, conformity has limits. When replacement property lands outside Arkansas, the Department of Finance and Administration (DFA) treats the transaction differently than a purely intrastate exchange.

The DFA enforces withholding obligations through Ark. Code Ann. 26-18-505, which requires closing agents to withhold Arkansas income tax from nonresident sellers. Even resident exchangors face reporting obligations that, if missed, can unravel the tax deferral months after closing. For agents facilitating these deals, understanding the state-specific triggers is not optional—it is the baseline for competent representation.

## How Arkansas Taxes Capital Gains in Exchange Transactions

**Arkansas taxes capital gains as ordinary income at the individual level, with a top marginal rate of 4.4% for tax year 2026 following the phased reductions enacted by Act 115 of 2023.** This rate applies to net capital gains realized on real property unless deferral under Section 1031 is properly elected and documented at both the federal and state level.

When a property owner sells investment real estate in Arkansas and identifies replacement property within the state, the deferral generally flows through without additional state filing beyond standard returns. The exchangor reports the transaction on their Arkansas individual return using federal Form 8824 data, and the DFA accepts the deferral as computed federally. But this clean path only works when replacement property remains within Arkansas jurisdiction.

The friction begins with out-of-state replacement property. Arkansas asserts taxing authority over the realized gain because the relinquished property sat within its borders. The state's position—consistent with positions taken by other states including [Wisconsin's approach to 1031 withholding](/blog/wisconsin-1031-exchange-rules-2026)—is that gain attributable to Arkansas-situs property belongs to Arkansas regardless of where the exchanger reinvests.

## Nonresident Withholding and the AR1099-S Obligation

Closing agents in Arkansas must file Form AR1099-S for any real property transaction exceeding $150,000, reporting the gross proceeds to the DFA. This parallels the federal 1099-S requirement but carries independent state penalties for noncompliance. The form must be filed by February 28 of the year following the closing.

For nonresident sellers attempting a 1031 exchange, the withholding requirement under Ark. Code Ann. 26-18-505 creates a timing problem. The closing agent must withhold 2% of the gross sales price unless the seller provides a valid exemption certificate. A properly structured 1031 exchange qualifies for exemption, but only if the exchanger provides the closing agent with documentation—including the exchange agreement and identification of replacement property—before or at closing.

| Scenario | Withholding Required? | Form Filed | Rate |
|----------|----------------------|------------|------|
| Nonresident seller, no exchange | Yes | AR1099-S + AR941 | 2% of gross proceeds |
| Nonresident seller, valid 1031 exchange | Exemption available | AR1099-S + exemption cert | 0% if cert provided |
| Resident seller, exchange to out-of-state property | Depends on filing | AR1099-S | Gain taxed on return |
| Resident seller, exchange to in-state property | No | AR1099-S | Deferred |

The exemption certificate is not a state-published form with a specific number—it is a written declaration from the exchanger, typically prepared by their CPA or qualified intermediary, affirming that the transaction qualifies under IRC 1031. Agents should confirm this document exists before closing day, because without it, the title company will withhold by default.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Arkansas 1031 Exchanges

**The most frequent error agents commit is telling clients their 1031 exchange "takes care of" all tax obligations.** That statement, even made informally, can create liability exposure under the Arkansas Real Estate License Law (Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-101 et seq.) if the client later faces an unexpected state tax bill. Here are the specific mistakes that surface repeatedly:

First, agents fail to distinguish between federal and state deferral requirements. An exchanger who moves proceeds into replacement property in Texas may achieve full federal deferral but still owe Arkansas income tax on the gain. Agents who do not flag this distinction for their clients are allowing them to walk into a trap.

Second, listing agents neglect to verify whether the seller's qualified intermediary has established the exchange agreement before the closing date. Under both federal and state rules, the exchange must be structured before the transfer of the relinquished property. An agent who orders closing without confirming the QI agreement is in place has functionally allowed their client to complete a taxable sale.

Third, agents misunderstand the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines as flexible guidelines rather than absolute statutory limits. Arkansas conforms to these federal deadlines, and missing either one converts the entire transaction into a fully taxable event at both levels—no partial credit, no extension.

Fourth, agents representing buyers in a 1031 exchange fail to account for the exchangor's timeline pressure, resulting in blown deadlines and failed exchanges. The buyer's agent who drags out inspection negotiations past day 44 may have just cost the seller a six-figure tax bill.

Fifth, agents do not document their disclosures about tax implications. When a deal sours and the client alleges they were never told about state tax exposure, the agent with no written record faces a "he said, she said" scenario before the [Arkansas Real Estate Commission](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov/) (AREC). A simple email summarizing the referral to a tax professional provides meaningful protection.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under AREC Rule 10.8 for ensuring agents within their office do not provide tax advice beyond their competency. This is not an abstract standard—AREC has issued disciplinary actions for agents who offered specific tax guidance that later proved incorrect or incomplete. Brokers need a concrete review process.

**Every transaction file involving a 1031 exchange should contain three documents the broker can verify: the qualified intermediary agreement, evidence of timely identification (the 45-day letter), and a written referral to a CPA or tax attorney.** If any of these three items is missing, the broker should flag the file before closing.

| Audit Item | What Broker Checks | Red Flag |
|------------|-------------------|----------|
| QI agreement | Signed before closing date | Agreement post-dates deed transfer |
| 45-day identification | Written, signed, delivered to QI | Missing or delivered late |
| Tax referral documentation | Email or letter referring client to CPA | Agent gave specific tax advice instead |
| Nonresident withholding | Exemption cert in file or withholding confirmed | Neither present |
| AR1099-S filing | Title company confirmation | No record of filing |

Brokers should also review whether agents are marketing properties with 1031 exchange benefits as a selling point without appropriate disclaimers. Advertising that implies guaranteed tax savings—without disclosing that state tax may still apply—can trigger AREC complaints under deceptive advertising provisions in Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311.

A quarterly file audit that specifically checks 1031-flagged transactions takes minimal time but provides substantial protection. Brokers who build this into their [weekly pipeline review system](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) catch problems before they become commission complaints.

## Liability Exposure When Compliance Fails

An agent's failure to comply with disclosure obligations in a 1031 exchange does not just expose the client to tax liability—it exposes the agent to professional discipline, civil claims, and potential deal rescission. AREC can impose fines up to $2,500 per violation under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311, suspend or revoke licenses, and require additional continuing education.

Civil liability presents the larger financial risk. If a client can demonstrate that the agent's misrepresentation or omission regarding state tax obligations caused quantifiable harm, the agent faces a breach of fiduciary duty claim. Arkansas courts apply a duty of reasonable care standard—agents need not be tax experts, but they must know enough to refer clients to qualified professionals when 1031 exchange state tax questions arise.

Deal cancellation is also a real consequence. When a seller discovers mid-transaction that Arkansas will tax their gain despite the 1031 structure—because the replacement property is out of state, for example—they may refuse to close. The listing agent who failed to raise this issue earlier now has an angry client, a blown commission, and a potential errors and omissions claim.

## The Out-of-State Replacement Property Problem

This is the scenario that catches the most experienced agents off guard. An Arkansas investor sells a rental property in Fayetteville, engages a qualified intermediary, identifies replacement property in Tennessee within 45 days, and closes within 180 days. Federal deferral: complete. Arkansas state tax: fully owed.

The DFA's position is straightforward. The gain originated from Arkansas-situs property. When replacement property leaves the state, Arkansas loses future taxing authority over that deferred gain. Therefore, the state accelerates recognition of the gain to the year of sale. The exchanger must report and pay Arkansas income tax on the full capital gain, currently at rates up to 4.4%.

This creates an awkward conversation for agents. The client believed the exchange deferred all tax. It deferred federal tax—potentially hundreds of thousands of dollars—but the state bill remains. Agents who raise this issue during listing presentations demonstrate the kind of competency that earns long-term client loyalty. Agents who stay silent risk the opposite outcome.
