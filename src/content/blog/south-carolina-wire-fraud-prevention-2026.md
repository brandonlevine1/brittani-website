## Why South Carolina Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

Wire fraud stole over $446 million from real estate transactions nationally in 2023 according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and South Carolina has responded with increasingly specific compliance expectations for licensees. The South Carolina wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 framework places direct obligations on agents — not just closing attorneys — to warn clients and document that warning. Ignorance of these requirements is not a viable defense before the SC Real Estate Commission.

South Carolina operates as a closing-attorney state, which means real estate agents sometimes assume all wire-related duties belong to the attorney's office. That assumption has cost agents their licenses. The Commission has made clear through enforcement actions and formal guidance that agents bear an independent duty to educate clients about wire fraud risks before funds move.

## The Regulatory Framework: Statutes, Rules, and Forms

The primary statutory authority governing agent conduct in South Carolina is the SC Real Estate License Law, codified at SC Code Title 40, Chapter 57. Section 40-57-137 establishes the duty of licensees to act with reasonable skill and care, which the Commission interprets to include warning clients of known transaction risks — wire fraud now chief among them.

The SC Real Estate Commission, housed within the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation (LLR), publishes [regulatory guidance](https://llr.sc.gov/rec/) that has been updated to reflect wire fraud as a foreseeable risk agents must address. Commission Rule 105-1 requires that all transaction documents, including fraud disclosures, be retained for at least four years.

SC REALTORS (the state REALTOR association) developed Form SCR 905 — the Wire Fraud Warning — specifically for this purpose. While not mandated by statute the way the Residential Property Condition Disclosure (SC Code Section 27-50-40) is mandated, failure to use SCR 905 or an equivalent written warning effectively eliminates your best defense in a complaint or civil suit.

## What Form SCR 905 Actually Requires

**Form SCR 905 must be signed by all parties to the transaction before any wire instructions are communicated.** The form accomplishes three things: it notifies clients that wire fraud exists in real estate, it instructs them to verify all wiring instructions via a known phone number, and it creates a documented paper trail that the agent fulfilled their warning obligation.

The form language specifically advises clients never to rely solely on emailed wire instructions, even if those emails appear to come from the closing attorney's office. It also recommends clients call the closing attorney's office using a phone number obtained independently — not from the same email containing wire instructions.

Agents should present SCR 905 at the same time they present the Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate (SC Form 310) or shortly thereafter. Waiting until three days before closing defeats the purpose and weakens your compliance position.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Skip the Warning

The consequences of failing to provide wire fraud warnings break into three categories: regulatory, civil, and insurance-related. Each one can end a career independently.

| Consequence Type | What Happens | Typical Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| SC REC Disciplinary Action | Formal complaint filed under Section 40-57-137 | Reprimand, fine ($500-$5,000), license suspension |
| Civil Liability | Buyer sues agent for negligence | Agent may be liable for full wire loss (often $50K-$300K+) |
| E&O Insurance Denial | Carrier denies claim for failure to follow standard of care | Agent pays defense costs and any judgment out of pocket |
| Brokerage Termination | Broker removes agent for compliance failure | Loss of affiliation, reputation damage |
| Transaction Cancellation | Buyer cannot close without stolen funds | Deal dies, commission forfeited |

**A single wire fraud incident where the agent failed to warn the buyer can generate all five consequences simultaneously.** The SC Real Estate Commission has publicly noted that wire fraud complaints have increased year over year, and it treats failure to warn as a serious competency issue under the license law.

South Carolina courts apply a "reasonably prudent agent" standard. Given that SCR 905 has existed for years and wire fraud is extensively covered in continuing education, no agent can credibly claim they did not know about the risk. The standard of care has shifted permanently.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

Agents who think they are compliant often fall short in specific, predictable ways. These are the errors the Commission sees repeatedly in disciplinary files.

The first mistake is presenting SCR 905 in a stack of documents at the initial signing without verbally explaining it. A signed form means nothing if the client later testifies they had no idea what they signed. Agents should spend sixty seconds explaining wire fraud in plain language and confirm the client understands the callback verification process.

The second mistake is failing to re-warn clients as closing approaches. The initial disclosure happens weeks or months before funds move. A brief email or text reminder three to five days before closing — telling the client to call the attorney's office to verify wiring instructions — reinforces the warning when it matters most. This follow-up is not required by form, but it demonstrates the reasonable skill and care the statute demands.

The third mistake is forwarding wire instructions from the closing attorney to the client. Agents should never be the intermediary for wiring instructions. When agents forward those details, they insert themselves into the chain of custody and create additional phishing opportunities. The correct practice is to direct clients to contact the attorney's office directly.

The fourth mistake is using personal email accounts without encryption or multi-factor authentication. Hackers target agents' email accounts precisely because agents communicate with buyers daily. A compromised agent email becomes the launch point for fraudulent wire instructions that appear legitimate to the buyer. Agents operating outside their brokerage's secured email system create liability for themselves and their broker.

The fifth mistake is assuming the closing attorney handles everything. South Carolina's attorney-state model means attorneys manage closings, but that does not absolve agents of their independent statutory duty under Section 40-57-137. The Commission has been explicit: agents have their own obligation.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry supervisory liability under SC Code Section 40-57-135. If an agent in your office fails to deliver wire fraud warnings and a client loses money, the Commission can and will examine whether you had systems in place to prevent that failure.

**Every broker-in-charge in South Carolina should audit for three things quarterly.** First, confirm that every transaction file contains a signed SCR 905 or equivalent wire fraud disclosure. Second, verify that agents are not forwarding wire instructions through their email accounts. Third, check that all agents are using brokerage-approved email systems with multi-factor authentication enabled.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Documentation Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Signed SCR 905 in every file | Every transaction | Copy of signed form with date |
| Agent email security compliance | Quarterly | MFA verification log from email provider |
| Wire instruction forwarding check | Random spot-check monthly | Email search for "wire" or "routing" in agent sent folders |
| CE completion for wire fraud topics | Annually | Certificate of completion from approved provider |

The Commission's four-year record retention requirement under Rule 105-1 means these audits produce records you must keep. If a complaint arrives two years after closing, you need that documentation accessible and organized. Agents who rely on memory instead of systems create gaps that [hurt everyone in the transaction](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Brokers should also establish a written office policy on wire fraud prevention procedures. This policy should specify when SCR 905 is presented, prohibit agents from forwarding wire instructions, and require MFA on all email accounts. A written policy, signed annually by each agent, becomes powerful evidence of supervisory diligence if a complaint is filed.

## The Closing Attorney Relationship: Boundaries and Coordination

South Carolina's requirement that attorneys supervise closings creates a natural division of labor, but it does not create a wall between agent responsibilities and attorney responsibilities. The best practice is coordination, not delegation.

At the start of every transaction, agents should confirm with the closing attorney's office what their wire instruction delivery process looks like. Many firms now use encrypted portals rather than email. Knowing the attorney's process allows you to accurately tell your client: "The attorney's office will send wire instructions through their secure portal — if you receive instructions any other way, call them immediately."

This coordination also protects you if something goes wrong. Documenting that you directed the client to the attorney's verified process — and that you warned them about fraudulent alternatives — creates a robust defense. Agents who work transactions without understanding the [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) at the attorney's office are flying blind.

## Continuing Education and Staying Current

The SC Real Estate Commission requires licensees to complete continuing education for renewal, and wire fraud prevention has become a regular topic in approved coursework. The 2025-2026 CE cycle includes multiple approved courses specifically addressing cybersecurity and fraud prevention in real estate transactions.

Agents should document their CE completion related to wire fraud topics as additional evidence of competency. If a complaint is filed, showing that you completed targeted training — beyond just presenting the form — demonstrates the reasonable skill and care the statute requires.

The Commission also publishes advisories and enforcement summaries that agents and brokers should monitor. These publications signal where enforcement focus is shifting, and wire fraud has been a consistent priority since 2022.
