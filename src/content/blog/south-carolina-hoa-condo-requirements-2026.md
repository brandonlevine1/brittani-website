# South Carolina HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Deliver Before Closing

The South Carolina hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 carry real teeth for agents who treat them as afterthoughts. Roughly 40 percent of residential resales in the state now involve a homeowners association or condominium regime, and the statutory framework governing disclosures has not become simpler since the legislature last amended the Horizontal Property Act. Missing a single document delivery can hand a buyer a free walk-away, expose your brokerage to liability, and tank a commission you spent months earning.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, deadlines, and enforcement consequences that licensed agents and their managing brokers must understand to stay compliant through the rest of 2026.

## The Two Statutes That Control Everything

South Carolina splits HOA and condo disclosure obligations across two primary bodies of law. The **South Carolina Horizontal Property Act** (S.C. Code Title 27, Chapter 31) governs condominiums. The **South Carolina Homeowners Association Act** (S.C. Code Section 27-30-110 et seq.) governs planned communities with mandatory membership associations.

Agents often conflate the two, but the disclosure triggers and buyer remedies differ. Condominiums carry a statutory rescission period that HOA properties do not automatically receive unless the contract provides one. Understanding which statute applies to your listing is the first compliance checkpoint, and it is the one most frequently skipped.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) does not publish a standalone HOA disclosure form the way some states do. Instead, agents rely on SCR Form 310 (the standard contract of sale) and the [Condominium Addendum (SCR Form 370)](https://www.llr.sc.gov/re/) to memorialize obligations and timelines within the purchase agreement.

## Condominium Resale Disclosure Requirements Under S.C. Code 27-31-120

**For condominium resales, the seller must furnish a resale certificate to the buyer before or at the time the contract is executed.** This certificate must include the monthly common expense assessment, any pending special assessments, the association's current budget, the most recent audited financial statement, and the declaration plus all amendments.

S.C. Code Section 27-31-120 grants the buyer a 15-day rescission window measured from the date of receipt of the resale certificate. If the seller never delivers the certificate, that rescission window never closes. This means a buyer could theoretically rescind weeks after closing if no delivery occurred, though courts have imposed reasonableness limits in contested cases.

The resale certificate is typically prepared by the association's management company or board, not by the listing agent. However, the listing agent carries the practical burden of ensuring the seller orders it early enough to meet contractual deadlines. Delays from management companies are not a valid excuse for missed delivery under the statute.

## HOA Disclosure Obligations for Non-Condo Properties

Planned communities governed by the Homeowners Association Act require disclosure of different materials. Sellers must provide the buyer with a copy of the governing documents (CC&Rs, bylaws, rules and regulations), current assessment amounts, any outstanding balances or liens on the property, and information about pending litigation involving the association.

Unlike the condominium statute, the Homeowners Association Act does not create an automatic statutory rescission period. The buyer's right to cancel depends on what the parties negotiated in the purchase contract. This is where SCR Form 310 and any HOA-specific addendum become the controlling documents.

| Requirement | Condominiums (Ch. 31) | HOA Planned Communities (Sec. 27-30) |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory rescission period | 15 days from receipt | None unless contractual |
| Resale certificate required | Yes, by statute | Not required by statute but standard practice |
| Financial statement delivery | Mandatory | Best practice, not mandated |
| Governing documents delivery | Mandatory | Mandatory |
| Who prepares package | Association/management company | Association/management company |

Agents representing buyers in HOA communities should insist on contractual contingency language that mirrors the condominium rescission protections. Without it, the buyer's only remedy for undisclosed assessments may be a post-closing damages claim—far more expensive and uncertain than a clean rescission.

## Forms South Carolina Agents Must Know

The South Carolina Association of Realtors publishes the standard forms used in most MLS transactions. The relevant forms for HOA and condo disclosure compliance in 2026 include:

SCR Form 310 is the Residential Contract of Sale and contains blanks for HOA name, management company contact, assessment amounts, and document delivery deadlines. SCR Form 370 is the Condominium Addendum, which specifically addresses the resale certificate delivery, the 15-day rescission window, and the buyer's acknowledgment of receipt. SCR Form 390 (the HOA/Community Addendum) addresses non-condo associations and allows parties to define document delivery timelines and contingency periods.

**Failing to attach the correct addendum is not a harmless paperwork oversight—it removes the contractual scaffolding that protects both parties.** If an agent submits a contract on a condominium unit using only Form 310 without attaching Form 370, the statutory rescission right still exists under Section 27-31-120, but the contract lacks clear delivery procedures. That ambiguity creates dispute opportunities that no one wants to litigate.

Agents should also be aware of SCR Form 320 (the Buyer's Due Diligence Request) which can be used to request additional HOA records beyond what the statute requires, such as reserve study reports, insurance certificates, and meeting minutes. Sophisticated buyer agents use this form routinely in [transactions where deadlines intersect with multiple contingencies](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of non-compliance fall into three categories: transaction-level remedies, licensee discipline, and civil liability.

At the transaction level, a buyer who never received a condominium resale certificate retains rescission rights indefinitely until reasonable time limits apply. Courts in South Carolina have allowed rescission claims filed months after closing when the seller demonstrably failed to deliver required documents. The buyer can demand return of earnest money and, in some cases, recovery of closing costs.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action against agents who demonstrate a pattern of negligent practice. Under S.C. Code Section 40-57-145, grounds for discipline include incompetence, negligent handling of documents, and failure to properly supervise transactions. Sanctions range from reprimands and mandatory continuing education to license suspension.

Civil liability is the most expensive risk. A buyer who purchases a unit without knowledge of a pending $15,000 special assessment has a viable claim against the seller and potentially against the listing agent who failed to ensure disclosure. Errors and omissions insurance may cover some claims, but carriers increasingly scrutinize whether the agent followed standard procedures.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA and Condo Disclosures

**The first and most frequent error is waiting until after contract execution to order the resale package.** Management companies in high-volume markets like Charleston, Greenville, and Myrtle Beach routinely take 10 to 14 business days to produce documents. If the contractual delivery deadline is 10 days after binding agreement, the listing agent has already created a problem before the ink dries.

The second mistake is treating the HOA disclosure obligation as the seller's sole responsibility. While the seller must provide the documents, the listing agent has a professional duty to advise the seller of their obligation and ensure timely action. Agents who say "that's the seller's problem" are betting their license on the seller's diligence—a poor wager.

Third, buyer's agents frequently fail to calendar the rescission deadline separately from the due diligence period. The 15-day condominium rescission window and the general inspection contingency period often expire on different dates. Missing the rescission deadline because an agent assumed it aligned with the inspection period is a rookie error that happens to experienced agents more often than anyone admits.

Fourth, agents neglect to verify that the resale certificate is current. An association may have approved a special assessment between the date the certificate was prepared and the closing date. If the certificate is more than 30 days old at closing, best practice demands requesting an updated estoppel letter confirming no new assessments exist.

Fifth, listing agents sometimes omit material HOA information from MLS entries—particularly pending litigation or known assessment increases. While MLS fields are not statutory disclosure documents, inaccurate MLS data can support a misrepresentation claim when paired with inadequate formal disclosures. Agents should cross-reference MLS entries against association documents before going live, a task that becomes much easier with [systematic checklist-driven workflows](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers carry supervisory liability under S.C. Code Section 40-57-137. If an agent in the office consistently fails to attach condominium addenda or misses disclosure deadlines, the broker is exposed to discipline alongside the agent.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Addendum attachment | Form 370 or 390 present for every HOA/condo listing | Every new contract |
| Resale certificate tracking | Ordered within 48 hours of listing or contract | Weekly pipeline review |
| Rescission deadline calendaring | 15-day window docketed separately from inspection period | Every condo transaction |
| MLS accuracy | HOA fees, special assessments, and restrictions match governing docs | Listing launch + quarterly |
| Estoppel letter at closing | Current balance confirmation obtained within 15 days of closing | Pre-closing file review |

Brokers should build these checkpoints into their transaction management systems rather than relying on individual agent memory. A [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that flags HOA/condo transactions without confirmed document delivery dates catches problems before they metastasize into rescission claims or commission disputes.

Quarterly training sessions focused exclusively on HOA and condo compliance are not overkill for offices operating in association-heavy markets. The 2026 legislative session did not produce major amendments to either the Horizontal Property Act or the Homeowners Association Act, but SCREC interpretive guidance continues to evolve, and brokers who wait for a complaint to update their procedures pay a steep tuition.
