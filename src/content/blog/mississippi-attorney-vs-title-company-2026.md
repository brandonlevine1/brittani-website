## Mississippi Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Rules Agents Cannot Afford to Misread

The question of Mississippi attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up more agents than any other procedural issue in the state. Mississippi sits in a gray zone: it is not a pure "attorney state" like Georgia or South Carolina, nor is it a "title company state" like California or Texas. The practical result is a hybrid system where failing to understand who does what can expose agents and their brokers to unauthorized practice of law (UPL) allegations, deal delays, and regulatory discipline from the Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC).

This post breaks down exactly what Mississippi law requires, where agents cross the line, and what brokers must put in place before a single file reaches the closing table in 2026.

## What Mississippi Law Actually Says About Closing Authority

Mississippi has no single statute titled "who must close a real estate transaction." Instead, the legal framework is built from multiple sources: Mississippi Code Annotated Section 73-53-1 et seq. (title insurance regulation), Mississippi Code Section 73-35-1 et seq. (real estate licensing), and advisory opinions from the Mississippi Bar's Committee on Unauthorized Practice of Law.

**The foundational rule is this: any activity that constitutes the "practice of law" must be performed by a Mississippi-licensed attorney.** The Mississippi Supreme Court defined the practice of law broadly in *Darby v. Mississippi State Board of Bar Admissions* (1983) to include drafting legal instruments, interpreting legal rights, and advising parties about legal obligations. Title companies operate under the Mississippi Department of Insurance and are authorized to issue title insurance policies, conduct closings as settlement agents, and disburse funds — but they cannot draft deeds, deeds of trust, or other conveyancing documents unless a licensed attorney supervises or prepares them.

In practice, most residential closings in Mississippi are conducted in an attorney's office. A 2024 survey by the Mississippi Land Title Association found that roughly 78 percent of residential transactions in the state involve a closing attorney who prepares the deed, reviews the title commitment, and certifies the title opinion.

## Attorney vs. Title Company: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

The confusion often stems from agents assuming that one party handles everything. Here is how responsibilities actually split in a typical Mississippi transaction:

| Function | Attorney | Title Company |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| Deed preparation | Required — must be attorney-drafted | Cannot draft; may provide template for attorney review |
| Title examination and opinion | Performs or supervises the exam | Orders the search; relies on attorney opinion for underwriting |
| Title insurance issuance | Does not issue (unless also a title agent) | Issues policy per Mississippi Dept. of Insurance rules |
| Escrow/disbursement | May hold escrow; must comply with MREC Rule 3.1 if also acting as broker's agent | Holds escrow; regulated under Miss. Code 83-23-101 |
| Deed of trust preparation | Required — attorney must prepare | Cannot prepare |
| Closing facilitation (signing) | May conduct | May conduct if no legal interpretation required |

This division means that many closings require both parties at the table — or at minimum, an attorney who has prepared documents in advance and a title company representative issuing the policy and handling disbursement.

## The Unauthorized Practice of Law Trap for Agents

MREC Rule 3.1 and Mississippi Code Section 73-35-21 make clear that a real estate licensee shall not engage in acts constituting the practice of law. Filling in blanks on MREC-approved forms (such as the Mississippi Association of REALTORS Form 101 Residential Purchase Agreement or Form 201 Counter-Offer) is permitted — that is an express exception carved out by the Mississippi Supreme Court in *In re: Mississippi Land Title Association* (1987). But the moment an agent drafts a clause from scratch, modifies a deed, or advises a buyer about the legal effect of a title exception, the agent has crossed into UPL territory.

> "The preparation of deeds, deeds of trust, and other conveyancing instruments constitutes the practice of law in Mississippi and must be performed by a licensed attorney." — Mississippi Bar Formal Ethics Opinion No. 231

Penalties are not theoretical. MREC can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation under Miss. Code Section 73-35-21(2), suspend or revoke the agent's license, and refer the matter to the Mississippi Bar for criminal UPL prosecution. The agent's brokerage also faces vicarious exposure if the broker failed to supervise.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make at Closing

**Mistake one: telling buyers they do not need an attorney.** Agents sometimes push back on attorney fees to keep closing costs low and preserve the deal. This is not legal advice the agent is qualified to give. If the buyer later discovers a title defect that a closing attorney would have flagged, the agent faces a negligence claim.

Mistake two: drafting addenda that go beyond fill-in-the-blank MAR forms. The Mississippi Association of REALTORS provides Form 301 (Property Condition Addendum), Form 401 (Financing Contingency Addendum), and others specifically to keep agents within legal bounds. When an agent writes a custom addendum — even a "simple" repair agreement — without attorney involvement, that agent is practicing law. MREC has issued consent orders on exactly this fact pattern as recently as January 2025.

Mistake three: selecting a closing attorney or title company without disclosing affiliated business arrangements. RESPA Section 8 still applies, and Mississippi's version of the Affiliated Business Arrangement Disclosure (HUD-1 era holdover, now formalized in CFPB Regulation X, 12 CFR 1024.15) requires written disclosure at or before the time of referral. Agents who own an interest in a title company and fail to disclose face both federal RESPA penalties and MREC disciplinary action.

Mistake four: acting as the conduit for wire instructions without verification. Mississippi has seen a 40 percent increase in wire fraud attempts targeting real estate transactions since 2022, according to the [Mississippi Secretary of State's Cybersecurity Division](https://www.sos.ms.gov). Agents who forward wire instructions without confirming them through a verified callback procedure share liability when funds disappear. The proper protocol is to let the closing attorney or title company deliver wire instructions directly to the parties.

Mistake five: skipping the closing disclosure review deadline. Under TRID rules (12 CFR 1026.19(f)), the buyer must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before consummation. Agents who pressure closings into an accelerated timeline — pushing the title company or attorney to issue the CD late — can trigger a mandatory delay that blows up the contract's closing date, exposing the buyer to default.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce in 2026

Brokers carry supervising responsibility under MREC Rule 1.6, which requires the designated broker to maintain policies and procedures ensuring all licensees comply with Mississippi real estate law. That obligation is not theoretical — MREC's 2025 audit cycle flagged 14 brokerages for inadequate closing file documentation, resulting in consent agreements ranging from $2,500 to $10,000.

**Every Mississippi brokerage should have a written policy specifying which closing functions the agent may perform and which must be referred to an attorney.** This policy should explicitly prohibit agents from drafting legal documents, providing title interpretation, or recommending that buyers waive attorney representation.

Brokers should audit the following in every file: confirmation that an attorney prepared the deed and deed of trust, a copy of the title commitment with attorney opinion letter, evidence that the Closing Disclosure was delivered within the TRID three-day window, and documentation of any affiliated business arrangement disclosures. If the transaction closed through a title company without an attorney, the broker must verify that no document preparation or legal advisory function was performed by unlicensed personnel.

The [MREC website](https://www.mrec.ms.gov) publishes quarterly disciplinary orders. Brokers who review these orders will notice a recurring theme: agents who assumed the title company "handled everything" and brokers who never checked. A simple file audit checklist — reviewed weekly — eliminates most exposure. For brokerages scaling beyond 20 to 30 transactions a month, manual audits become unsustainable, which is exactly where [automated compliance tracking](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) outperforms memory-based systems.

## How This Differs From Other States Agents May Know

Agents licensed in multiple states or relocating from other markets often assume Mississippi works like their previous state. It does not.

| State | Attorney Required at Closing? | Title Company Can Close Alone? | Deed Preparation |
|-------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|------------------|
| Mississippi | Not by statute, but attorney must prepare legal documents | Can facilitate signing/disbursement only | Attorney only |
| Wisconsin | No | Yes | Attorney or title company |
| Alabama | Yes (attorney must supervise) | No | Attorney only |
| Tennessee | No | Yes | Attorney or title company |
| Texas | No | Yes (escrow officer) | Attorney or title company |

Mississippi's hybrid approach is closest to Alabama's but without a statutory mandate for attorney supervision at the closing table itself. The distinction matters: an attorney must prepare the documents, but a title company representative can oversee the signing ceremony if no legal interpretation arises during the appointment. For agents working [multi-state transactions or comparing compliance models](/blog/wisconsin-attorney-vs-title-company-2026), understanding this nuance prevents costly assumptions.

## Practical Workflow: How to Structure a Compliant Mississippi Closing

Step one: at contract execution, the listing agent and buyer's agent confirm whether the parties will use a closing attorney, a title company, or both. The MAR Form 101, Section 12, provides a blank for identifying the closing agent. Fill it in at offer — not three days before closing.

Step two: the closing attorney orders a title search (or the title company orders it and sends the commitment to the attorney for opinion). The attorney reviews exceptions, prepares the deed and deed of trust, and drafts any corrective instruments needed to clear title.

Step three: the title company issues the title insurance commitment based on the attorney's opinion, sets up escrow, and prepares the Closing Disclosure in coordination with the lender. If the attorney is also acting as the title agent (common in Mississippi), these functions collapse into one office.

Step four: at closing, the attorney (or the attorney's designee) explains documents requiring legal interpretation. The title company representative handles escrow math and disbursement. The agent's role is limited to confirming commission instructions, ensuring the buyer and seller complete any contractual obligations (like delivering keys or transfer of warranties), and verifying that [deadlines have been met throughout the transaction](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).
