## Understanding South Carolina Buyer Agency Agreement Requirements 2026

The South Carolina buyer agency agreement requirements 2026 represent one of the most significant practice shifts agents in the state have faced since the SC Real Estate Commission last overhauled its disclosure rules. Every licensed agent operating in South Carolina must now obtain a signed written buyer representation agreement before delivering substantive brokerage services to a prospective buyer. This is not optional guidance from your brokerage—it is a regulatory expectation backed by state statute and reinforced by the fallout from the NAR settlement.

The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC), operating under the authority of [SC Code Title 40, Chapter 57](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php), has made its position clear through advisory opinions and bulletins issued throughout 2025. Agents who fail to obtain written agreements face disciplinary proceedings, loss of compensation claims, and personal liability exposure that no errors-and-omissions policy will cover willingly.

This article breaks down the specific forms, statutory requirements, enforcement mechanisms, and operational mistakes that trip up even experienced agents.

## Which Forms Satisfy the Written Agreement Requirement

South Carolina REALTORS (SCR) publishes the standard forms most agents and brokerages use statewide. The primary buyer agency agreement form is **SCR 510**, the Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement. This document establishes the agent as the buyer's sole representative for a defined period and geographic scope, and it requires explicit disclosure of how the agent will be compensated.

For agents who want a non-exclusive arrangement—or for buyers unwilling to commit exclusively—**SCR 511** (Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement) satisfies the written agreement requirement while allowing the buyer to work with multiple agents simultaneously. A third option, **SCR 512** (Single-Property Showing Agreement), covers situations where a buyer wants representation for a specific property tour without broader commitment.

| Form Number | Agreement Type | Duration Flexibility | Compensation Clause Required |
|-------------|---------------|---------------------|------------------------------|
| SCR 510 | Exclusive Buyer Agency | Negotiable (typically 90-180 days) | Yes |
| SCR 511 | Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency | Negotiable | Yes |
| SCR 512 | Single-Property Showing | One showing/one property | Yes |
| Custom Brokerage Form | Varies | Must meet SCREC minimums | Yes |

Brokerages are permitted to use their own proprietary forms, but those documents must contain every material term the SCREC requires. Missing even one mandatory element—such as a clear statement of compensation or the agreement's expiration date—renders the form deficient and exposes the agent to the same liability as having no agreement at all.

## Statutory Basis and Regulatory Authority

**SC Code Section 40-57-145 governs the duties of licensees and establishes the requirement for written agreements that define the brokerage relationship.** The statute mandates that before a licensee provides brokerage services on behalf of a buyer, the terms of representation must be reduced to writing and signed by both parties. This is not new language, but the SCREC's enforcement posture has sharpened dramatically since 2024.

Section 40-57-350 gives the Commission broad authority to discipline licensees who violate any provision of the License Law, including the written agreement requirement. Penalties range from a formal reprimand to license revocation, with monetary fines authorized up to $1,000 per violation under Section 40-57-360.

The Commission also references SC Regulation 105-11, which further defines brokerage relationships and the disclosure obligations that accompany them. Under this regulation, the relationship disclosure form (SCR 523) must be presented at first substantive contact—and the buyer agency agreement must follow before services begin.

> "A licensee shall not perform any acts that constitute brokerage services for a buyer without a written agreement specifying the terms of compensation and the scope of representation." — SC Code Section 40-57-145(A)

The distinction matters: the disclosure form (SCR 523) tells the buyer what types of relationships exist in South Carolina. The buyer agency agreement (SCR 510, 511, or 512) actually creates the relationship. Agents who confuse these two steps—or who believe the disclosure form alone suffices—are making a compliance error that carries real consequences.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of operating without a signed buyer agency agreement fall into three categories: regulatory, financial, and transactional. Understanding all three is essential because they can stack on top of each other in a single complaint.

**Regulatory penalties** include formal complaints filed with the SCREC, which can trigger an investigation under Section 40-57-350. If the Commission finds a violation, the agent faces sanctions ranging from mandatory continuing education to license suspension. For repeat offenders or egregious cases involving consumer harm, the Commission has authority to revoke a license entirely.

**Financial exposure** extends beyond Commission fines. An agent without a signed buyer agreement has no enforceable claim to compensation. If a buyer completes a purchase and disputes the commission, the agent lacks the contractual basis to recover fees. South Carolina courts have consistently held that oral agreements for brokerage compensation are unenforceable under the Statute of Frauds (SC Code Section 32-3-10), meaning verbal promises from buyers carry no legal weight.

**Transactional risk** is the category most agents underestimate. A listing agent or seller who discovers the buyer's agent lacks a proper agreement can challenge the cooperation offer, refuse to pay the buyer-side compensation, or raise the deficiency during a dispute over earnest money or contract performance. These situations create deal-killing complications that harm your client and your reputation simultaneously.

| Consequence Category | Specific Risk | Who Initiates |
|---------------------|---------------|---------------|
| Regulatory | License suspension, fine up to $1,000/violation | SCREC |
| Financial | Loss of compensation claim | Buyer, seller, or cooperating broker |
| Transactional | Deal cancellation, earnest money dispute | Any party to the transaction |
| Civil Liability | Breach of fiduciary duty lawsuit | Buyer-client |

Agents who treat the buyer agreement as a formality rather than a foundational document are gambling with their livelihood. The post-NAR-settlement environment means more scrutiny from all directions—consumers, cooperating agents, and regulators alike.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Buyer Agency Agreements

Five errors appear repeatedly in SCREC disciplinary actions and brokerage compliance audits. Each one is preventable with proper training and process discipline.

**First, agents present the agreement too late.** The most frequent violation is showing property—sometimes multiple properties over several weeks—before obtaining a signature. The standard is clear: the agreement must be signed before substantive services begin. An open house visit where the agent provides generic information may not trigger the requirement, but the moment you schedule a private showing or begin sending targeted listings, you need ink on paper.

**Second, agents leave the compensation field vague or blank.** SCR 510 and 511 both require a specific statement of how the agent will be compensated. Writing "per MLS" or "standard commission" is insufficient. The form must state a dollar amount or percentage, and it must explain whether compensation comes from the seller, the buyer, or a combination. Ambiguity here is the single fastest path to a compensation dispute.

**Third, agents fail to specify a termination date.** Every buyer agency agreement must include an expiration date. Open-ended agreements violate SCREC expectations and expose agents to claims of overreaching. If you need to extend the relationship, execute an amendment or a new agreement—do not leave the end date blank.

**Fourth, agents neglect to provide a signed copy to the buyer.** SC Code Section 40-57-145 requires that the buyer receive a copy of the executed agreement. Failure to deliver a copy is itself a violation, even if the agreement was otherwise properly executed.

**Fifth, agents confuse the relationship disclosure (SCR 523) with the buyer agency agreement.** These are separate documents serving separate legal functions. Presenting SCR 523 does not create a buyer agency relationship—it merely informs the consumer about available relationship types. The [same confusion trips up agents in other states](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026), and the consequences are equally severe regardless of jurisdiction.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry supervisory responsibility under SC Code Section 40-57-145(B) and SCREC Regulation 105-7. If an affiliated agent operates without proper buyer agreements, the broker faces independent disciplinary exposure—not just vicarious liability through the agent.

**Every brokerage should maintain a system that verifies a signed buyer agency agreement exists before any showing is scheduled or any offer is drafted.** This is not a suggestion—it is the minimum standard of supervision the SCREC expects from brokers-in-charge. Quarterly file audits are insufficient if agents can operate for weeks without agreements on file.

Brokers should verify these five elements in every buyer file: the agreement is signed by both buyer and agent, the compensation terms are specific and complete, the termination date is filled in, the buyer received a copy (documented by signature or delivery confirmation), and the agreement predates any substantive services rendered.

Transaction management systems help enforce these checkpoints, but many brokerages still rely on agents to self-report compliance—a model that [breaks down predictably](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) when agents are juggling multiple clients. Automated compliance verification reduces the broker's exposure and creates an auditable trail for any future SCREC inquiry.

Brokers should also track agreement expirations. An agent who continues providing services after a buyer agreement expires is operating without authorization—functionally the same violation as never having an agreement at all. Calendar-based alerts for approaching expiration dates prevent this silent compliance failure.

## Handling Buyer Objections Without Sacrificing Compliance

Some agents avoid presenting buyer agreements early because they fear losing prospects. This fear is understandable but cannot override legal requirements. The agent who skips the agreement to seem more accommodating is the agent who later cannot collect a commission and may face a licensing complaint.

**Frame the agreement as a protection for the buyer, not a trap.** The document guarantees the buyer specific services, establishes fiduciary duties, and creates accountability. Without it, the buyer has no contractual right to loyalty, confidentiality, or diligent representation. Most buyers, when presented with this framing, sign willingly.

For reluctant buyers, SCR 512 (the single-property showing agreement) offers a low-commitment entry point. It covers one property at a time and carries no long-term obligation. Once the buyer sees the value of professional representation, converting to SCR 510 or 511 becomes a natural next step.

Agents who track [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) over time will notice that compensation disputes and representation confusion appear frequently in failed transactions. The buyer agency agreement eliminates both of those failure points by establishing clear expectations before anyone walks through a front door.
