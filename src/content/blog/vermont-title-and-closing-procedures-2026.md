Vermont handles real estate closings differently than most of its New England neighbors, and misunderstanding the process can stall a deal or expose an agent to liability. If you are active in the state this year, a firm grasp of **Vermont title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional — it is the baseline for staying compliant and keeping transactions on schedule. This guide breaks down the attorney requirement, the forms you will encounter, the mistakes that sink closings, and the audit responsibilities brokers carry.

## Vermont Is an Attorney-Closing State — No Exceptions

Unlike states where title companies run the show, Vermont requires attorney involvement at closing. The Vermont Supreme Court's [Administrative Order 42](https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/sites/default/files/documents/PROMULGATED-Amended%20AO%2042.pdf) and the Vermont Rules of Professional Conduct establish that preparing deeds, conducting title examinations, and overseeing the transfer of funds constitute the practice of law. A title insurance company or real estate agent cannot perform these functions independently.

**This means the closing attorney — not the agent, not the lender — controls the closing table.** The attorney reviews the title search, resolves encumbrances, prepares the warranty deed or quitclaim deed, and ensures recording happens with the appropriate town clerk's office. Agents who try to shortcut this process by directing title work to unlicensed parties risk violating the unauthorized practice of law statutes under 26 V.S.A. Chapter 59.

The practical implication for agents is scheduling. You need to confirm that both buyer and seller have retained or been assigned a closing attorney early — ideally within the first week after contract execution. Rural Vermont towns sometimes have limited recording hours and a single town clerk, which can add days to the timeline.

## Key Vermont Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Vermont transactions involve a mix of state-specific and standardized forms. The Vermont Association of Realtors (VAR) publishes the primary contract forms, while state agencies mandate tax and disclosure documents. Knowing which form goes where keeps agents out of trouble.

The standard purchase and sale agreement is the **VAR Form 200**, which includes contingency addenda for financing, inspection, and appraisal. Agents must also be familiar with the **VAR Form 300 (Lead Paint Disclosure)** for properties built before 1978 and the **VAR Form 600 (Property Disclosure)** required under 27 V.S.A. Section 377. Failure to deliver the property disclosure form before contract execution gives the buyer a right to rescind — a deal-killing oversight that happens more often than it should.

On the tax side, the **PT-172 (Vermont Property Transfer Tax Return)** must be filed with the Vermont Department of Taxes within 30 days of closing. The closing attorney typically prepares this, but agents should verify it is on the checklist. The transfer tax itself is split between buyer and seller at rates defined under 32 V.S.A. Section 9602, as shown below.

| Party | First $100,000 | Amount Over $100,000 | Principal Residence Exemption |
|-------|----------------|----------------------|-------------------------------|
| Seller | 1.25% | 1.45% | None |
| Buyer | 0.50% | 1.45% | 0.50% applies only to first $100,000 for principal residence buyers |
| Clean Water Surcharge | 0.2% of purchase price | Same rate on full price | No exemption |

The Clean Water Surcharge, added under Act 76, applies to all transfers and is included on the PT-172. Agents who fail to disclose these costs in the buyer's estimated closing statement create last-minute surprises that erode trust and delay settlements.

## Title Searches and Insurance: How Vermont Differs

**Vermont's land records system is town-based, not county-based, which creates a unique title search environment.** Each of the state's 255 municipalities maintains its own land records through the town clerk's office. There is no centralized county recorder. This decentralized system means title searches can take longer in towns with older, less digitized records.

The closing attorney or a title abstractor working under attorney supervision performs the title search. Standard practice is a 40-year search, though some attorneys go back further when the chain of title is unclear. Title insurance is not legally required in Vermont, but virtually every lender mandates a lender's policy. Agents should always recommend that buyers obtain an owner's policy as well, since the title search alone does not protect against undiscovered defects.

Vermont recognizes title insurance under 8 V.S.A. Chapter 101, and insurers must be licensed by the Vermont Department of Financial Regulation. Agents sometimes confuse a title opinion letter — which an attorney may provide — with title insurance. They are not the same thing. A title opinion is the attorney's professional judgment about the state of the title. Title insurance is an indemnification product that pays claims if a covered defect surfaces later. Making this distinction clear to buyers is part of your fiduciary duty.

## Common Mistakes Vermont Agents Make at Closing

Experienced agents still trip over procedural details that are unique to Vermont or easy to overlook under deadline pressure. Here are five errors that show up repeatedly in disciplinary files and deal post-mortems.

**First, failing to confirm the closing attorney early enough.** Vermont's attorney requirement means there is no fallback if the attorney is unavailable. Agents who wait until week three of a 30-day contract to coordinate attorney involvement often find themselves requesting extensions. The Vermont Real Estate Commission, which operates under the Office of Professional Regulation (OPR), expects agents to demonstrate competence in managing transaction timelines per 26 V.S.A. Section 2296.

Second, mishandling earnest money deposits. Vermont requires earnest money to be deposited into a designated escrow account — typically the listing broker's trust account or the closing attorney's IOLTA account — within the timeframe specified in the contract. Agents who hold checks in desk drawers or delay deposits violate 26 V.S.A. Section 2299 and face disciplinary action, including license suspension. For more on how escrow missteps derail transactions, read this post on [where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Third, neglecting to verify property tax proration accuracy. Vermont's fiscal year varies by municipality — some run July 1 to June 30, others April 1 to March 31. Agents who assume a uniform fiscal year produce inaccurate closing estimates. The closing attorney handles the final proration, but agents who provide wildly wrong estimates on the front end damage their credibility and create friction at the closing table.

Fourth, skipping the well and septic disclosures. Vermont's Agency of Natural Resources requires disclosure of private water supply and wastewater system status. The **Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Permit** (state permit) must be confirmed for properties not on municipal systems. Agents in rural markets who ignore this create title clouds and inspection failures.

Fifth, failing to disclose agency relationships properly. Vermont mandates written agency disclosure before any confidential information is exchanged, per 26 V.S.A. Section 2297. The VAR Agency Disclosure Form must be signed and retained. Agents who treat this as a formality and skip it face complaints to the OPR.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability for every transaction their agents touch. The Vermont Real Estate Commission holds brokers accountable under 26 V.S.A. Section 2295 for ensuring that agents comply with state law, commission rules, and contractual obligations. This is not a passive role.

| Audit Area | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Escrow deposits | Deposit timing matches contract terms; funds in designated account | Every transaction |
| Agency disclosures | Signed VAR Agency Disclosure Form in file | Every transaction |
| Property disclosures | VAR Form 600 delivered before contract execution | Every transaction |
| Closing attorney coordination | Attorney retained and title search ordered within first week | Every transaction |
| PT-172 filing | Confirmation that transfer tax return filed within 30 days | Monthly review |

**Brokers who rely on agents to self-report compliance are setting themselves up for enforcement actions.** The OPR's investigation process, triggered by consumer complaints or audit findings, can result in fines, license suspension, or revocation for both the agent and the supervising broker. A structured file review after each closing is the minimum standard.

Brokers should also verify that agents are not providing legal advice about title defects or deed preparation. The line between explaining a process and practicing law is thin in Vermont, and crossing it exposes the brokerage to unauthorized practice of law claims. When agents encounter title issues, the correct response is always to direct the client to the closing attorney. ## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences for procedural failures in Vermont are not theoretical. The Vermont Real Estate Commission has authority under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296 to impose disciplinary sanctions ranging from written warnings to license revocation. Fines can reach $1,000 per violation, and the commission can order restitution to harmed consumers.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer who discovers an undisclosed title defect or a missed property condition disclosure can sue for damages. Vermont courts have held that agents owe a duty of care that includes accurate representation of property conditions and proper handling of transaction documents. Errors and omissions insurance covers some of this exposure, but intentional omissions — like knowingly skipping a required disclosure — fall outside most E&O policies.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. A buyer who does not receive the VAR Form 600 property disclosure before signing the purchase agreement can void the contract. A seller who discovers that the agent misrepresented the transfer tax obligation may refuse to close. These are not edge cases. They are the predictable outcomes of sloppy compliance practices, and understanding [why agents frequently miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) can help you avoid them.
