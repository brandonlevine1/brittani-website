## Arkansas New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Statutory Changes That Matter

The Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) finalized a package of rule amendments and the state legislature passed Act 412 and Act 587, both of which take effect July 1, 2026. Together, these changes represent the most significant overhaul to Arkansas residential transaction requirements since the 2019 disclosure reform. If you hold an active license in this state, the phrase **Arkansas new real estate laws 2026** should already be triggering audit conversations between you and your principal broker.

This is not a cosmetic update. The new laws alter disclosure timing, increase monetary penalties for noncompliance, mandate a new continuing education module, and introduce wire fraud prevention duties that did not previously exist in Arkansas statute. Agents who treat this as background noise risk fines, license suspension, and deal-killing liability exposure.

## Revised Property Condition Disclosure: AREC Form 5-2026

**Act 412 amends Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-107 to expand the seller's property condition disclosure from 23 line items to 31.** The eight new items include questions about previous flooding events (regardless of whether the property sits in a FEMA-designated flood zone), the presence of polybutylene piping, known foundation movement, and whether the property has ever been used as a methamphetamine production site.

AREC released the updated [Form 5-2026](https://www.arec.arkansas.gov) to replace the prior Form 5-2019. Sellers must complete the new version for any listing agreement executed on or after July 1, 2026. Transactions already under contract before that date may proceed with the old form, but any extension or amendment that pushes the closing past September 30, 2026 will trigger the new form requirement retroactively.

Agents who continue using the outdated Form 5-2019 after the effective date expose their sellers to potential rescission claims from buyers, because an incomplete disclosure under the new statute does not satisfy the legal obligation. That distinction matters when earnest money disputes land in front of a judge.

## Agency Disclosure Timing Gets Tighter

Under the prior framework, Arkansas required agents to disclose their agency relationship before showing property to a prospective buyer. Act 587 moves that trigger point forward to "first substantive contact," which AREC defines in Rule 10.15 as any conversation—phone, text, email, video, or in-person—where specific property details, pricing, or client needs are discussed.

This means the agency disclosure (AREC Form 1) must be presented before or during the first real conversation, not at the doorstep of a showing. The practical impact hits buyer's agents hardest: that initial phone call where a lead asks about a listing they found online now counts as substantive contact.

**Failure to deliver Form 1 at the correct time exposes the agent to a fine of up to $2,500 per occurrence and gives the consumer a 72-hour rescission window after learning of the violation.** AREC has publicly stated it will treat repeated violations as a pattern warranting license suspension under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-305(d).

| Requirement | Before July 1, 2026 | After July 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Agency disclosure trigger | Before showing property | First substantive contact |
| Form version required | AREC Form 1 (2020) | AREC Form 1-2026 |
| Penalty for late delivery | Up to $1,000 fine | Up to $2,500 fine + rescission right |
| Consumer rescission window | None specified | 72 hours after discovery |
| Broker audit obligation | Annual file review | Quarterly file review |

The shift from annual to quarterly broker file review is buried in AREC Rule 10.16 but carries real consequences. Brokers who cannot demonstrate quarterly audits during a Commission investigation face separate administrative penalties.

## Wire Fraud Prevention Duties: AREC Rule 10.18

Arkansas now joins a growing list of states that impose affirmative duties on licensees to prevent wire fraud in real estate closings. AREC Rule 10.18, effective July 1, 2026, requires listing agents and buyer's agents to independently verify wire transfer instructions by calling the title company or closing attorney at a known phone number—not one provided in an email.

The rule does not make agents liable for every instance of wire fraud. But it does create a per se violation if an agent forwards wire instructions to a client without first calling to confirm them. The penalty structure starts at a written reprimand for a first offense with no actual loss, escalating to a $5,000 fine and mandatory 30-day suspension if a client suffers financial harm.

Arkansas is catching up to what several commission-level bodies have already implemented.

## Mandatory Continuing Education: The New 3-Hour Module

**Every active licensee must complete a 3-hour continuing education course titled "2026 Disclosure and Consumer Protection Updates" before their next renewal date on or after January 1, 2027.** This is a one-time mandatory module, not a recurring requirement, and it does not count toward the existing 6-hour annual CE obligation.

AREC-approved providers began offering the course in May 2026. The Commission has confirmed that online, self-paced completion qualifies. Agents who fail to complete the course by their renewal date will be placed on inactive status without a grace period—a departure from the 30-day grace AREC previously allowed for standard CE shortfalls.

The course covers the new Form 5-2026 line items, the substantive contact standard for agency disclosure, wire fraud prevention obligations, and updated guidance on dual agency documentation under AREC Form 6. Skipping it is not an option, and "I didn't know about it" will not restore your license once it lapses.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

Based on how prior regulatory changes played out in Arkansas and similar states, these are the most likely compliance failures AREC will encounter in the first 12 months.

**First: using the wrong form version.** This sounds basic, but after the 2019 form update, AREC reported that 14 percent of audited files in the first six months contained the outdated disclosure form. Agents who rely on pre-populated templates in their CRM or transaction management software must verify that their systems have been updated. A stale template equals a statutory violation, full stop.

Second: treating text messages as exempt from the "first substantive contact" standard. AREC's definition explicitly includes text and direct message exchanges. If a buyer texts you asking "What's the HOA fee on 412 Elm Street?" and you respond with specifics, that is substantive contact. Form 1-2026 must be delivered before or simultaneously with your reply.

Third: forwarding wire instructions via email without calling to verify. This habit is deeply embedded in transaction workflows. The rule does not require agents to handle wire coordination directly—but if you touch those instructions at all, you must have called first. Many agents will learn this the hard way when a complaint file lands on their desk.

Fourth: failing to document the date and method of agency disclosure delivery. Under the new quarterly audit requirement, brokers need a paper trail showing exactly when Form 1-2026 was delivered and how. Agents who "remember giving it to them at some point" will not survive an audit. Electronic delivery with a timestamp is the safest path.

Fifth: ignoring the retroactive Form 5-2026 requirement for extended transactions. If your transaction was listed before July 1 but closes after September 30 due to an extension, the new disclosure form applies. This catches deals with construction contingencies, delayed closings, and short-sale approvals more than typical residential transactions.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce Starting Q3 2026

AREC Rule 10.16 now mandates that principal brokers conduct file audits on a quarterly basis rather than annually. The Commission has stated that audits must verify five specific elements in every closed and pending file.

| Audit Element | What Brokers Must Verify | Consequence of Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Agency disclosure timing | Form 1-2026 delivered at or before first substantive contact, with date stamp | File deficiency; $1,000 broker fine per occurrence |
| Property disclosure version | Form 5-2026 used for all transactions listed after July 1 | Statutory violation attributed to supervising broker |
| Wire instruction verification | Agent's call log or CRM note confirming phone verification | Written warning first offense; $2,500 fine for pattern |
| Dual agency documentation | Form 6 signed by all parties before any substantive negotiation | Transaction voidable; possible license action |
| CE compliance tracking | Verification that all agents under supervision completed 3-hour module | Broker responsible for allowing unlicensed activity |

Brokers who run teams with high transaction volume should consider automating these audit checks. Relying on manual file review across 50 or 100 transactions per quarter is a recipe for missed items—and AREC has signaled it will hold brokers personally accountable for supervisory failures under Ark. Code Ann. 17-42-311.

## Penalties and Liability Exposure: What Noncompliance Actually Costs

The financial risk profile for noncompliance increased substantially under the 2026 amendments. Previously, most AREC fine authority topped out at $1,000 per violation. Act 412 raised the ceiling to $5,000 for disclosure-related violations and Act 587 introduced the $2,500 tier for agency disclosure timing failures.

Beyond fines, the rescission rights are what should keep agents up at night. A buyer who discovers a late agency disclosure now has a statutory 72-hour right to cancel the transaction. A buyer who receives an incomplete or outdated property condition disclosure can seek rescission and damages under Ark. Code Ann. 18-44-104, which the 2026 amendments explicitly cross-reference.

**Professional liability insurers in Arkansas have already begun issuing guidance that claims arising from 2026 disclosure failures may not be covered under standard E&O policies if the agent's noncompliance was knowing or reckless.** Check your policy language. If your E&O excludes "willful regulatory violations," using the wrong form version after July 1 could mean you are personally liable with no insurance backstop.

The agents most at risk are those managing high volumes without reliable compliance systems. If you are handling 30 or more transactions per year and still tracking deadlines in your head or in a basic spreadsheet, the [reasons agents miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) have not changed—but the consequences just got significantly more expensive.
