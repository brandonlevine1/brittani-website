## Understanding Connecticut Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Every Connecticut residential closing involves a web of state and municipal fees that directly affect your client's bottom line. Miscalculating the Connecticut transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 rates does not just embarrass you at the closing table — it can delay recording, trigger penalties from the Department of Revenue Services (DRS), and expose your brokerage to liability claims. This guide breaks down the exact statutory rates, required forms, and compliance traps that Connecticut-licensed agents and brokers need to master this year.

Connecticut is an attorney-closing state, which means the title attorney typically handles the tax filings. That does not absolve the listing or buyer's agent from understanding the numbers. Your clients will ask you to explain line items on the settlement statement, and your broker expects you to catch errors before they become complaints.

## Conveyance Tax Rates and Statutory Authority

The Connecticut real estate conveyance tax is governed by Chapter 223 of the Connecticut General Statutes (CGS), specifically Sections 12-494 through 12-504. The tax applies to every transfer of real property unless a specific exemption under CGS Section 12-498 applies.

**The 2026 rate structure has not changed from the revised schedule that took effect in 2020, but the mansion tax surcharge continues to catch agents off guard.** The base rate is 0.75% of the sale price for most residential transfers. Municipalities do not collect a separate local transfer tax — the 0.75% is split between the state (0.50%) and the municipality (0.25%).

For properties selling above $2.5 million, sellers face the additional surcharge of 2.25% on the amount exceeding that threshold, established under Public Act 19-117. A $3 million sale therefore triggers 0.75% on the full $3 million plus 2.25% on the $500,000 excess. This is not an academic concern for agents working Fairfield County's Gold Coast or certain Hartford-area luxury listings.

| Transaction Price | Base Rate (State + Municipal) | Mansion Surcharge | Effective Tax on Full Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| $400,000 | 0.75% = $3,000 | None | $3,000 |
| $1,000,000 | 0.75% = $7,500 | None | $7,500 |
| $2,500,000 | 0.75% = $18,750 | None | $18,750 |
| $3,000,000 | 0.75% = $22,500 | 2.25% on $500K = $11,250 | $33,750 |
| $5,000,000 | 0.75% = $37,500 | 2.25% on $2.5M = $56,250 | $93,750 |

Sellers relocating out of state face an additional wrinkle. CGS Section 12-498(b) requires withholding of the estimated conveyance tax at the time of closing if the seller is a nonresident, unless they obtain a DRS clearance certificate.

## Recording Fees: Town Clerk Schedules

Connecticut recording fees are set at the state level but collected by each of the 169 town clerks. Under [CGS Section 7-34a](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_090.htm#sec_7-34a), the fee schedule applies uniformly across all municipalities.

The current recording fee structure is $60 for the first page of any document submitted for recording, plus $5 for each additional page. A separate land record surcharge of $40 per document applies. So the minimum cost to record a single-page warranty deed is $100. Most deeds run two to three pages, putting the typical recording cost between $105 and $110 per document.

**Agents frequently forget that the recording fees multiply across documents.** A routine closing might involve a warranty deed, a mortgage deed, and a release of the prior mortgage — each carrying its own first-page fee and surcharge. Budget accordingly on your net sheet estimates.

| Document | Pages (Typical) | Recording Fee | Surcharge | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warranty Deed | 2 | $65 | $40 | $105 |
| Mortgage Deed | 4-6 | $75-$85 | $40 | $115-$125 |
| Mortgage Release | 1 | $60 | $40 | $100 |
| Assignment of Mortgage | 2 | $65 | $40 | $105 |

Town clerks will reject documents that are not formatted to their recording standards — margins too small, signatures missing, or the conveyance tax return not attached. Each rejection means a return trip and a delayed recording date, which can shift priority and create title insurance issues.

## Required Forms: OP-236, RCSA, and Related Documents

The primary tax form agents must know is the Connecticut Real Estate Conveyance Tax Return, Form OP-236. This form must be completed and signed by both the grantor and grantee (or their attorneys) and submitted to the town clerk at the time of recording. The town clerk will not accept a deed for recording without a properly executed OP-236 attached.

Form OP-236 requires disclosure of the sale price, the exemption claimed (if any), the tax calculated, and identifying information for both parties. The DRS periodically updates the form instructions, so using a prior-year version can result in rejection. The current version as of 2026 is available on the [DRS website](https://portal.ct.gov/DRS/Forms/Current-Forms).

For transactions involving a controlling interest transfer — where an entity holding Connecticut real property changes hands instead of the real property itself — Form AU-330 applies under CGS Section 12-638. These transactions are more common than many residential agents realize, particularly when LLC-held investment properties change ownership by membership interest assignment rather than deed.

Agents should also be aware of the RCSA (Regulations of Connecticut State Agencies) Section 12-494-1, which provides guidance on valuation disputes and contested assessments. If DRS believes the stated consideration on the OP-236 is below fair market value, they can assess additional tax plus penalties.

## Common Mistakes Connecticut Agents Make

**Mistake one: quoting the wrong rate to luxury sellers.** Agents listing properties near or above the $2.5 million threshold often quote a flat 0.75% on the full price, failing to disclose the mansion surcharge. When the seller sees an unexpected $11,000-plus line item at closing, it creates a trust problem — and sometimes a demand for the agent to make up the difference.

Mistake two: confusing who owes the tax. The statute places the obligation on the grantor (seller), but agents sometimes tell buyers they owe nothing toward recording costs. Buyers pay recording fees on their own mortgage documents and often split other recording charges by contract. Misrepresenting these allocations on a net sheet can constitute a material misstatement under the Connecticut Real Estate Commission's advertising and disclosure standards.

Mistake three: failing to flag exempt transactions correctly. Transfers between spouses, transfers pursuant to court order in a dissolution, and transfers to or from a trust where the grantor is the sole beneficiary are common exemptions under CGS Section 12-498. Agents who do not alert the closing attorney to these facts leave money on the table for their clients or, worse, cause the attorney to pay tax that was not owed.

Mistake four: using outdated recording fee estimates. The $40 surcharge under CGS 7-34a was a relatively recent addition, and some agents still work from older templates showing $60 flat per document. When the settlement statement comes in higher than your estimated net sheet, your credibility takes the hit. If you are building your own [checklists to track these details](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), make sure the fee figures match the current statute.

Mistake five: missing the nonresident withholding requirement. Out-of-state sellers trigger a withholding obligation. If the closing attorney does not withhold and remit, DRS can assess the tax against the buyer — who then has a claim against the seller and potentially against the agents who failed to flag the issue.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory responsibility under CGS Section 20-328-5a for the acts of their agents. A pattern of incorrect net sheets or missed tax disclosures does not just harm clients — it creates regulatory exposure for the brokerage during a Connecticut Real Estate Commission audit.

**Every brokerage should maintain a standardized net sheet template that reflects current 2026 rates and is locked against unauthorized edits.** Agents should not be calculating conveyance taxes by hand on scratch paper. The template should auto-flag any transaction above $2.5 million for the surcharge calculation and include a field confirming whether the seller is a Connecticut resident.

Brokers should audit a random sample of closed transaction files each quarter, checking that the OP-236 form in the file matches the sale price on the executed contract. Discrepancies between the contract price and the OP-236 stated consideration are a red flag for DRS audit selection — and for potential fraud allegations that implicate the entire office.

Training is the other enforcement lever. At least once per year, brokers should require agents to review the current DRS rate schedule and recording fee structure. This is not a suggestion — under RCSA Section 20-328-2a, brokers must ensure that affiliated licensees maintain competence in the transaction practices of their market area. Transfer tax compliance falls squarely within that mandate.

For brokerages handling high volumes, automating compliance checks can prevent the [kind of deadline failures](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that lead to recording gaps. A deed recorded one day late can lose priority to an intervening lien — a catastrophic outcome for the buyer and a potential E&O claim for everyone involved.

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

The DRS imposes a penalty of 10% of the unpaid conveyance tax for late filing, plus interest at the statutory rate (currently 1% per month under CGS Section 12-35). These penalties apply from the date the deed should have been recorded, not from when anyone notices the error.

Beyond DRS penalties, agents face professional discipline. The Connecticut Real Estate Commission can sanction licensees under CGS Section 20-320(a) for negligence, incompetence, or conduct prejudicial to the public interest. A pattern of conveyance tax errors fits all three categories. Sanctions range from a letter of reprimand to license suspension.

Civil liability is the third rail. If an agent's incorrect fee estimate causes a seller to accept a contract that nets less than expected, or if a buyer faces a lien because a deed was not recorded due to form rejection, the resulting claims go beyond E&O insurance deductibles. Deal cancellations tied to late recording or surprise tax assessments tend to produce both formal complaints and social media damage that no brokerage wants.
