## Understanding Maryland Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The regulatory framework governing real estate commissions in Maryland underwent significant updates heading into 2026, and agents who fail to track these changes risk losing deals and facing disciplinary action. Maryland commission & licensing rules 2026 now demand tighter documentation, clearer disclosures, and more rigorous broker oversight than at any point in the last decade. The Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC), operating under the Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing within the Maryland Department of Labor, enforces these standards through audits, complaints, and license renewals.

Maryland's statutory foundation sits in the Business Occupations and Professions Article, Title 17 of the Annotated Code of Maryland. COMAR Title 09, Subtitle 11 contains the implementing regulations that dictate day-to-day compliance for agents and brokers. If you operated under the assumption that the 2024 NAR settlement only affected MLS rules, the state-level fallout has landed squarely on your required paperwork.

## Written Commission Agreements: The New Non-Negotiable

**Every Maryland licensee must execute a written brokerage agreement before providing services that go beyond ministerial acts.** This requirement, codified in Section 17-530 of the Business Occupations and Professions Article, applies to both buyer representation and listing agreements. The era of handshake arrangements and verbal understandings about commission splits ended definitively in 2024, and MREC enforcement actions in 2025 confirmed the state takes violations seriously.

For buyer representation, agents must use or substantially mirror the Maryland Association of REALTORS (MAR) Form 1101 — the Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement. This form specifies compensation amount or methodology, duration of the agreement, and the scope of services provided. Listing agents continue to use MAR Form 100 (Exclusive Right to Sell) or Form 104 (Exclusive Agency), both of which must contain unambiguous commission terms.

The critical detail many agents miss: the compensation field cannot reference an MLS offer of cooperation as the sole source of buyer-agent payment. The agreement must state a specific amount or percentage the buyer is responsible for, even if offset by seller concessions. MREC issued Guidance Bulletin 2025-03 clarifying this interpretation.

## Commission Structures and Splits After the NAR Settlement

The distinction between who offers compensation and who pays it matters enormously in Maryland. Sellers may still contribute to buyer-agent compensation through concessions negotiated in the contract of sale, but the pathway must be transparent and documented. Maryland's Residential Contract of Sale (MAR Form 1001) includes a provision for seller-paid closing cost contributions that can encompass buyer-agent fees.

| Component | Pre-2024 Practice | 2026 Requirement |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------|
| Buyer-agent compensation source | MLS offer of cooperation | Written buyer rep agreement |
| Disclosure timing | At first substantive contact | Before touring properties |
| Commission amount visibility | Posted on MLS | Not displayed on MLS |
| Seller concession for buyer-agent fee | Uncommon structure | Increasingly standard |
| Broker-to-broker payment | Automatic via MLS | Requires separate written authorization |

Brokers must ensure that commission disbursements match the written agreements on file. Under COMAR 09.11.01.15, a broker cannot pay a commission to any person who is not licensed or to a licensee who is not affiliated with a licensed brokerage. Violations carry fines of up to $5,000 per occurrence and can trigger license suspension.

## Licensing Requirements and Renewal Deadlines

Maryland real estate licenses expire on a two-year cycle. Agents must complete 15 hours of continuing education during each renewal period, including 3 hours of legislative updates, 1.5 hours of fair housing, and 1.5 hours of ethics. The remaining 9 hours can cover elective topics approved by MREC. Failure to complete CE before the renewal deadline results in automatic license expiration — not suspension, expiration.

**An expired license means you cannot legally participate in any transaction, collect any commission, or even advertise your services.** Reinstatement within one year of expiration requires completion of all CE hours plus a $100 late fee. After one year, the former licensee must retake the state exam. The financial consequences extend beyond fees: deals that close while your license is expired create potential liability for your broker and can result in commission disgorgement.

Brokers hold additional responsibility here. Under Section 17-322, a supervising broker must verify that every affiliated agent maintains an active license. MREC has audited brokerages specifically for this issue, and the pattern of discovering one expired license often leads to a full-office review. For agents exploring [how compliance failures create deal breakdowns](/blog/where-deals-break-down), the licensing lapse scenario is one of the most preventable.

## Common Mistakes Maryland Agents Make

Three to five errors surface repeatedly in MREC disciplinary actions and audit findings. These are not edge cases — they represent patterns that cost agents money and licenses every year.

**First: failing to provide the Understanding Whom Real Estate Agents Represent disclosure form at first contact.** Maryland requires delivery of this form (sometimes called the Agency Disclosure) at the first scheduled meeting where substantive real estate matters will be discussed. Agents frequently hand it over at contract signing, which is too late. MREC treats this as a separate violation from any buyer representation agreement deficiency.

Second: co-mingling earned and unearned commissions with escrow funds. Some agents instruct their broker to deduct commission from a deposit held in escrow before closing, which violates COMAR 09.11.01.12's prohibition on disbursing trust funds prior to settlement or written authorization from all parties. This error can trigger both a licensing violation and a civil lawsuit.

Third: advertising commission rates or compensation offers on social media or public-facing websites. Post-settlement rules prohibit buyer-agent compensation details from appearing on the MLS, but agents sometimes circumvent this by posting on Instagram or their brokerage website. MREC interprets this as misleading advertising under Section 17-322.1 if the stated amount does not match the actual written agreement with the buyer.

Fourth: operating under an expired team name or trade name that was never registered with MREC. All trade names must be approved and appear on the license record. Closing a transaction under an unregistered name creates title issues and can void errors and omissions insurance coverage.

Fifth: accepting referral fees from out-of-state agents without verifying that the referring agent holds an active license in their home state. Under COMAR 09.11.01.15, the paying broker must confirm licensure status before disbursing funds. A payment to an unlicensed individual — even one who was licensed when they made the referral — constitutes a violation.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Maryland bear direct liability for the compliance failures of their affiliated agents under Section 17-322(b). This is not a theoretical risk — MREC has revoked broker licenses over systemic supervision failures without requiring proof that the broker personally committed a violation.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Statute/Regulation |
|------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Active license verification for all agents | Quarterly minimum | Section 17-322 |
| Written buyer representation agreements on file | Every transaction | Section 17-530 |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | COMAR 09.11.01.12 |
| Agency disclosure delivery confirmation | Every transaction | COMAR 09.11.02.05 |
| Advertising and trade name compliance | Monthly | Section 17-322.1 |
| CE completion tracking before renewal | 60 days before expiration | COMAR 09.11.01.08 |

**Brokers should implement a systematic file review process rather than relying on agents to self-report compliance.** The five-year retention requirement under COMAR 09.11.01.16 means that audit trails matter long after closing. A complaint filed three years post-settlement will be evaluated against the documentation in the broker's files, not the agent's memory.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

MREC's enforcement authority includes license suspension, revocation, reprimand, and monetary fines up to $5,000 per violation. For commission-related offenses, the Commission can order disgorgement of fees earned during the period of non-compliance. Civil courts add another layer: buyers and sellers who discover agency disclosure violations or unlicensed activity can void contracts and seek damages.

The Maryland Real Property Article, Section 10-702 intersects with licensing rules when disclosure failures accompany commission disputes. A buyer who was never given proper agency disclosure may argue they did not understand they were paying their agent's commission, creating grounds for rescission within a reasonable time after discovery. Title insurance companies increasingly flag these issues during claims investigations.

Criminal liability exists at the extreme end. Practicing real estate without a license constitutes a misdemeanor under Section 17-616, carrying penalties of up to $5,000 in fines and one year of imprisonment. While prosecution is rare, MREC refers cases to the Attorney General's office when the conduct involves consumer harm.
