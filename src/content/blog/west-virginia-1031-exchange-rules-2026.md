## Understanding West Virginia 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026

Few topics trip up experienced agents faster than the intersection of federal tax-deferral rules and state-level obligations. West Virginia 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 affect every transaction where an investor sells one property and acquires another of like-kind—and the state does not simply rubber-stamp the federal treatment without its own filing and withholding requirements. Agents who miss these details risk deal delays, personal liability exposure, and regulatory complaints filed with the West Virginia Real Estate Commission.

West Virginia generally conforms to Internal Revenue Code Section 1031 through its adoption of federal adjusted gross income as the starting point for the state personal income tax under W. Va. Code 11-21-12. That conformity means a properly executed exchange defers state tax the same way it defers federal tax. But conformity is not the same as exemption—agents must still confirm documentation, withholding, and filing obligations are satisfied before the transaction closes.

## How West Virginia Treats 1031 Exchanges Under State Law

The state's personal income tax, governed by the West Virginia Tax Procedures and Administration Act (Chapter 11, Article 10) and the Personal Income Tax Act (Chapter 11, Article 21), piggybacks on federal taxable income. When a taxpayer files IRS Form 8824 to report a like-kind exchange, that deferral flows through to the West Virginia return. Individual filers use WV Form IT-140, while pass-through entities and corporations use WV Form CNF-120 or SPF-100.

**No standalone state 1031 exchange form exists, which is precisely why errors occur.** The taxpayer attaches federal Form 8824 to their West Virginia return, reports any recognized (boot) gain on Schedule M adjustments if applicable, and carries the deferred basis forward. Agents do not prepare these forms, but they must know what documentation their investor clients need from the closing file to satisfy both the qualified intermediary and the state tax return.

The distinction matters most for partially taxable exchanges. If a seller receives cash boot or debt relief that exceeds the replacement property's mortgage, West Virginia taxes that recognized gain at the applicable individual rate—up to 5.12% in 2026 following the state's phased rate reduction under House Bill 2526 (2023 legislative session). ## Nonresident Withholding: The Rule Most Agents Forget

**W. Va. Code 11-21-71a requires 2.5% withholding on gross sales proceeds paid to nonresident sellers of West Virginia real estate.** The closing agent—typically the title company or settlement attorney—is the statutory withholding agent, not the listing agent. However, agents often serve as the conduit of information between the seller, the intermediary, and the closer, and failure to flag a seller's nonresident status early in the listing period causes last-minute scrambles.

A nonresident seller completing a 1031 exchange can avoid the 2.5% withholding by furnishing a completed WV/NRW-2 (Nonresident Sale of Real Property Exemption Certificate). The form requires the seller to certify, under penalty of perjury, that the transaction qualifies for deferral under IRC Section 1031. The closing agent then files WV/NRW-4 (Annual Reconciliation) with the West Virginia State Tax Department by January 31 of the following year.

If no WV/NRW-2 is provided, the closing agent must remit the withheld amount using WV/NRW-1 within 30 days of closing. Below is a comparison of forms agents should recognize and their purposes:

| Form | Full Name | Filed By | Purpose | Deadline |
|------|-----------|----------|---------|----------|
| WV/NRW-1 | Nonresident Income Tax Withholding Return | Closing agent | Remit 2.5% withholding | 30 days post-closing |
| WV/NRW-2 | Exemption Certificate | Seller | Claim 1031 or other exemption | At or before closing |
| WV/NRW-4 | Annual Reconciliation | Closing agent | Reconcile all NRW-1 filings | January 31 following year |
| WV IT-140 | Individual Income Tax Return | Taxpayer | Report income/deferral | April 15 (or extended) |
| IRS Form 8824 | Like-Kind Exchanges | Taxpayer | Report exchange to IRS | Filed with return |

Agents involved in [closing procedures](/blog/west-virginia-title-and-closing-procedures-2026) must confirm these documents are part of the pre-closing package, particularly when the qualified intermediary is located out of state and may not be familiar with West Virginia's withholding framework.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Real estate agents are not statutory withholding agents, so they do not face direct tax penalties for missed filings. The risk is different—and in some ways worse—because it arrives through civil liability, commission disputes, and licensing complaints.

If an agent advises a nonresident investor that "the 1031 exchange handles everything" without flagging the WV/NRW-2 requirement, and the closing agent withholds 2.5% of proceeds that the seller expected to roll into the replacement property, the exchange can fail due to insufficient funds. A failed exchange triggers immediate recognition of gain at both federal and state levels, potentially costing the seller tens of thousands of dollars. The seller's next call is to an attorney, and the listing agent's errors-and-omissions policy is the target.

The West Virginia Real Estate Commission (WVREC) can also take disciplinary action under W. Va. Code 30-40-19 if the licensee is found to have engaged in conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness. Providing tax-related guidance without proper disclosure, or failing to recommend the client consult a CPA, can constitute a violation. Penalties range from formal reprimand to license suspension.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with West Virginia 1031 Exchanges

**The first and most frequent error is treating a 1031 exchange as purely a federal matter.** Agents who have closed exchanges in states without an income tax—like Wyoming or Texas—sometimes assume West Virginia's state-level obligations are minimal. They are not.

The second mistake is failing to identify a client's residency status early enough. An investor who lives in Virginia but owns rental property in Morgantown is a nonresident for West Virginia purposes. If the agent does not flag this at listing, the title company may discover it at the closing table, causing a 48-hour delay to obtain the WV/NRW-2 or restructure the exchange proceeds.

Third, agents often confuse the 45-day identification period and 180-day exchange period with state filing deadlines. The withholding obligation attaches at the point of sale, not at the point of acquisition. Even if the replacement property closes 170 days later, the WV/NRW-2 must be in hand before the relinquished property closes.

Fourth, some agents recommend specific qualified intermediaries without disclosing that the intermediary lacks a physical presence in West Virginia and may not be bonded under state regulations. While West Virginia does not separately license QIs, the [West Virginia State Tax Department](https://tax.wv.gov) has issued guidance reminding taxpayers that the intermediary must meet all IRC Section 1031 safe-harbor requirements for the state deferral to apply.

Fifth, agents occasionally conflate boot with closing costs. A seller who receives $15,000 in net proceeds because their exchange is slightly unbalanced has recognized gain—that amount hits both their federal and West Virginia returns. Agents who tell clients "closing costs offset boot" without understanding the specific IRS rules create downstream tax problems.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under W. Va. Code 30-40-20. When an agent in the office handles investment property transactions, the broker must verify that proper disclosures were made and that the agent did not cross the line into tax advice.

A broker's audit checklist for 1031 exchange transactions should confirm three things at minimum: the client was advised in writing to consult a tax professional, the transaction file includes evidence that the closing agent received either withheld funds or a WV/NRW-2 certificate, and the agent's communications contain no language that could be construed as guaranteeing tax-deferred treatment.

| Audit Item | Documentation Required | Risk If Missing |
|------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Written tax-advice disclaimer | Signed disclosure in listing file | E&O exposure, WVREC complaint |
| Nonresident status confirmed | Seller questionnaire or NRW-2 copy | Withholding surprise at closing |
| QI engagement letter | Copy from seller's intermediary | Exchange failure, liability claim |
| Boot calculation reviewed by CPA | Email confirmation or letter | Misreported gain, client lawsuit |
| Timeline deadlines tracked | Calendar entries or TC system log | Missed 45/180-day windows |

Brokers running high-volume investment teams should consider automating deadline tracking and document verification. The cost of one blown exchange—both in dollars and reputation—far exceeds the investment in proper transaction oversight. Understanding [who pays which closing costs](/blog/west-virginia-who-pays-closing-costs-2026) is also part of this calculus, since improper allocation of costs can inadvertently create boot.

## Partial Exchanges, Installment Sales, and Multi-State Complications

Not every exchange is clean. Sellers who receive some cash (partial exchange) must report the recognized portion on both their federal and West Virginia returns. The state does not offer a separate installment treatment for partially recognized 1031 gains—it follows federal rules under IRC Section 453 as adopted through W. Va. Code 11-21-12's conformity provision.

Multi-state complications arise when the relinquished property is in West Virginia but the replacement property is in another state. West Virginia still requires the exchange to be properly structured and reported. If the exchange later fails (the replacement property is never acquired within 180 days), the entire gain becomes taxable to West Virginia because the income sourced from the sale of West Virginia real property.

Reverse exchanges under Revenue Procedure 2000-37 add another layer. The exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) takes title to the replacement property before the relinquished property sells. West Virginia has not issued specific administrative guidance on reverse exchanges, so agents should advise clients to work with counsel who can confirm that the state's conformity extends to this structure.
