# South Dakota Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Track

Missed deadlines kill deals. Worse, they generate complaints to the South Dakota Real Estate Commission (SDREC) and expose agents to malpractice claims that no errors-and-omissions policy enjoys paying out. Understanding South Dakota buyer contingency deadlines 2026 is not optional background knowledge — it is the mechanical foundation that holds every residential transaction together from acceptance to closing.

South Dakota's approach to buyer contingencies differs from many neighboring states in that the state does not codify specific contingency timeframes in statute. Instead, the deadlines live almost entirely inside the contract forms, which puts an enormous burden on agents to get the paperwork right and track every date with precision.

## How South Dakota Structures Buyer Contingencies

The governing framework starts with SDCL Chapter 36-21A, the South Dakota Real Estate Licensing Act, which establishes the duties of care and loyalty agents owe to their clients. The SDREC enforces these duties through administrative rules codified in ARSD 20:69. Neither body prescribes contingency durations by law, but both hold agents accountable when sloppy deadline management harms a client.

**The South Dakota Association of Realtors (SDAR) publishes the standard forms that functionally define how contingencies operate in most residential transactions.** The SDAR Purchase Agreement — commonly referenced as Form 100 — contains blank fields where agents fill in the number of days allotted for each contingency. The SDAR Inspection Contingency Addendum (Form 150) and the Financing Contingency Addendum (Form 160) layer on additional deadline provisions.

Because the forms leave timeframes negotiable, two agents in the same brokerage could write contracts with wildly different inspection windows. That flexibility is a feature, but it also means there is no statutory safety net. If an agent writes "10" in a field that should have said "15," the contract controls — period.

## The Major Contingency Categories and Their Default Timelines

While there is no statutory default, SDAR forms and common practice in South Dakota have established baseline timeframes that most transactions follow. The table below captures the typical structure agents encounter statewide.

| Contingency Type | Common Timeframe | Governing Form | Triggered From |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inspection | 10 business days | Form 150 | Date of acceptance |
| Financing / Loan | 30 calendar days | Form 160 | Date of acceptance |
| Appraisal | Tied to financing deadline | Form 160 | Date of acceptance |
| Title Review | 15 calendar days | Form 100, Section 8 | Delivery of commitment |
| Sale of Buyer's Property | Negotiated (often 30-60 days) | Form 170 | Date of acceptance |

These numbers are starting points, not rules. Every field is editable, and the responsibility to fill them in correctly sits squarely with the drafting agent. A blank field is not interpreted as "reasonable time" in South Dakota — it is a drafting defect that can void the contingency entirely or create ambiguity that leads to arbitration.

Notice the distinction between business days and calendar days. The inspection window on Form 150 typically runs in business days, while financing and title review periods run in calendar days. Confusing the two — or failing to specify which applies in a custom addendum — is one of the [most common reasons deals fall apart](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## South Dakota Buyer Contingency Deadlines 2026: Key Regulatory Updates

The SDREC has increased enforcement scrutiny heading into 2026, with particular attention to how agents document contingency waivers and extensions. At the Commission's March 2026 meeting, staff reported a 22% rise in consumer complaints related to deadline disputes since 2023. The Commission responded by issuing Advisory Opinion 2025-04, which clarifies that agents must provide written confirmation to all parties when a contingency deadline passes without buyer action.

**Under this advisory opinion, silence does not equal waiver.** If a buyer's inspection deadline expires and the buyer has not delivered a written response, the seller cannot simply assume the contingency is waived without the buyer's agent confirming in writing. This is a subtle but significant shift from prior practice, where many agents treated a missed deadline as automatic waiver.

Agents should also note that the [South Dakota Real Estate Commission](https://dlr.sd.gov/realestate/) updated its continuing education requirements for the 2025-2026 cycle to include a mandatory module on contract deadline management. If you have not completed this CE credit, your license renewal may be delayed.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Contingency Deadlines

Five errors show up repeatedly in SDREC disciplinary files and in brokerage E&O claims across South Dakota. Each one is avoidable.

**First, agents routinely miscalculate start dates.** The SDAR forms specify that contingency clocks begin on the "date of acceptance," which is the date the last party signs or initials the final counteroffer. It is not the date the offer was written, not the date it was presented, and not the date the signed copy was delivered back to the buyer. One calendar day of miscalculation can put a buyer's inspection response outside the window.

Second, agents confuse delivery methods with delivery dates. Under SDAR Form 100, written notices are effective upon delivery — not upon sending. An email sent at 11:58 PM on the deadline day is not delivered if it sits unread in the recipient's inbox until the next morning. Agents relying on text messages face even murkier ground, since the forms do not explicitly recognize SMS as a valid delivery method unless the parties have agreed to it in writing.

Third, agents fail to document contingency removals with the proper SDAR Contingency Removal Form (Form 180). A verbal statement from the buyer that they are "satisfied with the inspection" does not remove the contingency. Without Form 180 signed and delivered, the contingency technically remains alive, creating confusion and potential liability on both sides.

Fourth, agents write custom addenda that contradict the base purchase agreement. If Form 100 states a 10-business-day inspection window and a separate addendum states 14 calendar days, which controls? South Dakota courts generally apply the "last written, most specific" rule, but litigation to establish that costs everyone money. The safer practice is to reference the base form and clearly state that the addendum supersedes specific sections.

Fifth, agents neglect to track the sale-of-buyer's-property contingency under Form 170. This contingency often has a "kick-out" clause allowing the seller to continue marketing the property and issue a 72-hour notice to the buyer upon receiving another offer. Agents who forget to monitor this window can lose their buyer's position without any recourse.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under SDCL 36-21A-72, which states that a broker is responsible for the acts of their affiliated licensees performed within the scope of their real estate activities. A single agent's blown deadline can generate a Commission complaint against the entire brokerage.

Effective brokerages in South Dakota are building audit protocols around three areas. First, every executed purchase agreement should be reviewed within 24 hours of acceptance to confirm that all contingency fields are completed — no blanks, no contradictions between forms. Second, a centralized deadline calendar should track every active contingency across all pending transactions. Relying on individual agents to remember their own deadlines is a recipe for missed dates, as [research into why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) consistently shows.

Third, brokers should require a copy of Form 180 for every contingency removal before marking a milestone as complete. Verbal confirmations and informal emails do not meet the standard. A systematic [checklist-based approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to contingency tracking reduces errors far more reliably than training alone.

| Broker Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency fields in Form 100 | All blanks filled, no contradictions | Within 24 hours of acceptance |
| Deadline calendar accuracy | Start dates match acceptance date | Weekly review of all pending files |
| Form 180 on file | Signed removal for each expired contingency | At each milestone |
| Custom addenda review | No conflicts with base form language | Before delivery to opposing party |
| Form 170 kick-out tracking | 72-hour notice windows monitored | Real-time when applicable |

Failure to maintain these audit practices does not just risk Commission discipline. South Dakota's courts have recognized broker negligence claims where supervisory systems were inadequate, even when the individual agent acted in good faith. The broker's defense of "I didn't know" is not a defense under SDCL 36-21A-72 — it is an admission.

## Liability Exposure When Deadlines Are Missed

The consequences of blown contingency deadlines range from deal cancellation to license discipline to civil litigation. On the transaction level, a buyer who misses a financing contingency deadline may lose the right to exit the contract and forfeit earnest money. A seller who acts on a missed inspection deadline without proper documentation may face a claim that the contingency was never properly waived.

**At the regulatory level, the SDREC can impose fines up to $2,000 per violation under SDCL 36-21A-69, suspend or revoke a license, and require additional education.** The Commission has shown a pattern of escalating penalties for repeat offenders. A first offense involving a missed deadline might result in a letter of reprimand and mandatory CE. A second offense within three years frequently triggers suspension.

Civil liability adds another layer. South Dakota recognizes causes of action for negligent misrepresentation and breach of fiduciary duty against real estate agents. If a buyer loses a property — or gets locked into a contract they cannot exit — because their agent miscalculated a deadline, the damages can include the price differential on a replacement property, additional financing costs, and in some cases, emotional distress. E&O insurance covers some of this exposure, but policy deductibles and premium increases make even a covered claim expensive.
