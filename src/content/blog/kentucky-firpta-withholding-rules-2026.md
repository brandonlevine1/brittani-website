## Kentucky FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Agent's Liability Playbook

Failing to handle withholding correctly on a foreign seller transaction can turn a routine closing into a five-figure liability event. Kentucky firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 create a dual obligation—federal under IRC Section 1445 and state under KRS 141.206—that agents cannot afford to ignore. The penalties stack, the IRS moves slowly but decisively, and the Kentucky Department of Revenue has its own enforcement arm watching nonresident dispositions.

This is not a niche issue. According to NAR's 2025 Profile of International Transactions, foreign-owned residential property sales exceeded $42 billion nationally. Kentucky's equine industry, bourbon tourism corridor, and relatively affordable land attract international buyers who eventually become foreign sellers. If you list or co-broker one of these transactions, your compliance knowledge directly determines whether you face exposure.

## How Federal FIRPTA and Kentucky State Withholding Overlap

**Federal FIRPTA (IRC Section 1445) requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sales price and remit it to the IRS using Form 8288 and Form 8288-A.** The buyer—called the "transferee"—bears primary responsibility. But here is where agents get tangled: under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-1(e), any agent of the transferee or transferor who fails to ensure compliance can be held liable for the withholding amount.

Kentucky layers its own requirement on top. KRS 141.206 mandates that the "transferee" withhold Kentucky income tax from any nonresident seller's real property disposition within the Commonwealth. The regulation at [103 KAR 18:150](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/103/018/150/) specifies that the withholding is either 2% of the gross sales price or the tax on the actual gain—whichever method the parties select and document.

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | Kentucky (KRS 141.206) |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding rate | 15% of gross price | 2% of gross price OR tax on gain |
| Responsible party | Buyer (transferee) | Buyer (transferee) |
| Primary form | IRS Form 8288 + 8288-A | Kentucky Form 740NP-WH |
| Due date | 20 days after closing | Filing with seller's KY return |
| Reduction available | Yes (Form 8288-B) | Yes, if gain is documented |

The dual structure means a $500,000 sale to a foreign seller could trigger $75,000 in federal withholding plus $10,000 in Kentucky withholding if the parties default to the percentage method. Missing either obligation puts the buyer—and potentially the agents—on the hook.

## Forms and Filing Mechanics Agents Must Track

On the federal side, the closing attorney or settlement agent typically prepares IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons). These must reach the IRS within 20 days of closing. If the seller applies for a withholding certificate to reduce the amount, Form 8288-B goes in before or at closing, but the full 15% stays in escrow until the IRS issues the certificate.

For Kentucky, the operative document is Form 740NP-WH, which the nonresident seller files with their Kentucky income tax return. The withholding itself is transmitted as an estimated payment credited to the seller's Kentucky tax account. Closing attorneys in Kentucky often handle this remittance, but the statutory obligation belongs to the buyer. Agents who assume the title company "always handles it" are making a dangerous presumption—especially in transactions that close without a Kentucky-based closing attorney.

**If your transaction involves an out-of-state title company or a remote closing platform, confirm in writing who is responsible for both the federal and state withholding remittances.** This is where deals quietly become compliance nightmares six months after the commission check clears.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

The IRS does not distinguish between ignorance and intent when assessing transferee liability under IRC 1445. If the buyer fails to withhold and the agent "knew or had reason to know" the seller was a foreign person, the agent can be assessed the full withholding amount—up to 15% of the gross sales price. For a $600,000 transaction, that is $90,000 in personal liability before penalties and interest accrue.

Kentucky's enforcement is administered through the Kentucky Department of Revenue. Under KRS 141.206(5), failure to withhold can result in the assessment of the tax against the transferee, plus penalties of up to 25% of the unpaid amount and interest at the statutory rate. The Department has authority to issue liens and pursue collection independently of any IRS action.

Beyond financial penalties, a compliance failure can unwind the deal itself. Title insurance companies may refuse to insure if withholding obligations remain unresolved. Buyers may refuse to close once they understand their personal liability. And the Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC), while not directly enforcing tax law, can initiate disciplinary proceedings under [KRS 324.160](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/statutes/statute.aspx?id=54190) if an agent's conduct demonstrates incompetence or negligence that harms a client.

## Five Common Mistakes Kentucky Agents Make on Foreign Seller Transactions

The first mistake is accepting a seller's verbal claim of U.S. citizenship without documentation. FIRPTA compliance hinges on the seller's foreign or domestic status. Agents should request a completed IRS Form W-9 or, for entities, verify the ITIN/EIN and the entity's domestic or foreign classification before listing goes active.

The second mistake is conflating "nonresident" with "foreign person." Kentucky's KRS 141.206 withholding applies to any nonresident—including U.S. citizens who live in another state. FIRPTA applies only to foreign persons (non-U.S. citizens and non-resident aliens). An agent who confuses these categories may apply the wrong withholding rate or miss one obligation entirely.

**The third mistake is relying exclusively on the closing attorney without confirming the withholding path in the purchase agreement.** Agents should ensure the contract contains a FIRPTA addendum or clause identifying the seller's status and specifying how withholding will be handled. The standard Greater Louisville Association of Realtors contract and the Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR) forms include optional FIRPTA certification language, but agents routinely skip it.

The fourth error is failing to calendar the 20-day federal deadline. If the closing attorney misses the Form 8288 filing window, penalties begin accruing—and the agent's transferee liability exposure crystalizes. This is a [deadline management problem](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that compounds when agents juggle multiple closings without a tracking system.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the withholding certificate process. Sellers can apply via Form 8288-B to reduce withholding below 15% if their actual gain is lower than the statutory percentage. Agents who fail to inform foreign sellers of this option may face client complaints or, worse, lose the deal when the seller refuses to leave $75,000+ in escrow.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers operating under KRS 324.045 carry supervisory responsibility for their agents' transactions. A broker who discovers—or should have discovered—that a foreign seller transaction closed without proper withholding faces potential KREC disciplinary action and shared financial liability.

| Broker Audit Item | Why It Matters | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| FIRPTA certification in listing file | Confirms seller status was documented | Every listing intake |
| W-9 or W-8BEN on file | Verifies tax status before closing | Pre-closing review |

| Contract FIRPTA clause present | Prevents disputes over responsibilities | Contract review stage |

Brokers should build a FIRPTA flag into their transaction management workflow. When a listing agent enters a seller's country of citizenship or residency status, the system should trigger a compliance checklist automatically. This is not optional diligence—it is risk mitigation that protects the brokerage's E&O coverage. ## Exemptions and Reduced Rates Worth Knowing

Not every foreign seller transaction triggers the full 15% federal withholding. If the property sells for $300,000 or less and the buyer signs an affidavit stating they intend to use it as a principal residence, the withholding drops to zero. For sales between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer will use the property as a residence, the rate drops to 10%.

Kentucky's exemption structure is narrower. KRS 141.206 does not contain a residential-use exemption parallel to FIRPTA's tiered rates. The 2% gross withholding or gain-based calculation applies regardless of the buyer's intended use. However, if the seller can demonstrate zero gain (selling at a loss), the Kentucky withholding can be reduced to zero with proper documentation filed alongside the seller's 740NP return.

Agents should also know that transactions involving foreign governments, international organizations, and certain pension funds may be exempt under IRC 897(l). These situations are rare in residential practice but appear in Kentucky's commercial and agricultural markets, particularly in the Lexington horse farm corridor.

## Practical Steps for Your Next Foreign Seller Listing

**At listing intake, ask every seller to complete a FIRPTA affidavit or provide a W-9 confirming U.S. person status.** Do not wait until the buyer's agent asks. Document the response in your transaction file. If the seller is a foreign person, immediately discuss the withholding implications and recommend they consult a tax attorney about filing Form 8288-B for a reduced withholding certificate.

In the purchase contract, include a FIRPTA clause that identifies the seller's status and allocates responsibility for withholding mechanics. Specify whether the closing attorney or title company will handle remittance. Get this in writing—not in a verbal understanding at the closing table. The [checklist-driven approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) eliminates the most common failure mode: assuming someone else handled it.

At closing, verify that the settlement statement reflects the proper withholding amounts for both federal and state purposes. Confirm that Form 8288 will be filed within 20 days. Retain copies of all withholding-related documents in your transaction file for a minimum of seven years, consistent with IRS audit windows.
