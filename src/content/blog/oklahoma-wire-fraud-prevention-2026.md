## Oklahoma Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: The Rules Are No Longer Optional

Oklahoma lost more than $18 million to real estate wire fraud schemes in 2024, according to IC3 complaint data filtered by the Oklahoma Insurance Department. The Oklahoma Real Estate Commission responded by hardening its advisory guidance into enforceable requirements starting January 2026. If you hold an active license in the state, understanding Oklahoma wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer a best-practice suggestion — it is a condition of maintaining your license in good standing.

This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, and enforcement mechanisms that apply to every residential transaction you touch this year.

## The Statutory Framework Behind Oklahoma's New Mandate

Oklahoma's wire fraud prevention requirements rest on two pillars. The first is Title 59 O.S. Section 858-312, which grants the Oklahoma Real Estate Commission broad authority to discipline licensees who fail to protect consumers from foreseeable harm during a transaction. The second is OREC Rule 605:10-9-4, updated in late 2025, which now explicitly lists electronic fund transfer advisories among the mandatory disclosures agents owe to all parties.

**The practical effect is straightforward: every Oklahoma agent must deliver OREC Form 811 (Wire Fraud Advisory and Acknowledgment) to their client before or at the time wiring instructions are first discussed.** The form must be signed by the client and retained in the transaction file for at least five years per OREC record-keeping rules under 605:10-9-2.

OREC's updated position aligns with federal guidance from FinCEN and mirrors stronger stances already adopted by states like Colorado and Texas. The [Oklahoma Real Estate Commission's official rules page](https://www.ok.gov/orec/) hosts the current version of Form 811 and the associated advisory bulletin 2025-07.

## What Form 811 Actually Requires

Form 811 is a two-page document, but agents frequently treat it like a throwaway addendum. That is a mistake. The form contains three operative sections that create specific duties for the licensee.

Section A requires the agent to identify all parties involved in the transfer of funds, including the title company, escrow officer, and lender contact. Section B provides the client with explicit instructions to verbally verify any wiring instructions received via email by calling a known, previously confirmed phone number. Section C is the acknowledgment signature block where the client confirms they received the advisory and understand they should never rely solely on emailed wire instructions.

| Form Section | Agent's Duty | Client's Duty |
|---|---|---|
| Section A | Identify all fund-transfer parties by name and phone | Review and confirm accuracy |
| Section B | Explain verbal verification protocol | Follow the protocol before wiring |
| Section C | Obtain dated signature | Sign and retain a copy |
| Retention | Store in transaction file for 5 years minimum | Keep personal copy |

The form must be delivered no later than when earnest money wiring instructions are provided. For most transactions, this means within the first 48 hours of contract execution. Delivering it at closing is too late and does not satisfy the requirement.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

Agents fail on this requirement in predictable ways. Knowing the failure modes can keep you out of an OREC complaint hearing.

**First, agents deliver Form 811 buried inside a stack of other documents at contract signing without verbally explaining its contents.** OREC's advisory bulletin 2025-07 explicitly states that the licensee must "bring the advisory to the attention of the party and explain its purpose." A silent checkbox in a DocuSign package does not satisfy this standard.

Second, agents assume the title company handles everything. Title companies do send their own wire fraud warnings, but those communications do not relieve the agent of the independent duty under OREC Rule 605:10-9-4. The rule places the obligation on the licensee, not on third-party service providers. If the title company's email warning lands in a spam folder, the agent's duty remains unfulfilled unless they completed their own delivery.

Third, some agents use outdated versions of the form. OREC revised Form 811 in November 2025 to include language about AI-generated phishing and deepfake voice calls. Using the 2023 version — which many brokerages still have loaded in their template libraries — does not meet the current standard. Check the version date in the footer before sending.

Fourth, dual agents sometimes deliver only one copy and assume it covers both parties. Each party to the transaction must receive and sign a separate Form 811. One form, two signatures on the same page, does not comply.

Fifth, agents fail to document the delivery method. If you send Form 811 electronically, your transaction management system must log the timestamp, delivery confirmation, and signature. A verbal "I told them about it" with no paper trail invites liability. This is one area where having a [reliable wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) already built into your workflow pays dividends.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance: What Is Actually at Stake

OREC's enforcement actions under Title 59 O.S. Section 858-312 range from formal reprimands to license revocation. For wire fraud advisory failures specifically, the 2025 enforcement guidance memo from OREC's legal division outlines a tiered penalty structure.

| Violation Level | Circumstances | Likely Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| First offense, no client loss | Form missing from file but client not harmed | Written reprimand, mandatory CE course |
| First offense, client loss occurred | Form not delivered and client wired to fraudulent account | $5,000 fine, 90-day suspension, mandatory restitution hearing |
| Repeat offense | Second violation within 3-year period | $10,000 fine, 6-month suspension or revocation |
| Broker-level failure | Systemic non-compliance across multiple agents | Brokerage audit, $25,000 fine, potential office closure order |

Beyond OREC penalties, agents face civil liability. Oklahoma courts have allowed negligence claims against licensees who failed to warn clients about known risks. A $300,000 wire fraud loss becomes an E&O claim becomes a premium increase becomes a career problem. The connection between missed paperwork and financial devastation is shorter than most agents realize — a pattern explored further in [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and the cascading effects that follow.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Title 59 O.S. Section 858-307. If an agent in your office fails to deliver Form 811 and a client gets defrauded, OREC will investigate whether your brokerage had policies, training, and audit procedures in place. "I didn't know my agent skipped it" is not a defense.

**Every managing broker in Oklahoma should implement three specific controls before their next audit cycle.** First, add Form 811 to your transaction checklist as a mandatory early-stage document, flagged for completion within 48 hours of executed contract. Second, conduct quarterly file audits sampling at least 15 percent of closed transactions to verify signed Form 811 presence and version currency. Third, document annual training on wire fraud procedures, including a signed attestation from every affiliated licensee that they understand the requirement.

OREC's 2026 brokerage renewal questionnaire now includes a checkbox confirming the broker has a written wire fraud prevention policy. Checking that box without having the policy invites an administrative complaint. Build the policy, train your people, and keep records that prove both.

The audit burden is real but manageable. Brokerages already tracking [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) in transaction management will find this requirement slots naturally into existing workflows.

## How the Scams Actually Hit Oklahoma Agents

Understanding the mechanics helps agents explain the risk to skeptical clients. The dominant scheme in Oklahoma involves business email compromise targeting title company employees or agents. Attackers monitor email threads — sometimes for weeks — then send a spoofed email at the exact moment wiring instructions would normally arrive.

The spoofed email looks identical to the title company's formatting, uses a nearly matching email domain (one character off), and includes correct transaction details harvested from earlier messages. The client, expecting wiring instructions, follows the fraudulent link or account number. Funds leave within minutes. Recovery rates on domestic transfers sit below 30 percent; international transfers are almost never recovered.

> "In calendar year 2024, the FBI's Oklahoma City field office handled 147 real estate wire fraud complaints with an aggregate loss exceeding $18.3 million. The median individual loss was $87,000." — IC3 Annual State Supplement, Oklahoma, 2024

This is not theoretical. These losses happen to clients who trusted their agent to protect them.

## Building a Compliant Workflow Without Drowning in Paper

The good news: compliance does not require heroic effort. It requires a system. The minimum viable workflow for wire fraud compliance in Oklahoma looks like this. At contract execution, generate Form 811 with party-specific information pre-filled. Deliver it with a verbal or video explanation within 48 hours. Obtain a signed acknowledgment and store it with a timestamp.

At the point wiring instructions are issued (usually 3-5 days before closing), send a follow-up reminder to the client reinforcing the verbal verification protocol from Section B. Document that reminder. At closing, confirm with the title company that funds arrived at the correct account and note it in your file.
