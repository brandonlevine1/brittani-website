# New Mexico HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Deliver Before Closing

The New Mexico hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 cycle brings no dramatic statutory overhaul, but enforcement patterns have shifted enough that agents who coast on old habits are losing deals—and sometimes licenses. The New Mexico Real Estate Commission (NMREC) continues to flag incomplete or missing association disclosures as a top-five cause of consumer complaints filed against licensees. If you list or sell properties governed by homeowners associations or condominium regimes in this state, the disclosure obligations are non-negotiable.

This post breaks down the governing statutes, the forms you must use, and the real consequences of non-compliance. It also covers the specific errors that keep showing up in NMREC disciplinary actions and what managing brokers need to enforce inside their offices.

## The Two Statutes That Control Everything

New Mexico splits its HOA and condo disclosure obligations across two separate statutory frameworks. The New Mexico Condominium Act (NMSA 1978, Sections 47-7A-1 through 47-7D-120) governs condominium resales. The New Mexico Homeowner Association Act (NMSA 1978, Sections 47-16-1 through 47-16-21) covers planned communities with mandatory membership associations that are not condominiums.

**Agents routinely conflate these two frameworks, but the disclosure triggers, document lists, and cancellation rights differ.** A townhome in a planned community follows Chapter 47-16. A unit in a condo regime follows Chapter 47-7D. Misidentifying which statute applies is the first domino in a compliance failure.

The NMREC also requires adherence to the general Property Disclosure Statement (NMAR Form 1401) and the Association Disclosure Addendum that addresses HOA specifics within the purchase agreement. These forms work in tandem with—not as substitutes for—the statutory documents the association itself must produce.

## Condominium Resale Certificate: NMSA 47-7D-109

Section 47-7D-109 of the Condominium Act requires that a seller (or the seller's agent) furnish a resale certificate to the buyer before or at the time of the purchase agreement. The certificate must be prepared by the association and include a defined list of financial and governance information.

The required contents include: the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments currently due or planned, the association's current operating budget, a statement of unpaid assessments against the unit, the association's insurance coverage summary, and any pending litigation involving the association. The declaration, bylaws, and rules must also be provided.

| Required Document | Source | Statute Section |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly assessment amount | Association | 47-7D-109(b)(1) |
| Special assessments pending or planned | Association | 47-7D-109(b)(2) |
| Current operating budget | Association | 47-7D-109(b)(3) |
| Statement of unpaid assessments on unit | Association | 47-7D-109(b)(4) |
| Insurance summary | Association | 47-7D-109(b)(5) |
| Declaration, bylaws, and rules | Association | 47-7D-109(a) |

The buyer receives a 15-day cancellation right after receiving the resale certificate. If the certificate is never delivered, that cancellation right persists through closing—meaning a buyer could theoretically rescind the transaction even after recording.

## HOA Disclosures Under the Homeowner Association Act

For non-condominium HOAs, the Homeowner Association Act (NMSA 47-16-1 et seq.) imposes a parallel but distinct set of obligations. Section 47-16-10 requires the association to provide a disclosure certificate upon request from a unit owner who intends to sell. The contents overlap with the condo resale certificate but are not identical.

**The key difference: the Homeowner Association Act does not grant the buyer an explicit statutory cancellation period tied to receipt of the disclosure certificate.** Cancellation rights in non-condo HOA transactions come from the purchase agreement itself—typically through the NMAR forms that build in an inspection or due diligence period during which a buyer can object to association documents.

This matters because agents representing buyers in planned communities cannot rely on a statutory safety net. The contractual timeline in the purchase agreement is the only protection. If you fail to negotiate adequate review time for HOA documents, your buyer client has no fallback.

Agents should also be aware that under Section 47-16-15, a seller must disclose any known violations of the association's governing documents. This obligation exists independent of the association's disclosure certificate and runs through the seller's own Property Disclosure Statement (NMAR Form 1401).

## Forms You Must Know: NMAR Numbers and Usage

The New Mexico Association of Realtors (NMAR) publishes the standard forms that most brokerages adopt. For HOA and condo transactions, the following forms are directly relevant in 2026.

NMAR Form 1401 (Property Disclosure Statement) includes a section where the seller identifies whether the property is subject to a homeowners association or condominium regime. NMAR Form 1402 (Lead-Based Paint Disclosure) is unrelated but often confused during document assembly—do not substitute one for the other.

The NMAR Purchase Agreement (Form 2101 for residential resale) contains an Association section that addresses the buyer's right to review governing documents and the timeframe for objection. Paragraph references shift between form revisions, so confirm you are using the current 2026 version distributed through your MLS or brokerage.

Additionally, many brokerages use a supplemental HOA Information Sheet or Association Addendum (not a numbered NMAR form but a brokerage-specific document) to capture the management company name, contact information, transfer fee amounts, and move-in deposit requirements. Check your brokerage policy manual—your broker may require this form even though it is not mandated by statute.

## What Happens When You Fail to Comply

**Non-compliance with HOA and condo disclosure rules in New Mexico exposes agents to three distinct categories of harm.** Understanding each one separately clarifies why this is not a "soft" obligation you can paper over after the fact.

First, deal cancellation. Under 47-7D-109, a condo buyer who never received the resale certificate can rescind. This right is not discretionary—it is statutory. The listing agent who failed to obtain the certificate from the association has given the buyer an exit ramp that persists indefinitely until the documents are delivered.

Second, NMREC disciplinary action. Rule 16.61.19 NMAC establishes standards of practice for licensees. Failing to disclose material information—including association obligations—constitutes a violation that can result in a letter of reprimand, mandatory education, fines up to $1,000 per violation, license suspension, or revocation. The Commission has publicly cited agents for omitting HOA disclosures in its quarterly disciplinary reports.

Third, civil liability. A buyer who closes without knowledge of a special assessment or restrictive covenant may pursue damages against the agent under theories of negligence, misrepresentation, or breach of fiduciary duty. New Mexico courts have recognized that agents owe a duty to discover and disclose facts that a reasonably competent agent would have uncovered. Ignorance of the HOA's existence is not a defense if the CC&Rs were recorded and discoverable.

| Consequence | Legal Basis | Potential Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Contract rescission | NMSA 47-7D-109 | Deal unwinds; commission clawback |
| NMREC disciplinary action | 16.61.19 NMAC | Fine up to $1,000; suspension; revocation |
| Civil lawsuit (negligence) | Common law duty of care | Damages, attorney fees, E&O claim |
| Civil lawsuit (misrepresentation) | NMSA 47-7D-109; fraud statutes | Actual and punitive damages |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With HOA and Condo Disclosures

These five errors appear repeatedly in NMREC complaints and E&O claims in New Mexico. Each one is preventable.

**Mistake one: assuming the seller's verbal confirmation that "there's no HOA" is sufficient.** Agents must verify association status through the title commitment, recorded plat, and CC&Rs. Self-managed neighborhood associations often fly under the radar because there is no management company sending monthly bills, but the legal obligations still exist if recorded covenants create mandatory membership.

Mistake two: ordering the resale certificate too late. Associations have 10 business days to produce the certificate under 47-7D-109(d), but many small associations in rural New Mexico take longer. If you wait until the inspection period to order the certificate, you risk blowing past the buyer's review window.

Mistake three: delivering the declaration and bylaws but omitting the financial documents. The resale certificate is a package—not a single page. Handing over the CC&Rs without the budget, assessment statement, and insurance summary does not satisfy the statute. Partial delivery does not start the buyer's 15-day clock.

Mistake four: confusing the condo cancellation right with the contractual HOA objection period. These are two different mechanisms with different legal foundations. Agents who advise buyers in planned communities that they have a "15-day right to cancel" based on the Condominium Act are giving incorrect legal guidance when the property is not a condominium.

Mistake five: failing to document delivery. You must be able to prove when the buyer received the resale certificate or HOA disclosure package. Email with read receipt, signed acknowledgment, or transaction management system timestamp—pick one. Oral delivery is unverifiable and worthless in a dispute.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in New Mexico carry supervisory responsibility under NMSA 61-29-12 and NMREC Rule 16.61.3.9 NMAC. If an agent in your office botches an HOA disclosure, the Commission can—and does—hold the qualifying broker accountable.

**Start with a file audit protocol that flags every transaction involving a property subject to an association.** Your audit should confirm three things: that the correct statutory disclosure package was ordered, that delivery to the buyer is documented with a timestamp, and that the buyer's objection or cancellation deadline was tracked and met.

Build a mandatory checklist item into your transaction management workflow that requires agents to identify HOA or condo status within 48 hours of listing or buyer representation agreement execution. This catches the issue before contracts are written rather than after.

Brokers should also verify that agents are using the current 2026 NMAR form set. Outdated forms may reference superseded paragraph numbers or omit updated disclosure language. A quarterly form audit across active listings prevents this drift.
