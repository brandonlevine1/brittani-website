## Understanding Oregon Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

Oregon handles dual agency differently than most states, and the terminology alone trips up agents who have transferred from other markets. The state does not use the term "dual agency" in its statutes—instead, the framework revolves around "disclosed limited agency," which is Oregon's functional equivalent. Grasping the **Oregon dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** framework is not optional; it is a licensing requirement that carries real financial and legal exposure when violated.

The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) governs these rules under ORS Chapter 696 and the administrative rules in OAR 863-015. Oregon eliminated traditional "single agent" fiduciary duties in certain dual representation scenarios and replaced them with a limited-duty model that restricts what an agent can do for either party. This article breaks down exactly what the statutes require, which forms you must use, what failures cost, and what your principal broker should be auditing right now.

## How Oregon Defines Disclosed Limited Agency

**Oregon does not permit undisclosed dual agency under any circumstance.** ORS 696.800 through ORS 696.830 establish the framework for agency relationships, and ORS 696.820 specifically addresses situations where a single licensee or brokerage represents both sides of a transaction.

Under disclosed limited agency, the licensee owes limited duties to both parties rather than the full range of fiduciary obligations owed in a single-client relationship. The agent cannot advocate for one party's position over the other, cannot disclose confidential information from either party without written permission, and cannot advise either party on price or terms that would disadvantage the other. These restrictions are not suggestions—they are statutory mandates.

The Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet, required under ORS 696.810, must be provided at the first substantive contact with any party. This is the document that explains the three possible agency relationships: seller's agent, buyer's agent, or disclosed limited agent. Failure to deliver it at the correct time creates the first point of liability.

## Designated Agency in Oregon: The Brokerage-Level Split

Oregon's version of designated agency allows a principal broker to appoint separate licensees within the same brokerage to represent each party. OAR 863-015-0215 governs how this designation works. The principal broker becomes a disclosed limited agent of both parties, while the designated licensees maintain their full duties to their respective clients.

This structure only works when the brokerage has more than one licensee who can be assigned. A sole practitioner operating under their own principal broker license cannot designate themselves to both sides—that triggers the disclosed limited agency rules instead. The distinction matters because the duty levels differ substantially.

| Scenario | Agent's Duty Level | Form Required | Principal Broker Role |
|----------|-------------------|---------------|----------------------|
| Disclosed Limited Agency (single agent, both sides) | Limited duties to both parties | OREF Form 520 | Supervise compliance |
| Designated Agency (two agents, same brokerage) | Full duties to respective client | OREF Form 520 + internal designation | Disclosed limited agent of both parties |
| Single Agency (one side only) | Full fiduciary duties | Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet | Standard supervision |

The practical difference is significant. In a designated agency scenario within a brokerage, each agent can still advise their client on pricing strategy, negotiate aggressively, and advocate for favorable terms. In a disclosed limited agency scenario, neither party gets that level of advocacy.

## The Forms That Must Be in Every File

Oregon real estate forms are primarily published by the Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) LLC. For disclosed limited agency and designated agency transactions, the following documents must appear in the transaction file.

**OREF Form 520—the Disclosed Limited Agency Agreement—is the document that makes or breaks compliance.** Both buyer and seller must sign it before the licensee begins acting in the limited capacity. The timing requirement is absolute: if confidential information from one party has already been shared before the form is signed, the violation has already occurred.

The Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet is not a form you fill out—it is a pre-printed document that must be provided and acknowledged. Under ORS 696.810, the acknowledgment signature confirms delivery, not consent to a particular agency relationship. Many agents confuse these two requirements, treating the pamphlet delivery as sufficient for disclosed limited agency. It is not.

## What Happens When You Get It Wrong

The consequences of non-compliance with Oregon's agency disclosure rules fall into four categories: regulatory discipline, civil liability, transaction rescission, and reputational damage.

OREA can impose disciplinary action under ORS 696.301, which includes fines up to $5,000 per violation, license suspension, or outright revocation. The agency's enforcement division investigates complaints and has the authority to audit transaction files. A pattern of missing Form 520 signatures or late pamphlet delivery can escalate from a single fine to a suspension hearing.

Civil liability is the bigger financial exposure. An aggrieved party can sue for damages arising from undisclosed dual representation. Oregon courts have held that agents who failed to properly disclose their limited agency status breached their statutory duties, opening the door to compensatory damages, potential rescission of the sale, and in egregious cases, punitive damages. The [Oregon Real Estate Agency's licensing page](https://www.oregon.gov/rea) publishes disciplinary actions that serve as cautionary examples.

Transaction rescission is possible when a court determines that informed consent was never properly obtained. This means the deal unwinds—earnest money returns, title transfers reverse, and both parties can seek damages from the agent or brokerage. The cost of defending such a claim far exceeds the commission earned.

## Five Common Mistakes Oregon Agents Make

**Mistake one: confusing pamphlet delivery with disclosed limited agency consent.** The Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet informs consumers about agency options. It does not constitute agreement to disclosed limited agency. OREF Form 520 is a separate requirement, and both documents must appear in the file.

Mistake two: obtaining the Form 520 signature after negotiations have already begun. ORS 696.820 requires consent before the dual representation commences. If you have already received a buyer's offer strategy and then realize the listing is your own, the consent must predate any sharing of that information. Retroactive signatures do not cure the violation.

Mistake three: failing to restrict information flow in designated agency scenarios. When two agents in the same office represent opposing parties, the principal broker must establish protocols—sometimes called "information barriers"—to prevent one designated agent from accessing the other client's confidential file. Simply telling agents "don't talk about it" is not a documented protocol.

Mistake four: not updating the disclosure when the agency relationship changes mid-transaction. An agent who starts as a buyer's agent and then takes a listing from the seller in the same transaction must immediately stop, disclose the change, and obtain new written consent before proceeding. The original pamphlet acknowledgment does not cover this shift.

Mistake five: assuming that disclosed limited agency means you owe no duties at all. Limited duties still include honesty, reasonable care, disclosure of material facts about the property, and accounting for all funds. Agents who adopt a purely passive "I can't help either of you" posture may actually be breaching the limited duties they still owe. ## What Principal Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Oregon's OAR 863-015-0210 places supervision responsibilities squarely on the principal broker's shoulders. If an agent in your office violates agency disclosure rules, you share in the regulatory exposure.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|------------|--------------|-----------|
| Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet delivery | Signed acknowledgment dated at or before first substantive contact | Every transaction |
| OREF Form 520 execution | Both parties signed before disclosed limited agency began | Every dual/in-house transaction |
| Designated agency internal memo | Written designation of specific agents, dated before representation | Every in-house transaction |
| Information barrier documentation | Written protocol for file separation and communication restrictions | Quarterly review |
| Mid-transaction agency changes | Updated disclosures and new consent forms when relationships shift | As triggered |

**Your brokerage's transaction management system should flag in-house transactions automatically.** If your buyer's agent writes an offer on your office's listing, the system must generate a compliance checklist before the offer is presented. Relying on agents to self-report these conflicts is how violations happen.

Principal brokers should also conduct periodic file audits—not just at closing, but at the offer stage. A disclosed limited agency agreement signed at closing is worthless if the dual representation began weeks earlier. The timing of the signature relative to other transaction documents is the single most audited element in OREA disciplinary proceedings.
