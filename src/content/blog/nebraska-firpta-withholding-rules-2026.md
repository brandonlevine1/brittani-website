## Nebraska FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026: The Agent's Liability Starts Earlier Than You Think

If you list or sell property in Nebraska and your client holds foreign status, the Nebraska FIRPTA withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 should be front-of-mind the moment you sign a listing agreement—not at the closing table. The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act requires that buyers withhold up to 15% of the gross sales price when purchasing U.S. real property from a foreign person. Nebraska does not impose a separate state-level withholding on foreign sellers the way some states do, but the federal obligation still lands squarely on the transaction, and agents who miss it face real consequences.

The Nebraska Real Estate Commission (NREC), operating under Neb. Rev. Stat. Chapter 76, Article 22, does not directly enforce FIRPTA. That enforcement belongs to the IRS under 26 U.S.C. Section 1445. But the NREC absolutely disciplines agents who fail to disclose material facts or facilitate non-compliant closings. A FIRPTA failure can trigger license action if the Commission determines you breached your duty of competent service under Title 299, Chapter 2 of the Nebraska Administrative Code.

## How the 15% Withholding Actually Works in a Nebraska Closing

**The buyer—not the seller, not the agent—bears the legal obligation to withhold and remit FIRPTA funds to the IRS.** In practice, the title company or closing attorney handles the mechanical steps. But the statutory liability never shifts away from the buyer, which means buyer's agents must confirm their client understands this obligation before they sign a purchase agreement.

At closing, 15% of the gross purchase price is withheld from the seller's proceeds and submitted to the IRS using Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). These forms must be filed within 20 days after the transfer date. If the buyer fails to file, they can be assessed the full withholding amount plus penalties and interest under IRC Section 1461.

Nebraska closings commonly run through title companies—First American, Midwest Title, or local operations like Hometown Title. These companies will typically handle the FIRPTA escrow and filing as part of their closing services. However, in FSBO transactions or deals where agents coordinate closings without a title company (rare in Nebraska but not unheard of in rural counties), the burden of compliance tracking falls back to whoever is coordinating the deal.

## Exemptions and Reduced Withholding: The Details That Save (or Sink) a Deal

Not every transaction with a foreign seller triggers the full 15% withholding. Understanding the exemption tiers prevents you from over-withholding and killing a deal—or under-withholding and exposing your buyer client to IRS penalties.

| Scenario | Withholding Rate | Key Requirement |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Buyer will use property as residence, price $300,000 or less | 0% | Buyer signs residence-use affidavit |
| Buyer will use property as residence, price $300,001–$1,000,000 | 10% | Buyer signs residence-use affidavit |
| All other sales above $300,000 (investment, commercial, vacant land) | 15% | No affidavit needed; standard withholding applies |
| Seller obtains IRS Withholding Certificate (Form 8288-B approved) | Reduced or zero | IRS approval letter received before or at closing |
| Seller certifies non-foreign status (Form W-9 or affidavit) | 0% | Seller provides valid certification; buyer has no reason to disbelieve |

The residence-use exemption trips up agents regularly. The buyer must sign a written statement under penalties of perjury confirming they intend to reside in the property for at least 50% of the days it is in use during the first two 12-month periods after closing. This is not a casual checkbox—it is a sworn statement with legal consequences for the buyer.

**Foreign sellers can apply to reduce their withholding by filing IRS Form 8288-B before closing.** The IRS typically responds within 90 days. During that window, the withheld amount must be held in escrow rather than remitted. If your seller needs the reduced withholding to make the deal pencil out, you need that 8288-B filed months before the anticipated closing date—not during the option period.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

License-level consequences in Nebraska flow from the NREC's authority under [Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=76-2422), which authorizes disciplinary action for incompetence, negligence, or failure to disclose material facts. A FIRPTA compliance failure can implicate several of these grounds simultaneously.

If a listing agent knows or should reasonably know that a seller is a foreign person and fails to disclose this to the buyer's side or the closing agent, the resulting withholding failure becomes the buyer's problem—and potentially the listing agent's liability under negligence theories. Nebraska courts have not yet issued a landmark FIRPTA-specific ruling against an agent, but malpractice insurers across the state have paid claims where agents failed to ask the question.

On the buyer's side, the stakes are even more concrete. The IRS can pursue the buyer directly for the full 15% withholding amount, plus interest accruing from the 20th day after closing. There is no statute of limitations grace period that saves agents from professional liability claims arising from this failure. Deals can also fall apart at the title company level—most Nebraska title companies will refuse to close if they suspect a foreign seller and cannot confirm compliance.

## Five Common Mistakes Nebraska Agents Make on FIRPTA Transactions

**First, agents assume that permanent residents are exempt.** They are not automatically exempt. A green card holder is generally treated as a U.S. person for tax purposes, but a person holding only an H-1B visa, an L-1 visa, or certain other non-immigrant statuses is a foreign person under FIRPTA regardless of how long they have lived in Nebraska. The test is tax residency, not physical presence.

Second, listing agents fail to ask the question at all. The [Nebraska Association of Realtors](https://www.nebraskarealtors.com/) Seller Property Condition Disclosure form does not include a foreign-status question. If you rely solely on standard forms, you will never surface this issue. Add the question to your intake process or listing checklist.

Third, agents wait until the week of closing to raise FIRPTA with the title company. By then, if the seller needs a withholding certificate (Form 8288-B), the deal will stall for 90 days or collapse entirely. Similar to how agents miss contingency deadlines on standard transactions, the [deadline management problem](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) compounds rapidly when federal tax forms enter the picture.

Fourth, buyer's agents fail to explain the withholding obligation to their client before the purchase agreement is signed. The buyer who discovers at closing that they must fund 15% of the purchase price into an IRS escrow—separate from their down payment and closing costs—may not have the liquidity to close.

Fifth, agents confuse FIRPTA with Nebraska's own income tax withholding on nonresident sellers. Nebraska does impose withholding on certain nonresident sellers under Neb. Rev. Stat. 77-2753, but that statute targets sellers who are nonresidents of Nebraska (whether U.S. citizens or foreign nationals) and involves Nebraska Form 14N. These are separate obligations with different forms, different rates, and different filing deadlines.

## Nebraska Form 14N vs. Federal FIRPTA: A Side-by-Side Comparison

| Element | Federal FIRPTA (IRC 1445) | Nebraska Nonresident Withholding (Neb. Rev. Stat. 77-2753) |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Applies to | Foreign persons (non-U.S. tax residents) | Any seller who is not a Nebraska resident |
| Withholding rate | 15% of gross sales price (with exceptions) | 2.5% of net gain or alternative calculation |
| Form used | IRS Form 8288, Form 8288-A | Nebraska Form 14N |
| Filing deadline | 20 days after transfer | Due with seller's Nebraska income tax return |
| Responsible party | Buyer (transferee) | Buyer or closing agent |
| Penalty for non-compliance | Full withholding amount plus interest/penalties | Assessment of tax plus penalties from Nebraska DOR |

When your seller is both a foreign person and a nonresident of Nebraska, both obligations apply simultaneously. The closing agent must withhold and remit to two separate taxing authorities. This is where [having a reliable transaction coordination system](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) becomes the difference between a smooth closing and a compliance disaster.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Nebraska carry supervisory responsibility under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2401 through 76-2430. If an agent in your office botches a FIRPTA transaction, the Commission can hold the broker accountable for inadequate supervision.

**Brokers should audit for three specific items in any transaction involving a seller who may be a foreign person.** First, confirm that the listing file contains either a non-foreign affidavit (signed under penalties of perjury) or a notation that the seller is foreign and FIRPTA procedures are active. Second, verify that the title company or closing attorney has been notified in writing of the seller's foreign status before the closing package is prepared. Third, confirm that the buyer's agent has provided written disclosure to the buyer regarding their withholding obligation.

Beyond individual transactions, brokers should implement a policy that requires all listing agents to ask the foreign-status question during the listing intake process. Document this policy in your office procedures manual. Train on it annually during your brokerage compliance meetings. The NREC expects reasonable supervisory systems—not perfection, but demonstrable effort to prevent foreseeable errors.
