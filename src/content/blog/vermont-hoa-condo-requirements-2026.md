# Vermont HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: What Every Agent Must Deliver Before Closing

Getting the disclosure sequence wrong on a Vermont condominium or common-interest community sale can unwind an otherwise clean deal in a matter of days. The **Vermont hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** framework is rooted in a single but dense statute—the Vermont Common Interest Ownership Act, codified at Title 27A of the Vermont Statutes Annotated—and it imposes specific delivery requirements on sellers, associations, and the agents who represent them. Missing a form, blowing a deadline, or confusing the rules for planned communities with those for condominiums is more than an inconvenience; it hands the buyer a live cancellation right that can detonate a transaction at the eleventh hour.

This post breaks down the statutes, the forms, the deadlines, and the enforcement risks that Vermont licensees must understand for every HOA or condo listing they touch this year.

## The Statute That Controls Everything: 27A V.S.A. Chapters 1–4

Vermont's Common Interest Ownership Act (CIOA) governs condominiums, cooperatives, and planned communities created on or after January 1, 1999. For condominiums specifically, the resale disclosure obligations sit primarily in [27A V.S.A. Section 4-409](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/27A/004/04-409). Older condominiums created under the Vermont Condominium Ownership Act (27 V.S.A. Chapter 15) follow a parallel but slightly different framework, which is one of the first traps agents fall into.

The Vermont Real Estate Commission, housed under the Office of Professional Regulation (OPR), enforces licensee conduct. OPR does not publish a state-mandated HOA disclosure form the way some states do, but it holds agents accountable for ensuring statutory obligations are met. Failure to advise clients properly on disclosure requirements can trigger a Rule 11.6 violation under the Commission's administrative rules—specifically the duty to exercise reasonable skill and care.

**The practical takeaway is that agents must determine which version of the law applies to the property before drafting a single document.** A condo created in 1985 under the old act has different resale packet requirements than one created in 2005 under CIOA. Verify the declaration's recording date in the municipal land records before assuming which statute governs.

## Resale Certificates: The Core Disclosure Document

Section 4-409 of CIOA requires the seller of a unit in a common-interest community to furnish the buyer with a resale certificate. This is not optional and it is not a courtesy—it is a statutory prerequisite that activates the buyer's review and cancellation rights. The certificate must be prepared by the association (or its management company) and include specific financial and governance data.

The required contents include the monthly common expense assessment, any special assessments approved or anticipated, the association's current operating budget, the most recent audited or reviewed financial statement, the amount of reserves, any pending litigation involving the association, and any outstanding judgments against it. The certificate must also disclose whether the association's governing documents restrict the use or occupancy of the unit, such as rental caps or pet restrictions.

There is no single state-issued form number for the resale certificate in Vermont. Instead, associations typically produce their own document or use a management company template. Agents should still confirm that every item required by Section 4-409 appears in the packet. If even one required element is missing, the certificate is arguably incomplete, and the buyer's cancellation clock may not have started.

## Buyer Cancellation Rights and Delivery Deadlines

**Once the buyer receives a compliant resale certificate, a five-day cancellation period begins.** During those five days, the buyer may cancel the purchase agreement by delivering written notice to the seller, and the contract is voided without penalty. The seller must return any deposit in full.

Here is where delivery mechanics matter enormously. The five-day period starts upon actual receipt—not upon mailing, not upon the seller's request to the association, and not upon the date the agent uploads the document to a transaction management platform. If the certificate is mailed, agents should assume delivery takes at least a few business days and plan accordingly.

| Scenario | Cancellation Period Starts | Buyer's Right |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate hand-delivered at signing | Day of delivery | 5-day cancellation window |
| Resale certificate mailed after contract execution | Day of actual receipt | 5-day cancellation window from receipt |
| Resale certificate never delivered | Never starts | Buyer may cancel at any time before closing |
| Incomplete resale certificate delivered | Arguably never starts | Buyer may assert cancellation right at any time |

The risk in the "never delivered" scenario is obvious—a buyer with cold feet three days before closing can legally walk away from the deal, recover their earnest money, and leave the seller scrambling. Agents who treat the resale certificate as a low-priority checklist item are playing a dangerous game with their clients' transactions, similar to how agents in other states sometimes [miss critical contingency deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and lose deals as a result.

## Public Offering Statements for New Condo Sales

Resale transactions require a resale certificate, but initial sales of units by a declarant (developer) require a different document: the Public Offering Statement under 27A V.S.A. Section 4-403. This is a far more extensive disclosure package covering the declarant's background, the development plan, projected budgets, warranty information, and any conditions that could affect the buyer's use of the unit.

Buyers of new units from a declarant receive a 15-day cancellation period—triple the window for resale transactions. The declarant must deliver the Public Offering Statement before the buyer executes a purchase agreement. If the declarant fails to deliver it or delivers a materially deficient statement, the buyer's right to cancel extends indefinitely, and the declarant may face additional liability for damages under Section 4-406.

Agents representing buyers in new-construction condo projects must verify that the Public Offering Statement has been filed with the Vermont Attorney General's office, as required by statute. Representing a buyer without confirming this filing exposes the agent to claims of inadequate representation.

## Vermont HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Even experienced agents trip over the same handful of errors in Vermont condo and HOA transactions. Here are five that show up repeatedly in complaint files and post-closing disputes.

**First, agents assume the listing agent is responsible for ordering the resale certificate.** The statutory obligation falls on the seller, not the agent. However, if you represent the seller and fail to advise them of this requirement, you bear the professional liability. Best practice: order the resale certificate as soon as the listing goes active, not after an offer comes in. Associations and management companies can take two to three weeks to produce the document.

Second, agents confuse properties governed by simple homeowner associations with those governed by CIOA. A neighborhood with a voluntary HOA that collects dues for snow plowing is not the same as a planned community created under CIOA with recorded covenants and a declaration. Only the latter triggers statutory resale certificate requirements. Agents must read the declaration and determine whether CIOA applies before advising on disclosure obligations—a diligence step that parallels the [need to verify HOA requirements in other states](/blog/wisconsin-hoa-condo-requirements-2026).

Third, agents fail to confirm that the resale certificate is complete. Handing over a budget summary and the association's bylaws does not satisfy Section 4-409. If the certificate omits pending litigation, reserve balances, or special assessment information, it is deficient. An incomplete certificate is functionally the same as no certificate at all for purposes of the buyer's cancellation right.

Fourth, agents neglect to document the date and method of delivery. Without a signed receipt or tracked delivery confirmation, there is no proof that the five-day cancellation period ever started. If a dispute arises, the burden falls on the seller to prove delivery. Agents should obtain a dated, written acknowledgment from the buyer or buyer's agent confirming receipt.

Fifth, agents do not account for the cancellation period in their contract timeline. If the purchase agreement sets a 30-day closing and the resale certificate is not delivered until day 20, the buyer still has until day 25 to cancel. Stacking the cancellation period against other contingency deadlines—inspection, financing, appraisal—can create a scheduling collision that delays or kills the deal. Building in a buffer from the start, and [using checklists rather than relying on memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory), prevents this.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers who supervise agents handling Vermont condo and HOA transactions carry their own layer of risk. Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296, the broker is responsible for the supervision of all licensees operating under their license. If an agent's failure to deliver a resale certificate leads to a buyer lawsuit or a Commission complaint, the broker's supervision practices will be scrutinized.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate ordered | Confirmation email or association receipt in transaction file | Every condo/HOA listing at activation |
| Certificate completeness | Cross-reference delivered document against 27A V.S.A. Section 4-409 checklist | Before buyer delivery |
| Delivery documentation | Signed acknowledgment of receipt with date | Before cancellation period expires |
| CIOA applicability determination | Copy of declaration with recording date noted | At listing intake |
| Public Offering Statement (new units) | AG filing confirmation and 15-day cancellation tracking | Every new-construction condo deal |

Brokers should build these audit steps into their transaction review workflow rather than relying on agents to self-report compliance. A quarterly file audit sampling at least 20 percent of condo and HOA transactions is a reasonable standard. Brokers who discover deficiencies should document the corrective action in writing and retain it in the agent's supervision file.

## Liability When Disclosure Fails

The consequences of noncompliance land on multiple parties. For the seller, failure to deliver a resale certificate means the buyer retains an open-ended cancellation right—a cloud over the transaction that can surface weeks or months after closing if the certificate was never properly delivered. Courts in Vermont have interpreted Section 4-409 strictly; the right does not quietly expire.

For the agent, a pattern of disclosure failures can trigger disciplinary action by the Vermont Real Estate Commission. Sanctions range from a written warning to license suspension or revocation. The Commission may also impose continuing education requirements or practice restrictions. Civil liability is a separate track—buyers who suffer financial harm from missing or misleading disclosures can pursue damages against the agent, the broker, and the seller.

For the broker, vicarious liability under supervision statutes means that a single agent's repeated failures can jeopardize the brokerage's license. The Commission has the authority to audit brokerage files and impose firm-level sanctions when systemic compliance breakdowns are evident.

> "A unit owner shall furnish to a purchaser before execution of any contract for sale of a unit, or otherwise before conveyance, a copy of the declaration, the bylaws, the rules or regulations of the association, and a certificate containing the information specified in this section." — 27A V.S.A. Section 4-409

That statutory language does not offer discretion. It does not say "when practicable" or "if requested by the buyer." It says "shall furnish," and the timeline is "before execution of any contract for sale." Agents who treat this as a post-contract task are operating outside the statutory framework.
