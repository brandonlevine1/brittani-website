## Understanding South Carolina FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every agent working coastal or resort markets in South Carolina eventually encounters a foreign national selling property. When that happens, the South Carolina FIRPTA withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 dictate a rigid compliance framework that can stall or kill a deal if mishandled. The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (IRC Sections 1445-1446) requires the buyer—or the buyer's agent acting on their behalf—to withhold 15% of the gross sales price when the seller is a "foreign person" as defined by the IRS.

South Carolina layers its own nonresident withholding on top of that federal obligation. Under SC Code Section 12-8-580, the withholding agent must remit either 7% of the gain recognized or 2% of the total sales price to the South Carolina Department of Revenue (SCDOR). The dual withholding structure means a foreign seller can see nearly 17% of the gross price held back before receiving net proceeds.

**Agents who assume the closing attorney handles everything often discover too late that their own liability exposure is real.** The IRS looks at whether the transferee's agent had "actual knowledge" that the seller was foreign. If you knew and failed to ensure withholding, you can be personally assessed under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-4.

## Federal Withholding: The 15% Rule and Its Exceptions

The standard FIRPTA withholding rate is 15% of the amount realized (the full contract price, not just the gain). A reduced rate of 10% applies when the buyer intends to use the property as a personal residence and the price is between $300,001 and $1,000,000. Sales at or below $300,000 to a buyer-occupant are exempt entirely.

Foreign sellers who believe their actual tax liability will be lower than the withheld amount can apply for a withholding certificate using IRS Form 8288-B. Processing typically takes 90 days, so agents listing property for foreign owners need to address this during the listing period—not during the contract countdown.

The withholding agent remits the tax using IRS Form 8288 and provides the seller with Form 8288-A as a receipt. These forms must be filed within 20 days of the transfer date. Missing that deadline triggers penalties and interest that attach to the buyer first, then cascade to anyone who facilitated the failure.

## South Carolina's Additional Nonresident Withholding Layer

South Carolina treats all nonresidents—including foreign nationals—as subject to state income tax withholding at the point of sale. SC Code Section 12-8-580 requires the withholding agent (usually the closing attorney) to collect either 7% of the recognized gain or 2% of the sales price, whichever method the seller elects.

| Requirement | Federal (FIRPTA) | South Carolina State |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding Rate | 15% of gross price (standard) | 7% of gain or 2% of sales price |
| Governing Authority | IRS, IRC Section 1445 | SCDOR, SC Code 12-8-580 |
| Filing Form | IRS Form 8288 | SC Form WH-1601 |
| Filing Deadline | 20 days after closing | 15 days after closing |
| Exemption Application | Form 8288-B | SC Form I-309 (affidavit of exemption) |

Sellers who qualify for an exemption from state withholding—because they are reinvesting proceeds in another South Carolina property or meet specific criteria—must file SC Form I-309 with the closing attorney before closing. Without that affidavit, the withholding proceeds regardless of the seller's intentions.

The SCDOR publishes [updated guidance on nonresident withholding](https://dor.sc.gov/tax/withholding) annually, and agents should confirm current rates each January since legislative adjustments can occur during the prior session.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

**Liability under FIRPTA does not stop at the buyer's doorstep.** Treasury Regulation 1.1445-4 states that the agent of the transferee (buyer's agent) who has actual knowledge that a seller's affidavit of non-foreign status is false can be held liable for the withholding amount. The agent of the transferor (listing agent) faces exposure too—if the listing agent knows the seller is foreign and facilitates a closing without proper withholding, the IRS can assess a penalty equal to the full amount that should have been withheld.

At the state level, SC Code Section 12-8-590 imposes penalties on the withholding agent who fails to remit. The penalty is 100% of the tax required to be withheld, plus interest accruing from the date of closing. The closing attorney typically bears this responsibility, but an agent who instructs or pressures the attorney to skip withholding shares the exposure.

Beyond financial penalties, the South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) can pursue disciplinary action under SC Code Section 40-57-145 for conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness. A pattern of FIRPTA noncompliance fits squarely within that statutory language. License suspension is not hypothetical—it is a documented outcome in commission enforcement files.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Foreign Seller Transactions

The first and most frequent error is failing to ask the question at all. Many agents never confirm residency status during the listing intake. By the time an attorney discovers the seller holds a foreign passport, the deal is days from closing and there is no time to obtain a withholding certificate.

The second mistake is confusing residency with citizenship. A green card holder is a "resident alien" for tax purposes and is exempt from FIRPTA. An individual on a work visa who has not met the substantial presence test remains a foreign person. Agents who assume any immigrant with a Social Security number is exempt create dangerous gaps in compliance.

Third, agents routinely accept the seller's oral assurance that they are a U.S. person without obtaining the written certification. IRS regulations require the buyer to obtain a written affidavit under penalties of perjury—known informally as a FIRPTA affidavit or non-foreign affidavit—that includes the seller's name, U.S. taxpayer identification number, and home address. Without this document, withholding is mandatory regardless of what the seller claims verbally.

Fourth, listing agents fail to account for withholding in the net sheet. A seller expecting $500,000 in proceeds who learns at the closing table that $85,000 is being withheld federally and another $10,000 at the state level is a seller who refuses to close. Early disclosure of the withholding obligation preserves the relationship and keeps the transaction on track.

Fifth, agents confuse FIRPTA withholding with the seller's final tax liability. Withholding is not a tax—it is a deposit. The seller files a U.S. tax return and may receive a refund if the actual capital gains tax is less than the amount withheld. Agents who tell sellers "you lose 15%" create unnecessary panic and sometimes lose the listing entirely.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerages operating in markets with significant international ownership—Hilton Head, Kiawah Island, Myrtle Beach, and the greater Charleston area—should have written FIRPTA procedures in their policy manual. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission expects brokers-in-charge to supervise licensees under SC Code Section 40-57-137, and that supervision extends to compliance procedures for specialty transactions.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake form | Includes residency/citizenship question | Every new listing |
| Non-foreign affidavit | Executed copy in transaction file | Before closing |
| Withholding certificate filing | Form 8288-B filed if applicable | 90+ days before close |
| Net sheet accuracy | Reflects both federal and state withholding | At listing and under contract |
| Closing attorney coordination | Written confirmation withholding will be remitted | 7 days before close |

**Brokers who rely on the closing attorney to catch these issues are outsourcing a duty they still own.** The transaction file should contain documentation proving the agent confirmed residency status, obtained the appropriate affidavit, and communicated withholding obligations to the seller in writing. If the file lacks this paper trail and a dispute arises, the brokerage's E&O carrier may deny coverage on the grounds that the agent failed to follow industry-standard practices.

Training is another enforcement gap. Annual continuing education in South Carolina requires 10 hours for license renewal, but FIRPTA is rarely covered in standard CE courses. Brokers should provide in-house training or require agents handling international transactions to complete a specialized course before representing foreign clients. This is particularly relevant for agents working with [transaction deadlines that commonly get missed](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in complex deals.

## The Timeline That Protects the Deal

Timing is the single biggest factor in smooth FIRPTA compliance. The withholding certificate application (Form 8288-B) takes 90 days minimum to process, which means a listing agent must raise the issue before the property hits the MLS. Waiting until a contract is ratified leaves no room for the IRS to act before closing.

A practical timeline begins at listing appointment. Confirm the seller's tax status. If they are foreign, discuss withholding immediately and connect them with a tax advisor who can prepare the Form 8288-B application. At contract ratification, notify the closing attorney in writing that the seller is a foreign person and that [proper deadline tracking](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) must include FIRPTA filing dates.

Fifteen days before closing, verify that the closing attorney has prepared Form 8288 and that both federal and state withholding amounts are reflected on the settlement statement. On closing day, confirm that withholding funds are being transmitted to the IRS and the SCDOR separately. After closing, ensure the seller receives Form 8288-A so they can file their U.S. tax return and claim any refund.
