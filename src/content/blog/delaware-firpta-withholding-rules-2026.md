## Why Delaware Firpta Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The Delaware firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 apply to every residential and commercial closing involving a non-U.S. person or entity. Agents who treat FIRPTA as someone else's problem risk personal liability, deal collapse, and disciplinary action from the Delaware Real Estate Commission. This is not a hypothetical risk—it is a statutory obligation backed by the Internal Revenue Code and enforced through penalties that land on the parties closest to the transaction.

Delaware's coastal resort markets in Sussex County and its corporate-heavy New Castle County attract significant foreign investment. The state does not impose its own separate foreign-seller withholding tax, but federal FIRPTA rules under IRC Section 1445 apply uniformly, and Delaware settlement practices place specific responsibilities on agents, title companies, and closing attorneys.

## The Federal Framework: IRC Section 1445 and How It Hits Delaware Closings

**FIRPTA—the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act—requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sale price and remit it to the IRS.** This is not a tax on the buyer. It is a withholding mechanism designed to ensure the IRS collects income tax from foreign sellers who might otherwise leave the country without filing a return.

The 15% rate applies to sales where the amount realized exceeds $300,000. For properties sold at or below $300,000 that the buyer intends to use as a personal residence, the withholding rate drops to zero. Sales between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer will use the property as a residence carry a reduced 10% withholding rate.

| Sale Price | Buyer Plans to Use as Residence | Withholding Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 or less | Yes | 0% |
| $300,001–$1,000,000 | Yes | 10% |
| Over $1,000,000 | Yes or No | 15% |
| Any amount | No (investment/commercial) | 15% |

These thresholds are tied to the amount realized, meaning the full sale price—not just the seller's gain. Agents working the Rehoboth Beach or Bethany Beach markets should pay close attention, because second-home and investment properties held by foreign nationals almost always trigger the full 15% rate.

## Forms That Must Be Filed—And Who Files Them

The primary IRS forms involved in a FIRPTA transaction are Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) and Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both must be filed within 20 days of the closing date, and copies of Form 8288-A are sent to the foreign seller so they can file their own U.S. tax return and claim credit for the withheld amount.

In Delaware, the settlement agent—typically a title company or closing attorney—handles the actual remittance. However, the legal obligation rests on the buyer as the transferee. If the buyer's agent knew or had reason to know the seller was foreign and failed to ensure proper withholding, the IRS can pursue the agent under the "transferee's agent" provisions of Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-1.

**If the foreign seller wants to reduce the withholding below the statutory rate, they must file IRS Form 8288-B (Application for Withholding Certificate) before closing.** This application asks the IRS to review the seller's expected gain and issue a certificate authorizing a lower withholding amount. Processing takes 90 days on average, so agents listing properties for foreign sellers need to flag this at the earliest possible stage—ideally at the listing agreement.

A fourth form, the [Affidavit of Non-Foreign Status](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9), is what domestic sellers sign to certify they are not foreign persons. In Delaware closings, this affidavit is almost always included in the settlement package. When it is missing or unsigned, that is your first red flag.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of FIRPTA noncompliance are not abstract. Under IRC Section 1445(e), the buyer who fails to withhold becomes personally liable for the tax that should have been withheld, plus interest from the date of closing. Penalties under IRC Section 6651 for failure to file Form 8288 on time can reach 25% of the unpaid amount.

Agents face exposure too. Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-1(e) defines a "transferee's agent" broadly—anyone who represents the buyer in the transaction and who participates in the transfer. If you are the buyer's agent and you knew the seller was foreign but did nothing to ensure withholding, the IRS can assess the full withholding liability against you. The Delaware Real Estate Commission, operating under 24 Del. C. Chapter 29, can also pursue disciplinary action for failure to exercise reasonable care in a transaction, which could include license suspension.

Deal cancellation is another real outcome. Title companies in Delaware will refuse to close if the FIRPTA issue is unresolved, because they do not want the liability either. A deal that falls apart at the closing table because no one addressed FIRPTA early enough is a deal where the agent's commission vanishes and their reputation takes a hit.

## Five Common Mistakes Delaware Agents Make With FIRPTA

The first mistake is assuming the title company will catch everything. Settlement agents in Delaware are generally diligent, but they rely on information you provide. If you never mention that your seller holds a Canadian passport or that the LLC selling the property is owned by a non-resident alien, the title company may not discover the issue until the day before closing—or worse, not at all.

The second mistake is confusing residency with citizenship. A foreign national who lives in Delaware full-time and holds a green card is generally treated as a U.S. person for FIRPTA purposes. But a U.S. citizen who has renounced citizenship may actually be treated as a foreign person. Agents who rely on accent, address, or assumptions instead of the affidavit process create unnecessary risk. Similar to how agents can miss critical deadlines across other compliance areas, [understanding why deadline tracking matters](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) can prevent this kind of oversight from compounding.

**The third mistake is failing to start the Form 8288-B process early enough.** Because the IRS takes up to 90 days to process withholding certificate applications, a seller who files the form after accepting an offer may not receive the certificate before the closing date. Agents should raise the 8288-B conversation at the listing appointment, not at contract execution.

The fourth mistake is misidentifying entity sellers. When a Delaware LLC, corporation, or partnership sells property, agents must determine whether the entity is domestically controlled. A Delaware LLC with a single foreign member is treated as a foreign person for FIRPTA purposes. This catches agents off guard because the entity is formed in Delaware and has a registered agent in Wilmington, which makes it look domestic on paper.

The fifth mistake is neglecting to keep documentation in the transaction file. Even when everything goes right, agents should retain copies of the non-foreign affidavit or the FIRPTA withholding documentation. The Delaware Real Estate Commission's record-keeping requirements under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 require brokers to maintain transaction records for a minimum of three years. If an audit or dispute arises later, you need that paper trail.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents in Delaware carry an independent obligation to ensure FIRPTA compliance across their brokerage. The Delaware Real Estate Commission holds brokers accountable under 24 Del. C. Section 2920 for the actions of their licensees, which means a single agent's FIRPTA failure can trigger a commission investigation of the entire office.

**Brokers should build a FIRPTA checkpoint into every transaction file review.** At minimum, this means verifying that one of two documents exists in every seller-side file: either a signed Affidavit of Non-Foreign Status or documentation that FIRPTA withholding was handled at closing with Forms 8288 and 8288-A filed. The absence of both is a compliance gap that needs immediate attention.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Look For | Red Flag if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Non-Foreign Affidavit | Signed by seller, includes TIN and address | No proof seller's status was verified |
| Form 8288 confirmation | Copy or settlement statement note showing withholding | Potential liability for the buyer and agents |
| Form 8288-B filing | Copy of application if reduced withholding was claimed | Closing may have proceeded without proper authority |
| Entity ownership verification | Operating agreement or corporate docs for LLC/corp sellers | Foreign ownership of entity may be hidden |
| Timeline documentation | Dates showing when FIRPTA was flagged in the transaction | Late identification increases deal failure risk |

Brokers should also consider training sessions specifically on FIRPTA identification. Many Delaware brokerages cover FIRPTA in passing during annual compliance meetings, but a dedicated 30-minute workshop with real scenarios—a Canadian couple selling their Lewes beach condo, a British-owned LLC disposing of a Greenville estate—is far more effective than a slide deck. ## State-Level Considerations Unique to Delaware

Delaware does not impose a separate state-level withholding tax on foreign sellers, which distinguishes it from states like Maryland and California that layer their own withholding requirements on top of FIRPTA. However, Delaware's Realty Transfer Tax under 30 Del. C. Chapter 54 applies to all sellers regardless of nationality, and the 4% combined state and county transfer tax rate (split between buyer and seller unless otherwise negotiated) still applies.

Foreign sellers must also comply with Delaware's income tax filing requirements if they recognize gain on the sale of Delaware real property. The Delaware Division of Revenue requires nonresidents—including foreign nationals—to file Form 200-02 NR for any Delaware-source income, which includes real estate gains. This is a separate obligation from FIRPTA and catches sellers who assume the federal withholding covers everything.

Agents should also be aware that [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wisconsin-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) apply with heightened urgency in foreign-seller transactions. International wire transfers are prime targets for fraud, and closing attorneys in Delaware have reported increasing attempts to redirect FIRPTA withholding remittances through spoofed email instructions.
