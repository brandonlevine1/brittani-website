The 2025 legislative session in Bismarck produced several bills that will reshape how residential real estate transactions work across the state. Understanding North Dakota new real estate laws 2026 is not optional — agents and brokers who fail to adapt risk disciplinary action, deal cancellations, and personal liability. The North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC) has already begun publishing guidance, and several updated forms are expected to go live before year-end.

This article breaks down the specific statutory changes, the forms you need to swap into your workflow, the penalties for noncompliance, and the audit items brokers should be reviewing right now.

## Updated Property Condition Disclosure Requirements

**North Dakota Century Code section 47-10-02.1 has been amended to expand the list of material conditions sellers must disclose.** The revised statute adds three new categories: known environmental contamination within 500 feet, history of basement or foundation water intrusion, and prior insurance claims related to structural damage. These additions bring North Dakota closer to the disclosure standards already in place in states like Wisconsin, which [recently updated its own seller disclosure framework](/blog/wisconsin-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026).

The NDREC will release an updated version of the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement, which agents informally refer to as the "47-10 form." The current form — last revised in 2021 — will no longer satisfy the statutory requirements after January 1, 2026. Agents who continue using the outdated version expose their sellers to liability and themselves to commission complaints.

Sellers must deliver the completed disclosure to the buyer no later than ten calendar days before closing, up from the prior standard of "before acceptance." This timing shift is significant. It means agents need to get the disclosure process started at listing, not after an offer arrives.

## Agency Disclosure and Dual Agency Changes Under North Dakota New Real Estate Laws 2026

North Dakota Administrative Code chapter 70-02-03 now requires agents to present a written agency disclosure at the first substantive contact with a prospective buyer or seller. The old standard — which tolerated verbal disclosure followed by a written confirmation — is gone. Written first, no exceptions.

**Dual agency consent has been tightened under the revised NDAC 70-02-03-01.** Both parties must sign a standalone dual agency acknowledgment form, separate from the purchase agreement. Previously, a clause embedded in the purchase agreement was sufficient. The standalone requirement means agents need a new form in their transaction files, and NDREC has indicated it will publish a model version by November 2025.

The penalty for failing to obtain proper dual agency consent is severe. Under the amended statute, a transaction closed without the standalone consent form is voidable at the election of either party for up to one year after closing. That is a longer rescission window than most agents realize, and it creates extended liability exposure for the brokerage.

## New Buyer Representation Agreement Mandates

Following the national trend accelerated by the NAR settlement agreement, North Dakota has codified buyer representation agreement requirements into state law. House Bill 1387, signed in April 2025, amends NDCC 43-23-11.1 to require a written buyer representation agreement before an agent shows any property to a prospective buyer.

The agreement must specify the duration of representation, the compensation structure, and the scope of services the agent will provide. It must also include a conspicuous statement that commission rates are negotiable. NDREC has signaled it will publish a model Buyer Representation Agreement form (anticipated form designation BRA-2026) alongside the other updated forms.

Agents who show property without a signed buyer representation agreement face potential license suspension. The first offense carries a mandatory 30-day suspension under the new enforcement schedule, and repeat violations can result in revocation. This is not a soft guideline — it carries teeth.

| Requirement | Before 2026 | After January 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer rep agreement before showing | Recommended, not required | Mandatory under NDCC 43-23-11.1 |
| Agency disclosure timing | Before offer or at first showing | At first substantive contact, written only |
| Dual agency consent format | Clause in purchase agreement | Standalone signed form required |
| Seller disclosure delivery deadline | Before acceptance | 10 calendar days before closing |

## Wire Fraud Prevention and Closing Procedures

Senate Bill 2214 introduces wire fraud prevention obligations for real estate licensees. While title companies and closing agents already follow verification protocols, the new law places an affirmative duty on both listing agents and buyer's agents to confirm wire instructions with their clients through a communication channel separate from email. If a client wires funds to a fraudulent account and the agent failed to provide this secondary verification, the agent may be held jointly liable under the statute.

**The practical effect is that agents must document — in writing — that they contacted their client by phone or in person to verify wire instructions before any funds transfer.** A simple file note saying "called client to confirm" is not sufficient. The statute requires a dated, signed acknowledgment from the client. Agents dealing with [similar wire fraud prevention rules in other states](/blog/wisconsin-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) will recognize the pattern, but North Dakota's version includes the joint liability provision, which is unusually aggressive.

The [North Dakota Real Estate Commission's website](https://www.realestatend.org/) is the authoritative source for updated forms and enforcement bulletins. Bookmark it now.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

Based on the statutory text and early guidance from NDREC, five errors are almost certain to plague agents in the first year of implementation.

**First, agents will keep using the old 47-10 disclosure form.** It is already loaded into every transaction management template in the state. Unless agents proactively swap in the 2026 version, the outdated form will auto-populate and nobody will catch it until a complaint is filed. Brokers and [transaction coordinators who manage checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) need to flag this immediately.

Second, agents will forget the standalone dual agency form. For years, checking a box in the purchase agreement was enough. That muscle memory will be hard to break. Expect NDREC complaint volume on this issue to spike in Q1 2026.

Third, buyer's agents will show property before getting a signed BRA-2026. The temptation to "just do a quick showing" for a walk-in buyer is enormous, and agents will rationalize that the agreement can be signed later. The statute does not allow it. The showing itself triggers the requirement.

Fourth, agents will treat the wire verification requirement as optional or informal. A phone call without a documented acknowledgment does not satisfy the statute. The documentation must exist in the file.

Fifth, agents will miscalculate the 10-day disclosure delivery window. The deadline runs backward from closing, not forward from listing. If closing is moved up — as often happens in competitive markets — the disclosure delivery date moves with it, and an agent who delivered "early enough" under the original timeline may suddenly be late.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under NDCC 43-23-12 for the acts of their affiliated agents. The 2026 amendments do not change this principle, but they expand the surface area of potential violations. A broker whose agent closes a deal without the standalone dual agency form is exposed to the same voidability risk as the agent.
