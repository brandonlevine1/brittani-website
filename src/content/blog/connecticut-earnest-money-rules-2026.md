## Understanding Connecticut Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Connecticut treats earnest money deposits with statutory precision that leaves little room for interpretation—or excuses. If you hold a Connecticut real estate license in 2026, the rules governing escrow deposits under **Connecticut earnest money & escrow laws 2026** are not suggestions; they are enforceable requirements backed by fines, license actions, and civil exposure. The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection (DCP), which houses the Real Estate Commission, audits escrow accounts and has increased enforcement activity over the past two years.

This post breaks down the exact statutes, timelines, forms, and pitfalls that active agents and their managing brokers face when handling deposits in residential and commercial transactions across the state.

## The Governing Statutes You Need to Know

Connecticut's escrow framework lives primarily in **Connecticut General Statutes (Conn. Gen. Stat.) Section 20-324a** through 20-324f. Section 20-324a establishes the fundamental rule: a real estate broker who receives money belonging to others in connection with a real estate transaction must deposit those funds into a separate, designated escrow account at a Connecticut bank or credit union. The deposit must occur within two banking days of receipt unless the parties' written agreement specifies otherwise.

Section 20-324e spells out prohibited conduct, including commingling personal or operating funds with escrow money and disbursing escrow deposits without written authorization from all parties (or a court order). Violations can trigger action under **Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-320**, which grants the Real Estate Commission authority to suspend, revoke, or refuse to renew a license.

**The Real Estate Commission's Regulations Section 20-328-8a further details recordkeeping requirements.** Brokers must maintain a running ledger showing the source, amount, date received, and date deposited for every escrow item. These records must be retained for a minimum of three years after the transaction closes or terminates—whichever is later.

## Connecticut-Specific Forms and Contractual Provisions

Most residential transactions in Connecticut use forms promulgated or endorsed by the Connecticut Association of Realtors (CAR). The standard **Residential Purchase and Sale Agreement (CAR Form RS-2)** includes designated fields for earnest money amount, the entity holding the deposit, the deposit deadline, and conditions for release. Agents should pay close attention to Paragraph 3 of the RS-2, which governs the deposit terms.

For disputes, CAR Form **ED-1 (Escrow Deposit Release)** provides a bilateral release mechanism. Both buyer and seller must sign before the escrow holder disburses contested funds. If one party refuses, the broker cannot unilaterally release—except under limited circumstances outlined in Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-324f, which permits interpleader or a request for written instructions after 90 days of dispute.

| Form | Purpose | When Used |
|------|---------|-----------|
| CAR RS-2 | Residential Purchase & Sale Agreement | Contract execution |
| CAR ED-1 | Escrow Deposit Release | Mutual release of deposit |
| CAR AM-1 | Amendment to Contract | Modifying deposit terms |
| Broker Escrow Ledger (internal) | Transaction-level tracking | Upon receipt through disbursement |

Agents who work with attorneys drafting custom contracts should confirm that deposit provisions align with Section 20-324a requirements. A contract that says "deposit due within 14 days" overrides the two-banking-day rule, but only if the broker does not physically receive the funds earlier.

## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

**A single escrow violation can end a career.** The Connecticut DCP treats escrow mishandling as one of the most serious infractions a licensee can commit. Under Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-320(a)(5), the Commission can revoke a license for "failure to account for or remit any moneys coming into the licensee's possession that belong to others." No hearing is required before an immediate suspension if the Commission finds the public is at risk.

Financial penalties vary. The Commission may impose fines up to $25,000 per violation under its administrative authority. Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability: a buyer whose deposit is mishandled can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, or violation of the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act (CUTPA, Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 42-110a et seq.), which allows recovery of punitive damages and attorney fees.

Deal cancellation is another consequence. If an agent fails to deposit funds within the required timeline, either party may argue the contract is unenforceable or that a material breach has occurred. Courts have sided with buyers seeking return of deposits where brokers violated statutory deposit deadlines—even when the buyer was otherwise in default. Agents who think [missed deadlines are minor oversights](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) are playing with borrowed time.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Connecticut Escrow

**Mistake 1: Holding the check "until the inspection is done."** Some agents delay depositing earnest money because the buyer wants to wait until after the inspection contingency. Connecticut law does not permit this unless the purchase agreement explicitly states the deposit is not due until a specific later date. If the agent has possession of the check, the two-banking-day clock is ticking.

**Mistake 2: Depositing into the brokerage operating account.** This is textbook commingling. Even temporarily routing a deposit through a general business account violates Section 20-324a. Some brokerages have made this error when their escrow account is at a different bank than where they do daily business. Convenience does not override statute.

**Mistake 3: Releasing deposits without dual authorization.** When a deal falls apart and the buyer asks for their deposit back, agents sometimes release funds based on a phone call or a single party's email. Connecticut requires written consent from all parties or a court order. The only exception is the 90-day interpleader process under Section 20-324f.

**Mistake 4: Failing to notify the broker of deposit receipt.** Agents are not the escrow holders—brokers are. When an agent receives a check at a showing or open house and does not immediately notify the managing broker, the statutory clock still runs. Responsibility flows upward, and the broker bears ultimate liability.

**Mistake 5: Not documenting earnest money receipt on the transaction file.** The Commission expects a paper trail from the moment a deposit changes hands. If an auditor asks for proof of the date received versus date deposited and the agent has no contemporaneous record, the presumption runs against the licensee.

These errors are exactly [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down)—not over price negotiations, but over procedural lapses that create legal exposure and erode client trust.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Managing brokers in Connecticut carry personal liability for escrow account compliance. Regulation Section 20-328-8a mandates that the broker designee maintain and supervise all escrow records. This is not delegable—a broker cannot claim ignorance because an agent or office manager handled the deposits.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Statute/Regulation |
|---|---|---|
| Escrow account reconciliation | Monthly (minimum) | Reg. 20-328-8a |
| Ledger entry verification | Per transaction | Reg. 20-328-8a |
| Deposit timing (2 banking days) | Per transaction | Conn. Gen. Stat. 20-324a |
| Signatory authorization review | Annually | Brokerage policy / DCP guidance |
| Record retention (3-year minimum) | Ongoing | Reg. 20-328-8a |

Brokers should conduct monthly reconciliations comparing the escrow bank statement balance against the sum of all open transaction ledger balances. Any discrepancy—even a few dollars caused by bank fees—must be identified and corrected immediately. The DCP has cited brokerages for shortages as small as the cost of a check-printing fee that was incorrectly charged against the escrow account.

Beyond reconciliation, brokers need a written escrow policy distributed to every agent. That policy should specify who is authorized to accept deposits, the exact procedure for logging receipt, and the chain of custody from agent to broker to bank. Relying on memory or verbal training is insufficient; the Commission expects documentation. Agents who think [checklists are overkill](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) have never been on the receiving end of a DCP audit letter.

## Escrow Disputes and the Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller cannot agree on deposit disposition, Connecticut provides a structured path. Under Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-324f, the escrow holder must retain the deposit and may not disburse it to either party without mutual written consent or a court order. After 90 days of unresolved dispute, the broker may file an interpleader action in Superior Court, depositing the funds with the court and requesting release from liability.

The interpleader route protects brokers from being caught in the crossfire, but it comes with costs. Filing fees, potential attorney fees, and the administrative burden of a court filing all fall on the brokerage unless the purchase agreement allocates those costs differently. Many experienced Connecticut attorneys recommend including an interpleader cost-allocation clause in the RS-2 addendum to avoid ambiguity.

> "A broker who holds disputed funds beyond 90 days without seeking interpleader or obtaining a release may face Commission scrutiny for inaction, even though no disbursement violation occurred."
> — Connecticut DCP Interpretive Guidance, 2024 Update

This is a nuance that catches brokers off guard. Holding money indefinitely is not a safe harbor. The Commission views indefinite retention without action as a potential failure to properly account for funds belonging to others.

## Interest-Bearing Escrow Accounts and Disclosure

Connecticut permits—but does not require—brokers to hold escrow deposits in interest-bearing accounts. If a broker uses an interest-bearing account, [Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 20-324a(b)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_392.htm#sec_20-324a) requires written disclosure to all parties regarding who receives the interest. In practice, most residential deposits are held in non-interest-bearing accounts to avoid the administrative complexity and tax reporting obligations.

If interest is earned and not properly disclosed or disbursed, the broker faces both a regulatory violation and potential CUTPA liability. The safest approach for most brokerages is maintaining a non-interest-bearing escrow account and documenting that choice in the purchase agreement.
