## Why North Dakota Teams & Supervision Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission has never been casual about brokerage supervision, but the enforcement pattern heading into 2026 signals a sharper focus on team structures. If you operate a team — or broker one — the **North Dakota teams & supervision rules 2026** framework requires a clear understanding of statutory obligations, advertising standards, and supervisory accountability. Getting any of these wrong carries consequences far beyond a letter of warning.

North Dakota's real estate licensing laws are housed primarily in [North Dakota Century Code Chapter 43-23.3](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t43c23-3.html) and the administrative rules found in North Dakota Administrative Code Title 70. These two sources form the backbone of every team-related compliance question agents ask. They define who can do what, how brokers must oversee it, and what penalties apply when someone cuts corners.

**The state's approach to teams is structurally different from neighboring states like Wisconsin or Wyoming.** North Dakota does not have a separate "team license" or formal team registration process. Instead, every team member must hold an individual license under a single designated broker, and the brokerage itself is the legal entity that matters. The team name is a marketing construct, not a regulatory one — and that distinction trips up agents regularly.

## The Statutory Framework: NDCC 43-23.3 and NDAC Title 70

North Dakota Century Code Section 43-23.3-14 establishes the designated broker's duty to supervise all licensees associated with the brokerage. This is not aspirational language. The statute places affirmative responsibility on the broker to ensure every licensed activity — from advertising to trust account deposits — meets legal standards.

Section 43-23.3-02 defines the licensing tiers. A "salesperson" must work under a broker. An "associate broker" holds a broker's license but chooses to operate under another broker's supervision. Both of these licensees can participate in teams, but neither can function independently of the designated broker's oversight.

NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 adds the advertising rules that catch most teams off guard. Every advertisement, including social media posts, yard signs, and website headers, must display the brokerage name at least as prominently as the team name. The team cannot appear to be a standalone entity. This rule is enforced through complaint-driven reviews and the Commission's own monitoring.

## How Teams Actually Work Under North Dakota Law

Since North Dakota has no team-specific license category, forming a team is essentially an internal brokerage arrangement. The designated broker agrees to allow a group of licensees to market themselves under a shared team name. That agreement should be memorialized in writing, though the state does not prescribe a specific form for it.

**North Dakota Real Estate Commission Form 201 (Application for License) is the starting point for every team member.** Each person on the team files their own Form 201, listing the same designated broker. There is no group application. If a team member transfers from another brokerage, they must file Form 203 (Transfer of License) and have it processed before conducting any business under the new brokerage umbrella.

The practical effect is that a "team" in North Dakota is really just a marketing identity layered on top of individual licensee-broker relationships. The Commission does not track teams as entities. It tracks individual licensees and their brokerages. When something goes wrong, the Commission looks at the individual licensee's conduct and the broker's supervisory compliance — not the team as a unit.

## Advertising Rules That Teams Get Wrong

NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 is the single most violated rule among North Dakota teams. The regulation requires that the licensed brokerage name appear in all advertising and that it be displayed at least as prominently as any team or personal branding. "Prominently" is measured by size, placement, and visual weight — not just by whether it appears somewhere on the page.

A team called "Fargo Elite Group" cannot run a Facebook ad with "Fargo Elite Group" in 24-point bold font and the brokerage name in 10-point gray text at the bottom. The Commission has issued guidance clarifying that comparable font size, color contrast, and positioning are all factors. This applies equally to printed materials, digital ads, email signatures, and video thumbnails.

Yard signs are another recurring problem area. Many national sign vendors ship templates that feature the team name prominently with a small broker logo below. In North Dakota, that layout may violate NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 if the brokerage identification is not visually equivalent. Brokers who allow non-compliant signs to remain in the field share in the liability, as the supervision duty under NDCC 43-23.3-14 extends to advertising oversight.

## Common Mistakes Agents and Teams Make

**First, teams fail to update advertising when a member leaves the brokerage.** A departing agent's name or photo lingering on team websites, social profiles, or printed brochures creates the impression that the person is still licensed under that broker. This violates NDCC 43-23.3-10, which prohibits practicing under a license that is not current and properly placed.

Second, team leaders sometimes handle earnest money or trust account deposits without following the designated broker's procedures. Under NDCC 43-23.3-16, all trust funds must be deposited into the brokerage's trust account within three business days of receipt. A team leader who maintains a separate "team account" is operating outside the law — there is no provision for team-level trust accounts in North Dakota.

Third, teams routinely neglect agency disclosure timing. North Dakota requires that agency relationships be disclosed using the Commission-approved Agency Disclosure Form at the earliest substantive contact with a client. When multiple team members interact with the same client, it is common for everyone to assume someone else handled the disclosure. That assumption leads to violations of NDCC 43-23.3-08, which governs agency relationships and disclosures.

Fourth, unlicensed team assistants sometimes cross the line from administrative tasks into licensed activity. Showing property, discussing contract terms, or negotiating on behalf of a client all require a license. A team that allows an unlicensed assistant to perform these functions exposes both the assistant (who could face criminal penalties under NDCC 43-23.3-20) and the supervising broker to enforcement action.

Fifth, continuing education lapses within teams create cascading problems. Each team member must independently complete [North Dakota's CE requirements](/blog/wisconsin-continuing-education-requirements-2026) — there is no team-level compliance credit. When one member's license lapses, every transaction they touched during the lapse period is potentially tainted.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry the heaviest compliance burden under North Dakota law. NDCC 43-23.3-14 does not offer a "reasonable effort" defense — if a licensee under your supervision violates the law, the Commission can and does hold the broker accountable. This makes regular auditing not optional but existential.

**Start with an advertising audit at least quarterly.** Pull every active advertisement — digital and physical — for every team operating under your brokerage. Verify that the brokerage name appears with proper prominence. Check that no departed agents appear in active materials. Document each audit with dates and findings.

Trust account reconciliation is the second critical audit category. Under NDAC 70-02-04-01, the designated broker must reconcile trust accounts monthly. For brokerages with multiple teams, this means verifying that no team is maintaining a shadow account and that every deposit is traceable to the correct transaction file.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Key Statute/Rule | What to Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | Quarterly minimum | NDAC 70-02-03-01.1 | Brokerage name prominence, departed agent removal |
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | NDAC 70-02-04-01 | No shadow accounts, deposits within 3 business days |
| Agency disclosure files | Per transaction | NDCC 43-23.3-08 | Signed disclosure at earliest substantive contact |
| License status verification | Monthly | NDCC 43-23.3-10 | All team members active and properly placed |
| Unlicensed activity review | Ongoing | NDCC 43-23.3-20 | Assistants not performing licensed activities |

Transaction file review is the third pillar. Every file should contain the signed agency disclosure, proof of timely trust fund deposit, and documentation that the licensee handling the transaction held an active license throughout. Brokers who delegate file review to team leaders still bear ultimate responsibility, so spot-checking delegated reviews is a necessary layer. ## Penalties for Non-Compliance: What Is Actually at Stake

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission has a graduated enforcement toolkit. For minor first-time violations, the Commission may issue a letter of concern or require corrective action. For more serious or repeated violations, NDCC 43-23.3-19 authorizes the Commission to suspend or revoke a license, impose civil penalties, or require additional education.

Fines under North Dakota law can reach up to $2,000 per violation under NDCC 43-23.3-19. That number may sound modest, but each instance of non-compliant advertising, each improperly handled trust deposit, and each missed disclosure counts as a separate violation. A team of five running non-compliant ads across three platforms could generate dozens of individual violations in a single audit.

**Beyond Commission penalties, non-compliance exposes agents and brokers to civil liability.** A buyer who discovers that agency was never properly disclosed can seek rescission of the contract or damages in court. Trust account violations can trigger criminal referrals under NDCC 43-23.3-20. The reputational damage alone — the Commission publishes disciplinary actions — can end a career in a market as relationship-driven as North Dakota's.

## Supervision in a Remote and Hybrid World

North Dakota's geography creates supervision challenges that states with dense metro areas do not face. A broker in Bismarck supervising a team member in Williston is separated by more than 200 miles of prairie. The supervision duty under NDCC 43-23.3-14 does not include a geographic proximity requirement, but it does demand meaningful oversight regardless of distance.

Digital tools help close that gap. Brokers should implement systems that allow real-time review of transaction files, advertising drafts, and trust account activity. Relying on [checklists instead of memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is one of the simplest ways to ensure that remote team members meet every compliance checkpoint without the broker physically reviewing each step in person.

The Commission has not issued specific guidance on virtual supervision standards, but the existing statutory framework makes the expectation clear: the designated broker must be able to demonstrate that supervision occurred. Emails, timestamped file reviews, and documented policy manuals all serve as evidence if a complaint triggers an investigation.

This agreement should address advertising approval workflows, trust account procedures, agency disclosure responsibilities, and the process for removing departing members from all marketing materials.
