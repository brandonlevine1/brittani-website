## Understanding Kentucky Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

Kentucky earnest money & escrow laws 2026 remain among the most scrutinized compliance areas for agents operating in the Commonwealth. The Kentucky Real Estate Commission (KREC), operating under the authority of KRS Chapter 324, treats escrow violations as grounds for disciplinary action that can end a career. Every agent and managing broker needs to know not just the spirit of these rules but their precise statutory teeth.

Earnest money is the buyer's good-faith deposit signaling intent to purchase. In Kentucky, the handling of that deposit is governed by KRS 324.160, KREC administrative regulations under 201 KAR Chapter 11, and the standard contract forms published by the Kentucky Association of Realtors (KAR). Mishandling these funds—even innocently—exposes both the individual licensee and the supervising broker to fines, license action, and civil lawsuits.

## Statutory Framework: KRS 324.160 and 201 KAR 11:121

**KRS 324.160 is the bedrock statute governing escrow accounts in Kentucky.** It mandates that any money received by a licensee on behalf of others must be deposited in a separate, federally insured escrow or trust account maintained by the broker. The statute explicitly prohibits commingling these funds with the broker's personal or operating accounts.

201 KAR 11:121 adds regulatory detail. It requires that escrow accounts be clearly labeled to indicate their trust nature, that records be maintained for a minimum of five years, and that the principal broker maintain oversight of all deposits and disbursements. KREC auditors can request escrow records at any time without prior notice.

The distinction between these two authorities matters. KRS 324.160 carries the force of state law—violations can trigger criminal referrals in extreme cases. 201 KAR 11:121 gives KREC the administrative power to fine, suspend, or revoke licenses without waiting for a court proceeding. Agents face exposure on both fronts simultaneously.

## Deposit Timing and the KAR Standard Forms

The Kentucky Association of Realtors publishes standardized purchase contract forms used across the state. Form KAR F201—the Purchase Agreement—contains specific language about earnest money deposit timing. Under current form language, the escrow holder must deposit the check by the close of the next business day after receipt.

This is tighter than many agents realize. If a buyer hands an agent a check on Friday afternoon, that check must reach the escrow account by the close of business Monday. Holding a check in a desk drawer over a long weekend—or waiting until you "get around to it"—violates both the contract terms and KREC regulations.

Form KAR F205, the Earnest Money Receipt, serves as the documentation that the deposit was received and identifies the escrow holder. Agents who fail to issue this form create an evidentiary gap that becomes a liability issue if the transaction later falls into dispute. [Missed deadlines cause real damage](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in these situations, and earnest money timing is no exception.

## Disbursement Rules and Dispute Resolution

**Kentucky law does not permit a broker to unilaterally release disputed earnest money to either party.** When a transaction falls apart and both the buyer and seller claim entitlement to the deposit, KRS 324.160 requires the broker to hold the funds until receiving either mutual written authorization from both parties or a court order.

| Scenario | Required Action | Timeline |
|----------|----------------|----------|
| Mutual release signed | Disburse per agreement | Within 5 business days |
| One party demands, other silent | Hold funds, notify both parties in writing | Immediately upon dispute |
| Both parties claim funds | Hold funds, advise parties to seek legal counsel or mediation | Indefinite until resolved |
| Court order received | Disburse per order | Per court directive |
| Interpleader filed by broker | Deposit funds with court | Per court filing rules |

If the dispute lingers, the broker may file an interpleader action in circuit court, depositing the funds with the clerk and asking the court to determine rightful ownership. This protects the broker from liability to either side but does involve legal fees. Some brokers build interpleader cost provisions into their office policies.

The KAR Purchase Agreement (F201) includes a mediation clause that encourages parties to resolve earnest money disputes through the local board's mediation program before resorting to litigation. Agents should remind clients of this option early in any dispute, as it typically resolves faster and cheaper than court.

## Common Mistakes Kentucky Agents Make With Escrow Funds

Even experienced agents stumble on escrow compliance. KREC disciplinary records reveal recurring patterns that cost licensees their standing.

**First, agents deposit earnest money into the wrong account.** This happens most often in teams where a team leader maintains a separate business account. Kentucky law is explicit: only the principal broker's designated escrow account is acceptable. Team accounts, personal accounts, and LLC operating accounts are all violations of KRS 324.160 regardless of intent.

Second, agents accept cash deposits without issuing proper documentation. Form KAR F205 exists for exactly this purpose, yet KREC complaints regularly cite situations where cash changed hands with nothing more than a text message confirmation. Without a signed receipt specifying the amount, date, and escrow holder, the agent has no defense if the buyer later claims a larger sum was deposited.

Third, agents communicate earnest money deadlines incorrectly to buyers. The deposit deadline in Form F201 runs from the date of acceptance, not the date the buyer signed. If a counteroffer extends negotiations by several days, the deposit clock resets upon final mutual acceptance. Miscounting this date leaves the buyer technically in breach and gives the seller a potential exit from the contract.

Fourth, agents release funds based on verbal authorization. A phone call from the seller saying "just give it back to them" is not sufficient under Kentucky law. KREC requires written, signed mutual release—Form KAR F206 (Earnest Money Release Agreement) is the standard vehicle for this. Anything less exposes the broker to claims from the party who later changes their mind.

Fifth, agents fail to inform their broker about escrow issues promptly. Under 201 KAR 11:121, the principal broker bears ultimate responsibility for escrow account integrity. When an agent sits on a dispute for days or weeks without escalating, the broker loses the ability to intervene early and the [entire deal can break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in ways that multiply everyone's liability.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers in Kentucky carry personal regulatory exposure for every escrow dollar that flows through their accounts. KREC does not accept "I didn't know" as a defense when an agent under supervision mishandles deposits.

**Brokers should conduct monthly reconciliation of all escrow accounts against transaction files.** 201 KAR 11:121 requires that records include the property address, parties' names, deposit amount, date received, and date deposited for every transaction. A ledger discrepancy—even a temporary one—can trigger a formal KREC investigation during a routine audit.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Source Document |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Bank statement reconciliation | Monthly | Bank records vs. transaction ledger |
| Deposit timing verification | Per transaction | F205 receipt date vs. bank deposit date |
| Disbursement authorization | Per transaction | Signed F206 or closing statement |
| Account label compliance | Annually | Bank account documentation |
| Record retention check | Annually | Five-year retention per 201 KAR 11:121 |

Brokers who rely solely on agents self-reporting are gambling with their license. Implementing a system where deposit receipts are uploaded within 24 hours of receipt—and flagged if missing—reduces the window for errors. [A disciplined checklist approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) to escrow documentation protects both the brokerage and individual agents from preventable violations.

KREC can impose fines of up to $5,000 per violation under KRS 324.160(4). For repeat offenders or egregious cases, the commission moves toward suspension or revocation. The [Kentucky Real Estate Commission's website](https://krec.ky.gov) publishes disciplinary actions publicly, meaning an escrow violation becomes a permanent stain on a licensee's professional reputation.

## Liability Consequences Beyond KREC Discipline

Regulatory fines and license actions represent only one layer of exposure. Civil liability opens a second front. A buyer whose earnest money is improperly disbursed can sue the agent, the broker, and the brokerage for conversion—the legal equivalent of theft, even when the disbursement was merely negligent rather than intentional.

Kentucky courts have awarded actual damages plus attorney fees in escrow mishandling cases. In scenarios involving bad faith, punitive damages enter the picture. The broker's errors and omissions insurance may cover some of these claims, but policies typically exclude coverage for regulatory fines and intentional acts—leaving the licensee personally exposed.

Transaction cancellation is often the immediate practical consequence. When a buyer's agent fails to deposit on time, the seller's agent may advise their client to issue a breach notice and terminate. The buyer loses the property, the agent loses the commission, and the resulting complaint to KREC triggers an investigation. A single deposit timing error can cascade into five-figure financial losses before anyone sets foot in a courtroom.
