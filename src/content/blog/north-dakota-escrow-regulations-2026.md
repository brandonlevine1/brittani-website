# North Dakota Escrow Regulations 2026: What Every Agent and Broker Must Get Right

Mishandling escrow in North Dakota is one of the fastest ways to lose your license, kill a deal, or both. The North Dakota escrow regulations 2026 framework rests primarily on the North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 43-23 and the administrative rules enforced by the North Dakota Real Estate Commission. If you have not reviewed how these rules apply to your day-to-day practice recently, this is the year to fix that.

Escrow disputes are also the single largest category of complaints the Commission fields from consumers. The good news is that compliance is straightforward once you understand the statutes, the required forms, and the audit trail your broker needs to maintain.

## The Statutory Foundation: NDCC 43-23 and Administrative Code 70-02-03

North Dakota's trust fund rules are codified in [NDCC 43-23-11](https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t43c23.pdf), which mandates that every broker maintain a trust account at a federally insured financial institution located in North Dakota. The statute is explicit: commingling personal or business operating funds with client trust funds is a violation that can trigger license suspension or revocation. There is no gray area.

**North Dakota Administrative Code 70-02-03 adds the operational details that agents often overlook.** It requires that trust account records include a journal of all receipts and disbursements plus individual ledger sheets for every transaction. These records must be preserved for a minimum of three years from the date of closing or the date the transaction terminates, whichever is later.

The Commission can order an audit of a broker's trust account at any time without prior notice. That authority comes directly from NDCC 43-23-16, which grants investigative power over any licensee suspected of violating Chapter 43-23. The practical implication is simple: if you cannot produce clean records on demand, you are already in violation.

## Forms Agents Must Know and Use Correctly

North Dakota residential transactions rely on a set of standardized forms published and maintained by the North Dakota Association of Realtors (NDAR). The purchase agreement form — NDAR Form No. 1 — includes specific escrow-related provisions that govern how earnest money is handled, who holds it, and what triggers its release or return. Agents who modify these provisions without broker approval or legal review are inviting liability.

NDAR Form No. 1 requires that the earnest money amount, the designated holder, and the deposit deadline all be filled in completely. Leaving any of those fields blank does not create flexibility; it creates ambiguity that leads to disputes. The form also references the conditions under which earnest money is disbursed, including contingency failures, mutual cancellation, and default.

For transactions involving addenda, NDAR Form No. 4 (the general addendum) and NDAR Form No. 15 (the inspection contingency addendum) both contain clauses that can affect escrow timelines. Agents should cross-reference every addendum against the original purchase agreement to confirm that deadlines do not conflict. Conflicting dates on contingency expirations and escrow release are a recurring source of deals falling apart, a pattern we explored in depth in our post on [where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

## Earnest Money Handling: The Rules That Bite

**Earnest money must be deposited promptly, and "promptly" has teeth in North Dakota.** While the Century Code does not specify an exact hour count the way some states do, the Commission interprets the requirement through the lens of reasonable business practice. If your purchase agreement states that earnest money will be deposited within two business days, that is now a contractual obligation enforceable by both the Commission and the courts.

Here is where agents get tripped up: holding a check "until the seller signs" is not permitted unless the purchase agreement explicitly authorizes that practice. The default expectation under NDCC 43-23-11 is that funds received in connection with a real estate transaction are deposited into the trust account without delay. An agent who tucks a check into a desk drawer for a week is violating the statute, full stop.

Disbursement is equally regulated. Earnest money cannot be released from the trust account until one of three things happens: both parties provide written authorization, a court order directs disbursement, or the conditions stated in the purchase agreement are satisfied. Unilateral release by the listing agent or the buyer's agent is a violation. This is a recurring area of confusion that mirrors issues in other states — [Wisconsin's earnest money rules](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026) provide a useful comparison for agents licensed in multiple jurisdictions.

| Escrow Action | Who Is Authorized | Governing Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit earnest money into trust | Designated broker or title company | NDCC 43-23-11 |
| Disburse upon mutual written release | Both buyer and seller (signed) | Purchase agreement terms |
| Disburse upon contingency failure | Per contract terms, verified by broker | NDAR Form No. 1 provisions |
| Disburse upon court order | Court of competent jurisdiction | NDCC 43-23-11 |
| Interplead disputed funds | Broker, via district court | North Dakota Rules of Civil Procedure |

When a dispute arises and neither party will authorize release, the broker's safest option is to interplead the funds with the district court. Sitting on disputed escrow money for months without action is itself a compliance risk.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Agents Their Licenses

Five errors appear in Commission disciplinary actions far more often than any others.

**First, agents fail to identify the escrow holder on the purchase agreement.** NDAR Form No. 1 has a designated field for this, and leaving it blank — or writing in a name that does not correspond to a licensed broker or authorized title company — creates an immediate compliance gap. The Commission treats this as a failure of supervision, which means the broker is on the hook even if the salesperson filled out the form.

Second, agents confuse "receipt" with "deposit." Receiving a check from the buyer is not the same as depositing it into the trust account. The clock on the deposit deadline starts when the agent physically or electronically receives the funds, not when they hand the check to the office manager. Agents who operate in rural areas with limited banking access need to build extra travel time into their workflows rather than treating geography as an excuse.

Third, agents release earnest money based on verbal instructions. A phone call from the other agent saying "my client wants to cancel, go ahead and release" is not written authorization. The Commission requires signed documentation — either a mutual release form or a cancellation agreement — before funds leave the trust account.

Fourth, agents fail to track contingency deadlines that trigger automatic escrow consequences. When an inspection contingency expires without a written objection, the contract may deem the contingency waived, which changes the escrow posture of the deal entirely. Missed deadlines are endemic in this industry, and [understanding why agents miss them](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) is the first step toward building a system that prevents it.

Fifth, agents deposit earnest money into their personal bank accounts. This is the most serious violation on the list and almost always results in license revocation. It does not matter whether the agent intended to transfer the funds later. The moment client money touches a personal account, the agent has violated NDCC 43-23-11.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear the ultimate responsibility for trust account compliance in North Dakota. NDCC 43-23-11.1 makes it clear that the supervising broker is accountable for the acts of every salesperson operating under their license. Delegation of escrow tasks does not equal delegation of liability.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Source Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | NDAC 70-02-03 |
| Individual transaction ledger review | Per transaction, at closing or termination | NDAC 70-02-03 |
| Verification of deposit timeliness | Each deposit | NDCC 43-23-11 |
| Review of disbursement authorization docs | Each disbursement | NDCC 43-23-11 |
| Three-year record retention confirmation | Annually | NDAC 70-02-03 |

**Monthly bank reconciliations are not optional — they are the single most important audit function a broker performs.** The reconciliation must match the bank statement balance to the sum of all individual transaction ledger balances. Any discrepancy must be investigated and resolved within the same month. Brokers who wait until the Commission knocks on the door to reconcile their accounts are already in trouble.

Brokers should also implement a written office policy that specifies how earnest money is received, logged, deposited, and disbursed. That policy should name the individuals authorized to handle trust funds, the backup procedures when those individuals are unavailable, and the documentation required for every step. The Commission looks favorably on brokerages that can produce a written policy during an audit — and harshly on those that cannot.

## Penalties for Noncompliance: What Is Actually at Stake

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission has graduated enforcement authority. Minor violations — such as a single late deposit with no consumer harm — may result in a letter of warning or a consent agreement requiring additional continuing education. Repeated or willful violations escalate to formal hearings under NDCC 43-23-16.

At the formal hearing stage, the Commission can impose fines, mandate supervised practice, suspend a license for a defined period, or revoke the license entirely. Revocation is not theoretical; the Commission publishes disciplinary actions on its website, and trust account violations appear regularly. Beyond the Commission, an agent who mishandles escrow funds may face civil liability from the harmed party and, in cases involving intentional misappropriation, criminal prosecution under North Dakota's theft statutes.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical consequence. When escrow problems surface during a transaction, buyers lose confidence, sellers get anxious, and attorneys get involved. The cost of unwinding a deal — appraisals already ordered, inspections already paid for, moving plans already set — falls on the parties, but the reputational damage lands squarely on the agent.

## Building a Compliance System That Actually Works

The agents who stay out of trouble are the ones who treat escrow handling as a system rather than a series of one-off tasks. That means checklists, calendar reminders tied to specific contract dates, and a clear chain of custody for every check or wire received.

North Dakota escrow regulations 2026 are not changing dramatically from prior years, but the Commission's enforcement posture has tightened. Brokers who audit consistently and agents who follow the forms as written will not have problems. Everyone else is gambling — and the house, in this case the North Dakota Real Estate Commission, always wins.
