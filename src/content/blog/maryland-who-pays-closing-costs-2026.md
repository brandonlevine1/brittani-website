## Maryland Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Statutory Framework

The question of Maryland who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 does not have a single statutory answer, and that is precisely why agents keep getting it wrong. Maryland law establishes certain mandatory costs tied to specific parties but leaves the majority of settlement charges open to negotiation between the principals. Understanding which costs are fixed by statute and which are contractual is the difference between a clean closing and a complaint filed with the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC).

Maryland's closing cost framework is governed primarily by the Tax-Property Article of the Annotated Code of Maryland, the Real Property Article, and the Business Occupations and Professions Article Title 17 that regulates licensee conduct. Unlike some states that prescribe rigid allocations, Maryland expects agents to advise clients accurately about custom, statute, and contract terms — and to document the allocation clearly on the Maryland Association of Realtors (MAR) Residential Contract of Sale.

## Statutory Cost Allocations: What the Law Actually Requires

**Maryland imposes specific taxes and fees where the statute names the responsible party, and agents cannot contractually override these obligations to the state — only the economic burden between buyer and seller.** The state transfer tax under Tax-Property Article Section 13-203 is imposed at 0.5% of consideration (0.25% for qualified first-time buyers). County transfer taxes vary; for example, Montgomery County imposes an additional 1% while Baltimore County charges 1.5%.

Recordation tax under Tax-Property Article Section 12-103 applies to the instrument of writing (the deed and the deed of trust). The rate is $7.00 per $500 of consideration statewide, with some counties adding a surcharge. While both taxes are technically imposed on the transaction, the contractual allocation between buyer and seller must be addressed in the purchase agreement.

Maryland's agricultural transfer tax under Tax-Property Article Section 13-301 through 13-307 applies when agricultural land is converted to a non-agricultural use, at a rate of 5%. Agents listing rural properties must flag this cost early because it can represent tens of thousands of dollars and is the seller's obligation when triggering the conversion.

## How the MAR Contract Allocates Costs

The MAR Residential Contract of Sale (Form 101, revised periodically) contains specific paragraphs addressing settlement cost allocation. Paragraph 25 of the current form addresses transfer taxes and recordation taxes, providing checkboxes and blanks for the parties to designate responsibility. Agents who leave these fields blank create ambiguity that can torpedo a closing or generate post-settlement disputes.

| Cost Category | Customary Payer | Statutory Basis | Negotiable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| State Transfer Tax (0.5%) | Split equally | Tax-Property 13-203 | Yes, between parties |
| County Transfer Tax | Split equally | County code varies | Yes |
| Recordation Tax (deed) | Buyer | Tax-Property 12-103 | Yes |
| Recordation Tax (deed of trust) | Buyer | Tax-Property 12-103 | Yes |
| Owner's Title Insurance | Buyer (most counties) | No statute | Fully negotiable |
| Settlement/Attorney Fee | Each party pays own | No statute | Negotiable |
| Agricultural Transfer Tax | Seller | Tax-Property 13-301 | No |

Agents representing buyers should note that lender-required costs — the appraisal, credit report, flood certification, and lender's title insurance — are always buyer obligations because they arise from the buyer's financing relationship. The contract does not need to allocate these, but the agent should ensure the buyer's estimated net sheet accounts for them.

## Seller Concessions and Their Limits

**Seller concessions remain one of the most misunderstood mechanisms in Maryland transactions, particularly when agents confuse contractual concessions with actual cost responsibility.** A seller concession under the MAR contract is a credit toward the buyer's closing costs, not a reassignment of who pays what. Lenders cap seller concessions based on loan type: conventional loans allow 3% to 9% depending on down payment, FHA permits up to 6%, and VA allows up to 4% in concessions.

When a listing agent markets a property with "seller pays closing costs," they must understand that the seller is not literally paying the buyer's lender fees. The seller is providing a credit at settlement that reduces the buyer's cash-to-close. Agents who draft addenda saying "seller shall pay all buyer closing costs" without specifying a dollar cap create enforceability problems if the costs exceed lender-allowed concession limits.

The MAR Addendum of Clauses (Form 103) provides standardized language for seller concessions. Using ad hoc language instead of the approved addendum form exposes agents to claims of unauthorized practice of law under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 10-101, which restricts who may draft legal instruments in Maryland.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

The first recurring error is failing to complete the transfer tax allocation section of the contract. When paragraph 25 of the MAR Form 101 is left blank, settlement attorneys must contact both sides for clarification, which delays closing and signals to MREC examiners that the agent did not properly advise the client. If a dispute arises, the listing agent and buyer's agent share liability for the omission.

The second mistake involves agents verbally promising cost allocations that contradict the written contract. Maryland follows the parol evidence rule strictly — if the signed contract says transfer taxes are split and the agent verbally told the buyer the seller would pay all of them, the contract controls. The buyer may then have a claim against the agent under [MREC's Code of Ethics regulations (COMAR 09.11.01.12)](https://dsd.maryland.gov/Pages/COMARSearch.aspx) for misrepresentation, which can result in license suspension, fines up to $25,000 per violation, or revocation.

Third, agents routinely miscalculate transfer and recordation taxes for jurisdictions with local surcharges. Howard County, for example, imposes a transfer tax of 1% in addition to the state's 0.5%. An agent who estimates closing costs based only on the state rate leaves the client short at the settlement table by thousands of dollars. This error is particularly damaging for buyer's agents whose clients may not have reserves to cover the shortfall.

Fourth, some agents fail to disclose that Maryland is an attorney-settlement state. Real Property Article Section 14-104 requires that settlement be conducted by or under the supervision of an attorney. Agents who direct clients to title companies operating without proper attorney oversight expose themselves to aiding unauthorized practice claims.

Fifth, agents occasionally overlook the first-time homebuyer transfer tax exemption. Under Tax-Property Article Section 13-203(b), first-time Maryland buyers pay 0.25% instead of 0.5% on the state transfer tax. Failing to flag this eligibility costs buyers real money and creates dissatisfaction that turns into commission complaints. Agents should verify eligibility using the buyer's affidavit form required at settlement.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokers have an independent duty under COMAR 09.11.02.03 to supervise all transaction documents and ensure licensees comply with disclosure obligations.** This means brokers cannot rely on agents self-reporting compliance. A systematic file review should check that transfer tax allocations are completed in every contract, that seller concession amounts do not exceed lender limits, and that estimated settlement statements are provided to clients within a reasonable time after contract execution.

Brokers should verify that agents are using current MAR-approved forms rather than outdated versions or self-drafted clauses. The Maryland Real Estate Commission periodically updates form numbers and language, and using a superseded form can void specific protections. Brokers operating [transaction coordination systems](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) should build compliance checkpoints that flag blank fields or missing addenda before the file reaches the settlement attorney.

MREC can hold brokers personally liable under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-322.1 for failure to supervise. Penalties include fines, mandatory additional education, or license suspension for the broker personally — not just the offending agent. ## The Settlement Statement and Final Verification

Maryland settlements use the ALTA Settlement Statement (or the CFPB Closing Disclosure for financed transactions). The settlement attorney prepares these documents, but agents must review them for accuracy before the client signs. Errors in tax proration, transfer tax allocation, or concession credits often go unnoticed until an agent catches them — or until the client discovers the mistake weeks later.

| Document | Who Prepares | Agent's Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| ALTA Settlement Statement | Settlement attorney | Review for accuracy, confirm allocations match contract |
| Closing Disclosure (lender) | Lender | Verify concession amount, confirm buyer received 3 days prior |
| Transfer/Recordation Tax Worksheet | Settlement attorney or title company | Confirm correct county rates applied |
| First-Time Buyer Affidavit | Buyer (with agent guidance) | Ensure eligibility and proper execution |
| MAR Form 101 Cost Allocation | Agents and parties | Complete all fields at contract drafting |

Agents should never sign off on a settlement statement they have not independently verified against the contract terms. If the contract specifies a 50/50 transfer tax split and the settlement statement charges 100% to the buyer, closing that transaction without correction exposes the listing agent to a [fiduciary breach claim](/blog/where-deals-break-down) and potential MREC discipline.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

An agent who misallocates or fails to disclose closing costs faces consequences on multiple fronts. MREC may initiate an investigation under Business Occupations and Professions Article Section 17-320 based on a consumer complaint. Possible outcomes include a letter of reprimand, probation, mandatory continuing education, fines, or license revocation. Civil liability is separate — a buyer who paid costs the contract assigned to the seller can sue both the agent and the brokerage for damages.

Deal cancellation is also a risk. If a buyer discovers at the settlement table that their actual costs exceed the agent's estimate by a material amount, they may refuse to close. Maryland contract law permits rescission where mutual mistake of fact exists, and a material misquotation of closing costs may qualify. The listing agent's seller then faces market time losses, carrying costs, and potential claims against the buyer's agent's brokerage.

Title insurance claims present a third vector. If transfer taxes were misallocated and the error is discovered post-recording, the title company may seek indemnification from the responsible party, pulling agents and brokers into litigation years after the transaction closed.
