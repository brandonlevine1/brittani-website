## Understanding New Hampshire Dual Agency & Designated Agency Rules 2026

The rules governing **New Hampshire dual agency & designated agency rules 2026** remain one of the most misunderstood compliance areas for licensees in the Granite State. Getting agency relationships wrong does not just create awkward conversations at the closing table—it exposes agents and brokers to license discipline, deal rescission, and civil liability. Every transaction starts with a disclosure obligation, and the margin for error is razor-thin.

New Hampshire permits both dual agency and designated agency, but treats them as fundamentally different relationship structures under RSA Chapter 331-A. The distinction matters because the duties owed to clients shift dramatically depending on which path you take. Agents who conflate the two—or fail to document the transition from one to the other—are the ones who end up before the New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC).

## The Statutory Framework: RSA 331-A

New Hampshire's agency law lives in [RSA 331-A](https://www.gencourt.state.nh.us/rsa/html/XXX/331-A/331-A-mrg.htm), specifically sections 25-a through 25-e. RSA 331-A:25-b mandates that every licensee provide a written agency disclosure to prospective buyers and sellers at the earliest opportunity—defined as first substantive contact. This is not a suggestion; it is a statutory duty triggered before you discuss property specifics.

RSA 331-A:25-c defines dual agency as a situation where a single licensee (or a brokerage through its agents) represents both the buyer and seller in the same transaction. RSA 331-A:25-d lays out the requirement for informed written consent from both parties before dual agency can exist. Without both signatures, you are operating outside your authorized scope.

**Designated agency, codified under RSA 331-A:25-e, allows a broker to appoint separate licensees within the same firm to represent the buyer and seller independently.** The supervising broker steps into a neutral facilitative role rather than advocating for either side. This structure preserves each client's right to full fiduciary representation—something dual agency cannot offer.

## Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency: A Side-by-Side Comparison

The practical differences between these two structures affect everything from negotiation strategy to liability exposure. The table below breaks down the key distinctions agents must internalize.

| Factor | Dual Agency | Designated Agency |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| Agent role | Single agent represents both parties | Two separate agents, one per party |
| Broker role | May also be the dual agent | Becomes neutral facilitator |
| Fiduciary duty | Limited; cannot advocate fully for either party | Full fiduciary to assigned client |
| Confidentiality | Cannot share material info favoring one side | Full confidentiality owed to each client |
| Required consent | Written consent from both parties (RSA 331-A:25-d) | Written designation plus disclosure addendum |
| Risk level | High—most common source of complaints | Lower—preserves fiduciary structure |

This comparison explains why many experienced brokerages default to designated agency whenever two of their agents end up on opposite sides of a deal. The fiduciary protections are stronger, the liability exposure is lower, and the documentation trail is cleaner.

## Disclosure Forms and Timing Requirements

New Hampshire does not publish a single universal form number the way some states do, but the NHREC mandates use of a compliant agency disclosure form that meets the content requirements of RSA 331-A:25-b. Most brokerages use the NH Association of REALTORS standard forms, including the Agency Disclosure and Acknowledgment Form (commonly referenced as NHAR Form 2507 in its current revision) and the Designated Agency Addendum.

**Timing is everything.** The initial agency disclosure must be presented at or before the first substantive discussion about a specific property, client motivation, or financial qualification. Waiting until a showing is already underway or until an offer is being drafted violates the statute's "earliest opportunity" standard.

For dual agency specifically, the written consent must be obtained before the dual representation begins—meaning before you start advising both sides. Retroactive consent documented after the fact does not satisfy RSA 331-A:25-d. The NHREC has consistently held that consent must be prospective, informed, and documented contemporaneously.

When transitioning from single agency to dual agency mid-transaction (for example, when your listed property attracts your own buyer client), a new consent form must be executed. The original agency disclosure alone is insufficient; it acknowledged a single-agency relationship that no longer exists. For designated agency, the broker must execute the designation in writing and ensure both designated agents and both clients acknowledge the arrangement.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make

**First, agents routinely confuse disclosure with consent.** Handing someone the agency disclosure form and getting their signature acknowledges they received the document—it does not constitute informed consent to dual agency. Consent requires a separate, explicit agreement in which both parties understand and accept the limitations dual agency imposes on their representation. These are two different legal acts, and treating them as one is the most frequent violation the NHREC sees in disciplinary proceedings.

Second, agents operating under designated agency share confidential client information with their brokerage's other designated agent or supervising broker. The entire premise of designated agency is information segregation. If your seller client tells you their bottom-line price, passing that detail to the buyer's designated agent—even casually in a hallway conversation—destroys the fiduciary wall and converts the arrangement into unauthorized dual agency without consent.

Third, agents fail to re-disclose when circumstances change. A buyer's agent who writes an offer on their own listing must pause and address the agency shift before proceeding. Many agents treat this as an administrative afterthought rather than the fiduciary inflection point it actually is. The timing gap between the relationship change and the documentation of that change is where liability breeds.

Fourth, some agents assume that because their broker has a company-wide designated agency policy, no additional documentation is required on a per-transaction basis. That assumption is wrong. Each transaction involving agents from the same brokerage on both sides requires its own written designation and client acknowledgment. A blanket office policy does not satisfy the per-transaction requirements of RSA 331-A:25-e.

Fifth, agents neglect to explain what dual agency actually costs the client in terms of reduced advocacy. The informed consent standard means the client must understand that a dual agent cannot negotiate aggressively on their behalf, cannot disclose the other party's motivations or financial limits, and cannot recommend a price strategy that favors one side. Simply saying "I'll represent you both fairly" does not meet the statutory bar. This is similar to the [disclosure pitfalls agents face in Wisconsin](/blog/wisconsin-dual-agency-rules-2026), where documentation failures trigger the same type of commission complaints.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear direct supervisory liability under RSA 331-A:26 when their agents violate agency disclosure requirements. A broker who fails to establish and enforce systems for compliance is not merely negligent—they are potentially subject to license suspension or revocation. The NHREC does not distinguish between willful noncompliance and systemic supervisory failure when imposing discipline.

**Every transaction file in your brokerage should contain, at minimum, a signed agency disclosure form dated prior to substantive contact, and if applicable, a separate dual agency consent or designated agency addendum dated prior to dual or designated representation commencing.** Auditing for the presence of these documents is table stakes. Auditing for correct timing—by comparing form dates against showing records, email timestamps, and offer submission dates—is where real compliance lives.

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy specifying whether the firm's default position is dual agency, designated agency, or a prohibition on dual agency entirely. Some New Hampshire brokerages have eliminated dual agency as an option, requiring designated agency any time two in-house agents are involved. That policy choice reduces risk substantially, but only if it is documented, communicated to all licensees, and actually followed.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Red Flag |
|-------------|---------------|----------|
| Initial disclosure | Signed form in file, dated before first showing | Date matches or follows showing/contact log |
| Dual agency consent | Separate signed consent from both parties | Missing entirely, or dated after offer submission |
| Designated agency addendum | Broker designation in writing, both clients acknowledge | No addendum despite two in-house agents on deal |
| Information barriers | No evidence of confidential info sharing between designated agents | Shared email threads, joint meeting notes |
| Policy acknowledgment | Annual signed acknowledgment by all licensees | Missing or outdated signatures |

Quarterly file audits catch problems before they become complaints. ## Liability, Penalties, and Deal Consequences

When an agent fails to comply with New Hampshire's agency disclosure and consent requirements, the consequences cascade. The NHREC can impose fines, require additional education, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely under RSA 331-A:28. Civil courts can rescind the transaction if a party demonstrates they were not properly informed of the agency relationship and were harmed as a result.

Damages in civil actions typically include commission disgorgement, out-of-pocket costs incurred by the harmed party, and in egregious cases, compensatory damages for financial harm caused by conflicted representation. An agent who advised a buyer to waive an inspection while simultaneously representing the seller who knew about a defect is the textbook scenario—and it results in six-figure liability judgments in New Hampshire courts.

Beyond individual liability, E&O carriers may deny coverage if the agent's conduct constituted a knowing violation of statutory duty. Most E&O policies exclude intentional misconduct, and failing to obtain written consent when you know the statute requires it walks dangerously close to that exclusion. ## Protecting Your Practice Under 2026 Rules

The 2026 regulatory environment has not loosened New Hampshire's stance on agency disclosure. If anything, increased consumer awareness and post-NAR-settlement scrutiny of agent compensation structures have made proper agency documentation more important than ever. Buyers who now sign explicit compensation agreements are more attuned to whose interests their agent serves—and less tolerant of ambiguity.

Agents who treat [agency disclosure as a checkbox rather than a client-protection mechanism](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) are the ones generating complaints. Building a practice that defaults to designated agency, documents every relationship transition in writing, and explains the practical implications of each agency type in plain language will keep you on the right side of the NHREC.

The bottom line for anyone operating under **New Hampshire dual agency & designated agency rules 2026**: document early, document completely, and never assume a client understands the limitations of dual agency without a clear, written explanation they have signed. Your license, your commission, and your brokerage's reputation depend on getting this right every single time.
