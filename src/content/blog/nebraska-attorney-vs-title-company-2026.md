## Nebraska Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Statutory Reality

The question of Nebraska attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up agents more often than you would expect. Nebraska is not an attorney-closing state. Unlike neighboring Iowa, which mandates attorney involvement in certain real estate closings, Nebraska allows title companies to handle residential closings without an attorney present at the table.

**This distinction matters because it directly affects how you structure your transactions, advise your clients, and avoid unauthorized practice of law complaints.** The Nebraska Real Estate Commission (NREC) and the Nebraska Supreme Court have both weighed in on the boundaries between what a title company can do, what an attorney must do, and where agents fall in between. Getting it wrong does not just kill deals — it can cost you your license.

The governing framework sits across several statutes and regulatory bodies: Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2401 through 76-2438 (the Nebraska Real Estate License Act), the [Nebraska Title Insurers Act under Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-1900](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=44-1900), and Nebraska Supreme Court opinions on unauthorized practice of law. Understanding how these interact is non-negotiable for any agent closing deals in 2026.

## What Nebraska Law Actually Requires at Closing

Nebraska operates as a "title company state" for residential closings. No statute mandates that a licensed attorney attend, witness, or supervise the closing of a standard residential real estate transaction. Title companies registered under the Title Insurers Act and their authorized agents may conduct closings, prepare standard form documents, and disburse funds.

That said, the Nebraska Supreme Court has drawn firm lines around what constitutes the "practice of law." In State ex rel. Nebraska State Bar Ass'n v. Butterfield, the Court held that preparing legal instruments — including deeds, mortgages, and contracts — constitutes legal practice unless done using standardized forms approved for lay use. Title companies operate within this exception by using pre-approved templates for warranty deeds, quitclaim deeds, and affidavits of title.

**When a transaction involves anything beyond standard residential forms, attorney involvement becomes a practical necessity.** Commercial closings, 1031 exchanges, transactions involving trusts or estates, boundary disputes, and seller-financed deals with custom promissory notes all push beyond what a title company can lawfully prepare without attorney oversight.

## Title Company vs. Attorney: A Side-by-Side Breakdown

Understanding the functional division between title companies and attorneys in Nebraska prevents agents from sending clients to the wrong professional — or worse, trying to fill the gap themselves.

| Function | Title Company | Attorney |
|----------|:------------:|:--------:|
| Conduct title search and issue commitment | Yes | Yes (but rarely does) |
| Prepare standard warranty deed (NE Form) | Yes | Yes |
| Draft custom legal instruments | No | Yes |
| Provide legal advice on contract terms | No | Yes |
| Hold escrow/earnest money | Yes | Yes |
| Issue title insurance policy | Yes | No |
| Resolve title defects requiring legal action | No — must refer | Yes |

This table is not theoretical. The NREC has disciplined agents who blurred these lines by drafting addenda that amounted to legal instruments or by advising clients on title defect resolution without referring them to counsel. The Nebraska Real Estate Commission's official position, reiterated in their 2024 newsletter, is that agents who exceed their statutory authority face discipline under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422(1), which covers "incompetency" and "improper dealings."

## The Forms Nebraska Agents Must Know

Nebraska residential transactions rely heavily on forms published by the Nebraska REALTORS Association (NRA). The primary purchase agreement is the **NRA Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 100)**. Addenda and disclosures carry their own form numbers, and agents must use the correct versions to maintain compliance.

Form 100 contains built-in provisions regarding closing procedures, including designation of the title company or attorney who will conduct closing. Section 14 of the current Form 100 specifies that the parties agree on who will provide title evidence and closing services. Agents must ensure this section is properly completed — leaving it blank creates ambiguity that can delay closings or trigger disputes over who bears closing costs.

Other critical forms include the **Seller Property Condition Disclosure Statement (Form 120)**, the **Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (Form 501)**, and the **Agency Disclosure (Form 600)**. While these are not directly related to the attorney-vs-title-company question, failure to complete them properly can give an attorney grounds to advise their client to delay or rescind, which brings attorney involvement into deals where it was not originally anticipated. Agents who keep their [transaction paperwork tight from day one](/blog/why-agents-hate-paperwork) rarely face these downstream complications.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make Around Closing in Nebraska

**Mistake 1: Drafting custom addenda that constitute legal practice.** When a deal hits a snag — say, a title defect or a seller who wants to retain mineral rights — agents sometimes draft addenda from scratch rather than referring the client to an attorney. The Nebraska Supreme Court's definition of unauthorized practice of law covers this squarely. Using an NRA-approved addendum form is acceptable. Writing original legal language is not.

**Mistake 2: Telling clients they do not need an attorney when the transaction is complex.** Nebraska law does not require attorneys at standard closings, but that does not mean agents should affirmatively discourage attorney involvement. If a client asks whether they need a lawyer, the safe answer is always to recommend they consult one. Agents who discourage attorney involvement and the deal later goes sideways face negligence claims with very little defense.

**Mistake 3: Failing to designate the closing agent in the purchase agreement.** Section 14 of NRA Form 100 exists for a reason. When agents leave this blank or assume "we'll figure it out later," disputes arise over who pays for title insurance, who selects the closing agent, and whether an attorney review period applies. Fill it out at contract execution. Every time.

**Mistake 4: Assuming all title companies offer the same level of service.** Some Nebraska title companies provide full closing services including document preparation and recording. Others issue title commitments and policies but rely on attorneys or third-party closers to conduct the actual settlement. Agents who do not verify their title company's service scope before scheduling closing create last-minute scrambles that erode client confidence.

**Mistake 5: Confusing Nebraska rules with Iowa's.** Agents licensed in both states — common along the Omaha-Council Bluffs corridor — sometimes apply Iowa's attorney-closing requirements to Nebraska deals or vice versa. Iowa requires attorney involvement in real property transfers under Iowa Supreme Court Commission on Unauthorized Practice of Law opinions. Nebraska does not. Mixing up which state's rules apply is a compliance failure that agents along the border make more frequently than anyone admits. The same principle applies to agents who work across multiple state lines and need to [understand why compliance deadlines get missed](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) when juggling different regulatory frameworks.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422 for the acts of their affiliated agents. The NREC can sanction a broker for failing to supervise agents who exceed their statutory authority at or before closing. This is not a hypothetical — the Commission has issued fines and license suspensions where brokers had no audit procedures in place.

| Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| Form 100 Section 14 completion | Closing agent designated in every executed contract | Every transaction |
| Custom addenda review | Any non-NRA-form addendum flagged for attorney review | Every transaction |
| Client attorney referral documentation | Written record when agent recommends counsel | As needed |
| Title company service verification | Confirmation that selected title co. offers full closing services | Quarterly |
| Cross-state compliance (NE/IA border) | Correct state procedures applied per property location | Every transaction |

**Brokers who implement a simple checklist-based audit catch 90 percent of compliance failures before they become Commission complaints.** The cost of a missed audit is steep: under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422, the NREC can impose fines up to $10,000 per violation, suspend or revoke licenses, and require continuing education as a condition of reinstatement. Beyond regulatory penalties, brokers face civil liability if a client suffers damages because an agent performed acts that should have been handled by an attorney or properly licensed title professional.

A [structured weekly review process](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) gives brokers a systematic way to catch these issues before they compound. The goal is not to add bureaucracy — it is to create a paper trail that protects the brokerage when someone inevitably files a complaint.

## When You Should Insist on Attorney Involvement

Even though Nebraska does not require it, there are transactions where failing to involve an attorney borders on malpractice-adjacent negligence for the agent who should have known better. Estate sales where probate has not been fully adjudicated, transactions involving land contracts or contract-for-deed arrangements, deals with unresolved mechanic's liens, and any closing where one party is a foreign national triggering FIRPTA withholding — these all demand attorney oversight.

The practical test is straightforward: if the title commitment reveals exceptions that cannot be resolved through standard curative documents, an attorney needs to be involved. If the transaction structure deviates from a conventional sale between two competent adults with clear title, an attorney needs to be involved. If either party is being asked to sign documents they do not fully understand and the title company cannot explain without crossing into legal advice, an attorney needs to be involved.

Agents who recognize these triggers and make prompt referrals protect themselves, their clients, and their brokerage. Agents who try to push deals through without appropriate professional involvement are the ones who end up in front of the Commission.
