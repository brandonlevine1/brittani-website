## Understanding Vermont Escrow Regulations 2026 and Why They Matter

Vermont escrow regulations 2026 carry real teeth for agents and brokers who handle earnest money deposits, security funds, and other client trust assets. The Vermont Real Estate Commission (VREC), operating under the Office of Professional Regulation within the Secretary of State's office, enforces these rules through audits, complaints, and disciplinary proceedings. If you hold a Vermont real estate license, ignorance of the escrow statutes is not a defense — it is a fast track to fines, license suspension, or worse.

The governing statute is 26 V.S.A. Chapter 41, specifically Sections 2291 through 2299, which outline the fiduciary duties of licensees and the handling of client funds. VREC's administrative rules, particularly Rule 14 (Trust Accounts and Record Keeping), lay out the operational specifics. These are not suggestions; they are binding requirements that the Commission actively enforces during routine and complaint-triggered audits.

Vermont is an attorney-closing state, which means a licensed attorney must oversee the closing process. That fact changes the escrow dynamic compared to states where title companies handle everything. Agents still have direct escrow responsibilities from the moment a buyer submits a deposit through the day funds are disbursed or returned.

## Deposit Timelines and Account Requirements

**The clock starts ticking the moment you receive earnest money from a buyer.** Under VREC Rule 14.3, a licensee must deposit client funds into a designated trust account "promptly," which the Commission has historically interpreted as within three business days unless the purchase and sale agreement specifies otherwise. The Vermont Purchase and Sale Agreement form (VR-1, published by the Vermont Association of Realtors) includes a section where parties can define deposit timing and the identity of the escrow holder.

Trust accounts must be held at a bank or credit union that is either chartered in Vermont or federally insured and operating within the state. Commingling personal or business operating funds with escrow deposits is a direct violation of 26 V.S.A. Section 2296(a)(10). The only exception is a small amount of broker funds — typically not exceeding $500 — kept in the account to cover bank service charges, which must be documented.

Every deposit must be recorded in the broker's escrow ledger with the date received, the date deposited, the source, the amount, the associated property, and the purpose. This is not optional bookkeeping. It is a statutory mandate that VREC auditors check first when reviewing a brokerage.

## Forms and Documentation Agents Must Know

Vermont agents working residential transactions rely on a specific set of forms that intersect with escrow obligations. The VR-1 (Purchase and Sale Agreement) is the primary contract that governs deposit terms, contingencies affecting escrow, and the conditions under which funds are released or returned. Agents must also be familiar with the VR-2 (Lead Paint Disclosure Addendum), as failure to include it can trigger contingency disputes that entangle escrow funds.

The VR-6 (Agency Disclosure Form) does not directly govern escrow, but it establishes the agency relationship that determines which broker is responsible for holding funds. When a listing agent receives a deposit on behalf of a seller client, the disclosure form's designation of agency type becomes relevant if a dispute arises over who had fiduciary control of the funds.

For transactions involving condominiums or properties with homeowner associations, agents should reference the VR-9 (Condominium Resale Disclosure) since unresolved HOA issues can delay escrow disbursement at closing. Understanding how each form connects to the escrow timeline prevents the kind of administrative tangles that lead to [deals falling apart at the worst possible moment](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

| Form | Purpose | Escrow Relevance |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| VR-1 | Purchase and Sale Agreement | Defines deposit amount, holder, timeline, and release conditions |
| VR-2 | Lead Paint Disclosure Addendum | Triggers 10-day contingency that can affect deposit return rights |

| VR-9 | Condominium Resale Disclosure | Affects escrow disbursement timing if HOA issues are unresolved |

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

VREC does not treat escrow violations as minor infractions. Under 26 V.S.A. Section 2296, the Commission can impose civil penalties of up to $1,000 per violation, suspend or revoke a license, require additional education, or place a licensee on probation. Conversion of escrow funds — using client money for any unauthorized purpose — is a criminal offense under Vermont law, potentially prosecuted as embezzlement under 13 V.S.A. Section 2531.

**Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face personal civil liability.** A buyer or seller whose escrow funds are mishandled can sue the agent and the broker individually. Vermont courts have upheld claims for breach of fiduciary duty in cases where agents failed to deposit funds on time, released deposits without proper authorization, or neglected to account for interest earned on trust accounts when required by the contract.

Deal cancellation is another real consequence. If a buyer discovers that their earnest money was not deposited into a trust account as required, they may have grounds to void the contract entirely under general contract law principles. The VREC disciplinary proceeding that follows the complaint often becomes a matter of public record, damaging the agent's reputation long after the fine is paid.

> "A licensee shall not commingle the money or other property of a client with the licensee's own or use the money or other property of one client for the benefit of another." — 26 V.S.A. Section 2296(a)(10)

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Escrow in Vermont

The first and most frequent error is late deposit of earnest money. Agents receive a check at a showing or open house, tuck it into a file folder, and forget about it for a week. VREC interprets "prompt" deposit as three business days. Missing that window puts the agent's license at risk and gives the opposing party a reason to question the transaction's integrity.

The second mistake is failing to get written authorization before releasing escrow funds. Vermont law requires that both parties consent in writing to the release of disputed earnest money. Agents who release funds based on a verbal instruction from one party — even their own client — expose themselves to liability. The VR-1 form includes provisions for this, but agents often skip the release documentation step in the rush to close or cancel.

**Third, many agents neglect to inform their broker when they receive escrow funds.** Under VREC rules, the broker of record is ultimately responsible for all trust account activity. An agent who deposits a check without logging it in the brokerage's system creates a reconciliation gap that will surface during an audit. This is exactly the kind of procedural lapse that [deadlines and checklists are designed to prevent](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Fourth, agents sometimes accept earnest money in cash without issuing a written receipt. Vermont does not prohibit cash deposits, but the burden of proof falls entirely on the agent to document the transaction. Without a signed receipt noting the amount, date, and parties involved, the agent has no defense if a buyer later claims a different amount was tendered.

Fifth, some agents operating in dual agency situations under VR-6 disclosures fail to designate a neutral escrow holder. When the same brokerage represents both sides, holding escrow funds internally creates a conflict of interest that VREC scrutinizes closely. Designating an attorney or independent title company as escrow agent in dual agency transactions is a best practice that avoids this exposure.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in Vermont carry statutory liability for every escrow dollar that touches their brokerage. VREC Rule 14.4 mandates monthly reconciliation of all trust accounts, and the broker of record must personally review or delegate this task to a qualified office manager with documented oversight.

| Audit Area | Frequency | VREC Rule Reference | Common Deficiency |
|------------|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | Rule 14.4 | Skipped months, no documentation |
| Deposit timing verification | Per transaction | Rule 14.3 | Deposits logged but not verified against receipt date |
| Written release authorization | Per disbursement | 26 V.S.A. 2296 | Verbal-only authorization on file |
| Agent receipt documentation | Per transaction | Rule 14.2 | Missing or unsigned receipts |
| Interest accounting | Quarterly or as contracted | Contract-dependent | Interest earned but not reported to parties |

**Brokers should conduct surprise spot audits at least quarterly in addition to the monthly reconciliation.** A VREC complaint can arrive at any time, and the Commission may request records going back seven years. Brokers who discover discrepancies during internal audits have an opportunity to self-correct before a formal investigation begins, which the Commission views favorably in disciplinary proceedings.

Training is another area brokers must own. Vermont's continuing education requirements, administered through the [Office of Professional Regulation](https://sos.vermont.gov/real-estate/), include trust account management topics, but brokers should supplement this with brokerage-specific training. New agents, in particular, often have no practical experience with escrow handling and need hands-on instruction before they are trusted to accept deposits.

Record retention is the final piece. VREC requires that all escrow records, including bank statements, deposit slips, ledgers, receipts, and written release authorizations, be maintained for a minimum of seven years. Brokers who rely on paper-only systems risk losing records to fire, flood, or simple disorganization. Digital backups stored securely are not just smart — they are effectively mandatory for any brokerage handling volume.

## How Vermont Differs From Neighboring States

Vermont's attorney-closing requirement fundamentally shapes the escrow process. In New Hampshire, for example, title companies routinely hold escrow and manage disbursement without attorney involvement. Vermont agents accustomed to working across state lines need to adjust their processes depending on which side of the border the property sits.

The deposit timing norm also varies. While Vermont's three-business-day standard is consistent with many states, [Wisconsin's earnest money rules](/blog/wisconsin-earnest-money-rules-2026) and other jurisdictions have codified specific calendar-day deadlines that differ meaningfully. Agents licensed in multiple states must track these distinctions or risk applying the wrong standard to a Vermont transaction.

Vermont's relatively small market size means VREC operates with closer oversight per licensee than larger-state commissions. Complaints are investigated more quickly, and the Commission's caseload allows for more thorough audits. Agents who assume they can fly under the radar because Vermont is a small state frequently learn otherwise.
