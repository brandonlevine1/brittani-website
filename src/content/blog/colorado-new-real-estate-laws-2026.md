The Colorado Real Estate Commission did not ease into 2026 quietly. Between a sweeping overhaul of buyer representation rules, updated seller property disclosure requirements, and a brand-new commission transparency mandate, the regulatory environment shifted faster than most agents expected. If you hold a Colorado license and haven't studied the **Colorado new real estate laws 2026** package yet, you're already behind.

These changes took effect on July 1, 2026, following a rulemaking process that concluded in December 2025 under the Colorado Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA). They apply to every transaction where a contract is formed on or after that date—regardless of when the listing went live.

## The Buyer Agency Agreement Overhaul Under Rule E-47

**Colorado Rule E-47 now requires a signed buyer agency agreement before any substantive real estate assistance is provided to a prospective buyer.** This mirrors the post-NAR settlement shift seen across the country, but Colorado's version adds teeth that other states skipped.

The approved form is the Exclusive Right to Buy Listing Contract (CBS1-8-25), published by the Colorado Real Estate Commission. Agents may also use the new Non-Exclusive Buyer Agency Agreement (CBS2-8-25) for clients who refuse exclusivity, but both forms must be executed before touring, sending private listing details, or writing offers.

The critical word in the rule is "substantive." DORA's position statement clarifies that answering general market questions or providing publicly available MLS data does not trigger the requirement. The moment you schedule a private showing, pull comps for a specific property, or discuss negotiation strategy, you need ink on paper.

## Commission Transparency: The CD14 Form

Colorado's new Commission Disclosure Form (CD14-7-26) replaces the informal practices most brokerages used to explain compensation. It must be delivered to consumers at "first substantive contact"—the same trigger as the buyer agency agreement—and it must clearly state the amount or percentage the agent expects to earn, who is expected to pay it, and whether that amount is negotiable.

| Requirement | Old Practice (Pre-July 2026) | New CD14 Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of disclosure | At contract signing or later | First substantive contact |
| Format | Verbal or brokerage-created form | Commission-approved CD14-7-26 only |
| Content | General explanation of compensation | Specific dollar amount or percentage, source of payment, negotiability statement |
| Signature required | No | Yes, from both agent and consumer |
| Retention period | Varied by brokerage | 4 years per Rule E-19 |

Agents who previously relied on a quick verbal explanation at the kitchen table are now required to produce this form early—often before a buyer has even committed to working with them. The form itself is two pages and must be printed or delivered electronically with an e-signature that meets Colorado's UETA standards.

## Seller Property Disclosure Updates: SPD19 Replaces SPD18

The Seller's Property Disclosure form received its first major revision since 2021. The new version, [SPD19-7-26](https://dora.colorado.gov/real-estate), adds three disclosure categories that reflect Colorado-specific risks: wildfire mitigation status, radon testing history, and short-term rental restrictions.

**Listing agents must confirm that sellers complete the SPD19 version—not the prior SPD18—for any contract formed after July 1, 2026.** Using the outdated form does not automatically void a transaction, but it creates liability exposure if a buyer later claims they were not informed of a material defect covered by the new categories.

The wildfire section requires sellers to disclose whether the property is within a Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) zone and whether any mitigation work was completed within the last five years. Given that over 40 percent of Colorado's residential inventory sits within or adjacent to WUI zones, this section will affect a significant portion of transactions along the Front Range foothills and mountain communities.

The radon disclosure question is not new in concept—Colorado has long had elevated radon levels statewide—but the SPD19 now requires sellers to attach prior radon test results if they exist, rather than simply checking "known" or "unknown."

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

Noncompliance with these updated rules carries three layers of consequences. Understanding them matters more than memorizing form numbers, because the enforcement mechanisms have changed alongside the rules.

First, DORA's enforcement division can initiate disciplinary action under C.R.S. 12-10-217 for failure to use mandatory forms or failure to disclose material information. Penalties range from a letter of admonishment to fines of up to $2,500 per violation. Repeated infractions or patterns of noncompliance can trigger suspension or revocation proceedings before the Commission.

Second, contract-level consequences exist. Under C.R.S. 38-35.7-102, a buyer who did not receive the required CD14 commission disclosure may have grounds to void the purchase contract within a defined rescission window. This remedy is new for 2026 and creates a scenario where a forgotten form could unwind a deal days before closing.

Third, civil liability expands. If a seller's failure to complete the SPD19 results in a buyer purchasing a property without knowledge of wildfire risk or radon levels, the listing agent who failed to ensure the correct form was used may face negligence claims. The Commission has explicitly stated that agents bear responsibility for ensuring the current version of disclosure forms is used, even if the seller provides an older version.

## Common Mistakes Agents Are Already Making

Based on early enforcement guidance from DORA and feedback from transaction coordinators processing July closings, several patterns have emerged.

**Mistake one: using the CBS1-8-24 form instead of the CBS1-8-25.** The version number matters. The 2024 buyer agency agreement lacks the compensation negotiability clause that Rule E-47 requires. Agents pulling forms from outdated brokerage folders or third-party template libraries are submitting agreements that do not satisfy the rule.

Mistake two: delivering the CD14 at offer submission rather than first substantive contact. Many agents treat the disclosure form as part of the offer package. That timing violates the rule. If you showed the buyer three houses before presenting the CD14, you have already triggered the requirement and missed the deadline by days or weeks.

Mistake three: allowing sellers to skip the new SPD19 wildfire and radon sections by writing "N/A." The form instructions specify that "N/A" is only acceptable where the question is factually inapplicable—such as the condo elevator question on a single-family home. A seller who lives in a WUI zone cannot write "N/A" to the wildfire mitigation question. They must answer it.

Mistake four: failing to retain the signed CD14 in the transaction file. Some agents provide the disclosure verbally or via text summary and never collect a signature. Rule E-19 mandates that the signed CD14 be retained for four years. Without it, an agent cannot prove compliance during an audit.

Mistake five: relying on MLS compensation fields as a substitute for the CD14 disclosure. The MLS cooperative compensation offer (where it still exists) does not satisfy the consumer-facing disclosure requirement. These are separate obligations. One informs cooperating brokers; the other informs your client.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Employing brokers carry supervisory liability under C.R.S. 12-10-224, and DORA has signaled that broker-level audits will be a focus of 2026-2027 enforcement sweeps. The statute places the burden on brokers to maintain systems that catch compliance failures before they become Commission complaints.

| Audit Item | Form/Document | Verification Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer agency signed before touring | CBS1-8-25 or CBS2-8-25 | Execution date must precede first showing date in MLS or calendar records |
| Commission disclosure timing | CD14-7-26 | Delivery date must match or precede first substantive contact log entry |
| Seller disclosure version | SPD19-7-26 | Form version number visible on page 1; no SPD18 forms accepted for July+ contracts |
| Radon attachment | SPD19 Addendum A | If seller indicates prior testing, lab results must be attached |
| File retention | All forms | Minimum 4-year retention from closing or termination date |

Brokers should update their transaction management checklists immediately. If your brokerage still uses a paper or spreadsheet-based file review system, consider whether that approach can realistically catch version-number errors on forms. This is exactly the kind of deadline and document management problem that [agents miss when relying on memory alone](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

The supervision standard is not perfection—DORA acknowledges that brokers cannot review every document in real time. But the standard is "reasonable systems and procedures." A broker who has no audit process at all, or who has not updated their checklist since 2024, will struggle to argue reasonable compliance during an investigation.

## How These Changes Interact With Federal Requirements

Colorado's new rules do not exist in isolation. Agents still must comply with RESPA, TILA, fair housing statutes, and—where applicable—FIRPTA withholding obligations on foreign sellers. The new CD14 disclosure does not replace the Closing Disclosure or Loan Estimate requirements under federal law. It is an additional, state-level obligation that runs on a different timeline.

For agents working with out-of-state buyers or handling transactions involving foreign nationals, the layering of federal and state disclosure timelines can create a paperwork bottleneck in the first week of a transaction. The [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) that most brokerages adopted over the past two years add another layer to the compliance stack.

Managing these overlapping obligations manually is where deals fall apart. A single missed form or incorrect version can cascade into a rescission notice or a complaint filed with DORA months after closing.
