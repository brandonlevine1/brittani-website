## Understanding Nevada Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

**If you hold a Nevada real estate license, the rules governing earnest money and escrow aren't suggestions—they carry statutory teeth.** The Nevada earnest money & escrow laws 2026 framework sits primarily within NRS Chapter 645 (Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons) and NRS Chapter 645A (Escrow Agencies), both enforced by the Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED) under the Department of Business and Industry. Mishandling even one deposit can trigger license discipline, civil claims, and deal collapse.

This post breaks down the statutes agents must follow, the forms that document compliance, the penalties for violations, and the operational gaps brokers need to close before an audit catches them first.

## Statutory Framework: NRS 645 and NRS 645A

Nevada treats earnest money as trust funds the moment they leave a buyer's hands. NRS 645.310 establishes that any money received by a licensee on behalf of a principal must be placed in a trust account or delivered to the escrow holder without unreasonable delay. The statute doesn't define "unreasonable" in hours, which is why the contract language you draft matters enormously.

NRS 645A governs licensed escrow agents and agencies separately from brokerages. If a title company or independent escrow company holds the deposit, that entity answers to NRS 645A.171 through 645A.178, which dictate how funds are received, held, disbursed, and reported. Agents who assume "it's the escrow company's problem" forget that NRED can still discipline a licensee who fails to ensure timely delivery.

The [Nevada Real Estate Division](https://red.nv.gov) publishes periodic advisories clarifying enforcement priorities. In 2025, NRED increased its focus on trust account audits following several high-profile commingling cases in Clark County. That heightened scrutiny carries into 2026.

## Key Forms and Contract Provisions Agents Must Know

The Nevada Association of Realtors (NVAR) and the Greater Las Vegas Association of Realtors (GLVAR) maintain the standard purchase agreement forms most agents use. The GLVAR Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 500/510) includes specific blanks for earnest money amount, deposit holder identity, and the deadline for deposit delivery. The Earnest Money Receipt section on Form 500 is where compliance begins—or where it breaks down.

| Form / Document | Purpose | Who Completes It |
|---|---|---|
| GLVAR Form 500 (Purchase Agreement) | Sets deposit amount, holder, and delivery deadline | Buyer's agent |
| GLVAR Form 515 (Counter Proposal) | Modifies earnest money terms during negotiation | Either agent |
| NVAR Trust Account Ledger (NAC 645.695) | Records receipt, holding, and disbursement | Broker or trust account holder |
| Mutual Release of Earnest Money Deposit | Authorizes disbursement upon cancellation | Both parties' signatures |
| NRED Audit Worksheet | Used during Division audits of trust accounts | Broker/compliance officer |

**Form 500's Section 4 is where most compliance failures originate.** If an agent leaves the deposit deadline blank or writes "ASAP," NRED treats the timeline as ambiguous—and ambiguity in a dispute almost always harms the licensee. Best practice: specify a calendar-day deadline (e.g., "within 3 business days of acceptance") and name the exact entity holding funds.

Agents working outside GLVAR's territory—say, in Washoe or rural counties—may encounter NVAR-specific forms with slightly different numbering. Regardless of form version, the statutory requirements under NRS 645 and NAC 645 remain identical statewide.

## Deposit Timelines and Delivery Requirements

Nevada does not impose a single statutory deadline for depositing earnest money. Instead, the deadline is contractual. The purchase agreement governs, and the agent's obligation is to ensure the buyer performs within that contractual window. If the contract says five business days and the deposit arrives on day six, the seller may have grounds to declare breach—and the listing agent has a duty to inform their client of the buyer's noncompliance.

Once funds reach the escrow holder or broker trust account, different rules apply depending on who holds them. A broker who accepts earnest money directly must deposit it into a trust account by the next banking day per NAC 645.695. This is stricter than the contractual deadline between parties. The broker's personal obligation to the trust account is immediate and nondiscretionary.

## Penalties for Noncompliance: What NRED Can Do to You

**NRED's disciplinary authority under NRS 645.633 includes license suspension, revocation, and fines up to $10,000 per violation.** The Division does not need a consumer complaint to initiate an investigation; routine trust account audits surface violations independently. Here's how the penalty structure breaks down in practice:

| Violation | Statutory Basis | Potential Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Commingling trust funds with personal/business funds | NRS 645.633(1)(e) | License revocation, $10,000 fine |
| Failure to deposit within required timeframe | NAC 645.695 | License suspension, corrective action |
| Disbursing without mutual written authorization | NRS 645A.171 | Civil liability, license discipline |
| Failure to maintain trust account records | NAC 645.695, NRS 645.310 | Fine, mandatory audit compliance |
| Conversion of trust funds (theft) | NRS 645.633, NRS 205.300 | Criminal prosecution, permanent revocation |

Beyond NRED actions, agents face civil exposure. A buyer whose earnest money is wrongfully disbursed can sue the agent, the brokerage, and the escrow holder. Errors and omissions insurance may cover some claims, but intentional violations or gross negligence typically fall outside coverage.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate practical consequence. When an earnest money dispute arises mid-transaction, lenders freeze, title work stalls, and closing dates collapse. Even if the agent avoids NRED discipline, a blown deal costs referral relationships and future business.

## Common Mistakes Nevada Agents Make With Earnest Money

Five recurring errors show up in NRED disciplinary actions and escrow disputes. Each one is preventable with basic process discipline.

**First, agents fail to specify who holds the deposit in the purchase agreement.** Leaving the holder field blank creates confusion about where funds should go, delays delivery, and gives both parties ammunition in a dispute. The form requires a named entity—fill it in every time.

Second, agents accept earnest money checks made payable to themselves or their team name rather than the brokerage trust account or escrow company. Under NRS 645.310, the licensee is personally liable for trust funds in their possession. A check payable to "Jane Smith Real Estate Team" instead of "XYZ Brokerage Trust Account" is a red flag NRED auditors specifically look for.

Third, agents verbally agree to hold a check uncashed "until inspections pass." This practice violates NAC 645.695's deposit-by-next-banking-day rule if the broker receives the check. Verbal side agreements about check-holding also create liability because they contradict the written contract terms. If the buyer wants a delayed deposit, it must be documented in the agreement itself.

Fourth, agents release earnest money based on one party's verbal instruction during a cancellation. NRS 645A.171 requires mutual written instructions. A listing agent who tells escrow to "go ahead and release it back to the buyer" without the seller's written authorization exposes themselves and the escrow holder to liability.

Fifth, agents confuse the contract cancellation deadline with the earnest money release deadline. A buyer who cancels within the contingency period has a right to cancel the contract—but the mechanics of actually getting the money back still require mutual release paperwork. These are two separate steps, and conflating them causes weeks of post-cancellation disputes. Our article on [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) covers this gap in detail.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers bear ultimate responsibility for trust account compliance under NRS 645.310 and NAC 645.695. NRED does not accept "my agent handled it" as a defense. If you supervise licensees in Nevada, here's what your internal audit process must cover.

**Monthly trust account reconciliations are not optional—they're required by NAC 645.695.** The reconciliation must match the bank statement balance to the ledger balance, and any discrepancy must be resolved and documented before the next month closes. NRED auditors request 12 months of reconciliations during routine reviews.

Brokers must verify that every open transaction with an earnest money deposit has a corresponding ledger entry showing receipt date, amount, source, and holder. A missing entry—even for a deposit held by a third-party escrow company—creates an audit finding if the broker received any funds in transit.

Agent training records matter during NRED investigations. Brokers who can demonstrate annual training on trust account procedures, form completion, and deposit timelines receive more lenient treatment in borderline disciplinary cases. Document the training, keep sign-in sheets, and archive the materials.

File review cadence should match transaction volume. A brokerage closing 50 transactions per month in Las Vegas needs weekly spot-checks, not quarterly reviews. Assign a compliance officer or transaction coordinator to verify Form 500 completion, deposit receipts, and mutual release forms on every file before it reaches closing.
