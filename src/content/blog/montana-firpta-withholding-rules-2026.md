## Understanding Montana FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act still trips up experienced agents every year, and Montana is no exception. If you list or facilitate a sale involving a foreign national selling real property in the state, the Montana FIRPTA withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 apply to your transaction—and your potential liability. Mishandling the process can freeze closings, expose buyers to IRS collection actions, and put your license at risk with the Montana Board of Realty Regulation.

FIRPTA itself is a federal statute codified at 26 U.S.C. Sections 897 and 1445. It requires the buyer (called the "transferee") to withhold a percentage of the gross sales price when acquiring a U.S. real property interest from a foreign person. The default withholding rate remains 15% for properties sold above $1,000,000, with a 10% rate applying to sales between $300,001 and $1,000,000, and a full exemption for buyer-occupied residences at or below $300,000.

Montana does not impose a separate state-level withholding tax on foreign sellers the way states like California, Hawaii, or Maryland do. That said, the Montana Department of Revenue does require nonresident sellers—including foreign persons—to address state income tax obligations at closing under Montana Code Annotated Section 15-30-2536. This dual compliance layer is where agents most commonly drop the ball.

## Federal Withholding Rates and Montana's Role

**The IRS determines the withholding percentage based on the sales price and the buyer's intended use of the property.** The table below clarifies the thresholds agents encounter most frequently in Montana transactions.

| Sales Price Range | Buyer Will Use as Residence | Withholding Rate | Applicable Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0 – $300,000 | Yes | 0% (exempt) | Affidavit of buyer intent |
| $300,001 – $1,000,000 | Yes | 10% | IRS Form 8288 / 8288-A |
| Over $1,000,000 | Yes or No | 15% | IRS Form 8288 / 8288-A |
| Any amount | No (investment property) | 15% | IRS Form 8288 / 8288-A |

Montana's involvement comes through the nonresident withholding requirement. Under MCA 15-30-2536, closing agents must withhold Montana state income tax from nonresident sellers at the highest applicable marginal rate—currently 5.9% after the 2024 rate restructuring—unless the seller provides a completed Montana Form NRW-Exemption or agrees to file a Montana income tax return. Foreign sellers rarely qualify for the exemption, meaning your closing will carry both a 15% federal hold and a 5.9% state hold on most investment property sales.

## Key Forms and Filing Deadlines

Agents need to know which forms are filed and who bears responsibility for each. The buyer is the legally responsible party for FIRPTA withholding, but the "transferee's agent"—defined broadly by the IRS to include listing agents, buyer's agents, and closing officers—carries secondary liability under [IRC Section 1445(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1445).

IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) must be filed within 20 days after the transfer date. Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) accompanies it and serves as the seller's receipt. If the seller seeks a reduced withholding amount, Form 8288-B (Application for Withholding Certificate for Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests) should be submitted well before closing—the IRS generally takes 90 days or more to process these applications.

On the Montana side, closing agents file Montana Form MW-4 to remit state withholding. The payment is due within 30 days of closing. Agents should confirm that the title company or attorney handling the closing has established an escrow protocol for both the federal and state withholdings simultaneously.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

**If the proper withholding is not collected and remitted, the IRS can pursue the full tax amount from the buyer first, and then from the agents involved.** Under IRC 1445(d), a transferee's agent who has "actual knowledge" that a seller is a foreign person—and fails to notify the transferee or ensure withholding—becomes personally liable for the tax, limited to the amount of compensation the agent received in the transaction.

In practical terms, that means your commission check. If you earned $18,000 on a listing and the withholding obligation was $75,000, the IRS can pursue you for up to $18,000. The buyer remains on the hook for the remaining balance plus interest and penalties.

Montana adds its own enforcement layer. The Department of Revenue can assess penalties against closing agents who fail to withhold under MCA 15-30-2536, and the Montana Board of Realty Regulation (operating under the Montana Department of Labor and Industry) can initiate disciplinary proceedings for violations that demonstrate incompetence or dishonesty under Montana Administrative Rules 24.210.641. Sanctions range from fines to license suspension.

Deal cancellation is also a real consequence. When a title company discovers mid-closing that FIRPTA was never addressed and the parties cannot agree on an escrow structure, transactions collapse. Sellers lose buyers. Agents lose closings. Everyone loses time that proper due diligence at listing intake would have saved.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make

Three to five errors recur in FIRPTA-related transactions across Montana brokerages. Knowing them in advance gives you a structural advantage.

**First, agents assume that a seller with a U.S. mailing address cannot be a foreign person.** FIRPTA defines "foreign person" by tax status, not by where someone receives mail. A Canadian national who owns a vacation home in Whitefish and gets mail there is still a foreign person unless they hold a green card or meet the substantial presence test.

Second, agents rely on the seller's verbal representation without collecting the proper documentation. The IRS accepts a Non-Foreign Affidavit (sometimes called a FIRPTA Affidavit or Certification of Non-Foreign Status) under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-2(b). If the seller provides this affidavit with a valid U.S. taxpayer identification number and signs under penalty of perjury, the buyer is relieved of withholding obligations. Without that affidavit in the file, the buyer and agents carry exposure regardless of what the seller claimed verbally.

Third, agents confuse the federal FIRPTA withholding with Montana's nonresident withholding and assume one satisfies the other. They are entirely separate obligations to entirely separate taxing authorities. A transaction can comply with FIRPTA but still violate MCA 15-30-2536 if the state withholding or exemption form is missing.

Fourth, listing agents fail to flag the issue early. The best practice is to determine foreign status during the listing intake. Waiting until the title company runs into the issue at closing compresses timelines dangerously—especially when a Form 8288-B withholding certificate application needs 90-plus days of lead time.

Fifth, agents working [deals where deadlines are already tight](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) sometimes pressure title companies to close without resolving withholding, creating downstream liability for everyone in the chain.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry supervisory obligations under Montana Administrative Rules 24.210.625 and 24.210.641. If an agent under your supervision fails to address FIRPTA and the transaction results in IRS collection action or a Department of Revenue penalty, your brokerage can face regulatory scrutiny.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Listing intake forms | Does the form ask about seller citizenship/residency status? | Every new listing |
| Non-Foreign Affidavit in file | Signed, dated, includes TIN | Before closing clears |
| Montana Form NRW-Exemption or MW-4 | Filed or documented as not applicable | Within 30 days post-close |
| Form 8288/8288-A confirmation | Copy in transaction file or title company confirmation | Within 20 days post-close |
| Agent training documentation | Annual FIRPTA training or CE completion | Annually |

Brokers should build FIRPTA and Montana nonresident withholding checkpoints directly into their transaction management workflows. If your brokerage still relies on agents remembering steps from memory, you already know how that ends—the same way it ends with [every other process that depends on recall instead of systems](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

Quarterly file audits should flag any transaction where the seller address is outside the U.S. or where the seller entity is organized in a foreign jurisdiction. Montana LLCs owned by foreign nationals still trigger FIRPTA because the look-through rules apply—the entity's tax classification determines the obligation, not the state of organization.

## Reduced Withholding and Exemption Strategies

Foreign sellers in Montana are not without options. The most common path to reducing the 15% federal withholding is filing Form 8288-B to request a withholding certificate. The seller must demonstrate that the tax liability on the gain will be less than 15% of the gross sales price—which is often the case when the property has not appreciated significantly or when the seller has deductible expenses.

The challenge is timing. Because the IRS processing window extends past 90 days, sellers should file 8288-B at the same time they list the property, not after they receive an offer. Agents who understand this timeline can advise foreign sellers properly during the listing consultation. The alternative is escrowing the full 15% at closing and waiting for the IRS to issue a refund after the seller files a U.S. tax return—a process that can take 6-12 months.

For Montana's state-level withholding, the seller can avoid withholding by agreeing in writing (using Form NRW-Exemption) to file a Montana individual income tax return and pay any tax due. This is often the simpler path for foreign sellers who already engage a U.S.-based CPA.
