## South Carolina Building Code Updates 2026: The Shift Agents Must Understand

The South Carolina Building Codes Council formally adopted the 2021 International Code Council (ICC) family of codes, and the South Carolina building code updates 2026 represent the most significant overhaul since the state moved to the 2015 editions. These changes took effect January 1, 2026, touching everything from wind-resistance standards in coastal counties to energy efficiency mandates statewide. If you hold a license issued by the South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC), your daily practice just changed in ways that go far beyond new construction.

Real estate agents are not building inspectors. But the disclosure obligations attached to your license do not care about that distinction when a buyer discovers post-closing that a property fails to meet code standards the seller — or their agent — should have flagged. The intersection of building codes and real estate practice is where lawsuits live, and the 2026 updates have widened that intersection considerably.

## What Actually Changed in the 2026 Code Adoption

**The South Carolina Building Codes Council, operating under the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation (LLR), adopted the 2021 IRC, 2021 IBC, 2021 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), and 2021 International Mechanical Code (IMC) with South Carolina-specific amendments.** The previous baseline was the 2015 editions with 2018 amendments. That means the state skipped an entire code cycle.

The most impactful changes for residential transactions fall into three categories: wind design, energy envelope requirements, and electrical service upgrades. Coastal counties — including Charleston, Beaufort, Horry, and Georgetown — now face enhanced wind-speed maps that reclassify certain areas into higher design categories. Homes built or substantially renovated after January 1, 2026, must meet these new thresholds.

Energy requirements under the 2021 IECC mandate higher insulation R-values, improved window U-factors, and blower-door testing for all new residential construction. The electrical code updates require arc-fault circuit interrupter (AFCI) protection in additional rooms and updated service panel standards that affect renovation permits.

| Code Area | Previous Standard (2015/2018) | New Standard (2021 with SC Amendments) |
|---|---|---|
| Wind Speed Maps (Coastal) | 140 mph ultimate design | 150-160 mph ultimate design (zone-dependent) |
| Ceiling Insulation | R-38 minimum | R-49 minimum |
| Window U-Factor | 0.35 | 0.30 |
| AFCI Protection | Bedrooms, family rooms | All habitable rooms plus laundry |
| Blower Door Test | Not required | Mandatory for new construction |

## How This Affects Existing Property Transactions

Existing homes are not automatically out of compliance — building codes are generally not retroactive. However, this is where agents get into trouble. Once a property undergoes renovation work that triggers a permit, the new code standards apply to that scope of work. Agents listing properties with recent unpermitted renovations are sitting on a disclosure time bomb.

**Under S.C. Code Section 27-50-40, the South Carolina Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act requires sellers to complete the SC Disclosure of Real Property Condition form (commonly referenced as Form 270).** This form asks about additions, structural changes, and whether permits were obtained. When an agent knows or should reasonably know that renovation work was done without permits — and thus potentially not to code — silence is not an option.

The SCREC has consistently held that agents have an affirmative duty not to misrepresent material facts. S.C. Code 40-57-145 outlines the grounds for disciplinary action, which include "failing to disclose a material fact of which the licensee has knowledge." The 2026 code updates raise the stakes because [understanding where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often comes back to inspection findings that reveal code gaps no one mentioned.

## The Forms That Matter and When to Use Them

South Carolina agents work primarily with forms published by the South Carolina Association of Realtors (SCR). The relevant forms for code-related disclosures and contingencies include:

Form 270 — Disclosure of Real Property Condition. This is the seller's disclosure document. Agents should review it carefully for answers related to structural modifications, HVAC changes, and permitting history. Any blank or "unknown" answer on items related to additions or major systems should prompt a conversation with the seller about permit records.

Form 310 — Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate. The standard purchase contract contains inspection contingency provisions that allow buyers to request repairs or terminate based on property condition findings. Agents representing buyers should understand that code-related deficiencies discovered during inspection fall within this contingency window.

Form 391 — Repair Request and Response. When inspections reveal code issues — particularly those tied to unpermitted work that now conflicts with 2026 standards — this form becomes the mechanism for negotiation. The distinction between "code at time of construction" and "current code" matters enormously in repair negotiations and agents must articulate which standard applies.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Issues

**The first and most frequent error is assuming that building codes are entirely the inspector's problem.** Agents who take this position misunderstand their disclosure duties under South Carolina law. Here are the specific mistakes that generate complaints to SCREC and civil liability:

Mistake one: Failing to ask sellers about permit history for visible improvements. When an agent walks a listing with an obvious addition, finished basement, or converted garage and never asks whether permits were pulled, they have arguably failed their duty of reasonable inquiry. The SCREC investigative process frequently flags this as willful ignorance.

Mistake two: Advising buyers that code issues found during inspection are "grandfathered in" without understanding the trigger points. The 2026 updates apply to any permitted work going forward. If a seller completed renovations in 2025 under a permit issued before January 1, 2026, those renovations must meet the code in effect at permit issuance. But unpermitted work discovered in 2026 creates an enforcement gray zone where local building officials may require compliance with current standards.

Mistake three: Using the phrase "as-is" as a shield against all code disclosure obligations. South Carolina courts have repeatedly held that as-is language does not eliminate an agent's duty to disclose known material defects. The SC Supreme Court's ruling in Lawson v. Citizens & Southern National Bank established that even as-is sales carry disclosure responsibilities.

Mistake four: Failing to distinguish between local amendments and state-minimum code. South Carolina allows municipalities to adopt stricter standards than the state baseline. Charleston, Columbia, and Greenville each maintain local amendments. An agent operating across jurisdictions who assumes uniform standards across the state is making a dangerous bet.

Mistake five: Not updating CMA comparables analysis to account for code-compliant versus non-compliant properties. As 2026 standards raise construction costs and insurance companies begin distinguishing between code-compliant and non-compliant homes for rating purposes, agents who ignore this factor in pricing recommendations are providing incomplete market guidance.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry supervisory liability under S.C. Code 40-57-137. When an agent under your supervision fails to properly disclose code-related issues, the complaint lands on your desk too. Here is what your audit process should include in light of the 2026 updates.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Form 270 Completion Review | Every listing | Blank answers on permits, additions, structural changes |
| Inspection Contingency Timelines | Every buyer transaction | Missed deadlines that waive code-issue discovery rights |
| Repair Request Documentation | Post-inspection | Proper code standard cited (year of construction vs. current) |
| Agent CE Records | Quarterly | Completion of code-related continuing education |
| Local Amendment Awareness | By jurisdiction | Agents working in municipalities with stricter local codes |

**Brokers should implement a policy requiring agents to document permit verification conversations with sellers at listing intake.** A simple email confirming what the seller reported about permits and renovations creates a paper trail that protects both agent and brokerage. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission's [disciplinary action database](https://llr.sc.gov/rec/) shows a pattern of escalating enforcement around disclosure failures — a trend that the 2026 code updates will accelerate.

Your brokerage's risk management program should include at least one training session specifically addressing how the 2026 code adoption intersects with Form 270 disclosures and inspection contingencies. Document attendance. If a complaint arises, showing that you provided training and supervision matters enormously to the Commission's disposition.

## Insurance Implications Agents Should Communicate

The 2026 code updates carry direct insurance consequences that affect transaction pricing and buyer decisions. South Carolina's coastal property insurance market already operates under pressure from hurricane risk. The new wind-speed classifications and construction standards mean that homes built to 2026 code may qualify for meaningful premium reductions, while older homes that have not been retrofitted face potential rate increases.

Agents should not provide insurance advice — that is outside your scope. But you should know enough to direct buyers toward their insurance agent early in the process, particularly for coastal properties. The difference between a home with hurricane straps, impact-resistant glazing, and a sealed roof deck versus one without these features can amount to thousands of dollars annually in premium savings.

This information belongs in your buyer consultation conversations, not as advice but as a factor in their cost analysis. The [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) process is where you catch these conversations before they fall through the cracks in an active transaction.

Code 40-57-110 include a mandatory four-hour core course every two years — look for 2026 offerings that address code updates specifically.
