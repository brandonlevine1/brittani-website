## Why Oregon HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The Oregon hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026 landscape has become a minefield for agents who treat HOA paperwork as an afterthought. Between ORS Chapter 100 (the Oregon Condominium Act) and ORS Chapter 94 (the Oregon Planned Community Act), the state imposes strict obligations on sellers—and by extension, on the licensees who represent them. Miss a form, blow a deadline, or deliver an incomplete packet, and your buyer's right to rescind survives all the way to the closing table.

Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) disciplinary records show that HOA disclosure violations rank among the top five complaint categories filed against licensees each biennium. The consequences range from transaction rescission to license suspension. This post breaks down exactly what you owe your clients, your brokerage, and the state.

## The Statutory Framework: ORS 100 vs. ORS 94

Oregon splits its community association disclosure obligations into two parallel statutory schemes. If you confuse them—or apply the wrong one—you are exposed.

**ORS Chapter 100 governs condominiums exclusively.** It requires the seller to furnish a "resale certificate" containing financial data, association rules, pending litigation disclosures, and reserve study summaries. The statute sets a five-day buyer rescission window upon receipt.

ORS Chapter 94 governs planned communities, which includes most single-family HOA neighborhoods, townhome associations, and mixed-use developments that are not recorded as condominiums. Under ORS 94.580, sellers must deliver a "resale disclosure" packet that mirrors much of the condo resale certificate but follows its own procedural timeline. Agents who assume one statute covers both property types make costly errors.

| Requirement | Condos (ORS 100) | Planned Communities (ORS 94) |
|---|---|---|
| Governing statute | ORS 100.210 | ORS 94.580 |
| Document name | Resale certificate | Resale disclosure packet |
| Buyer rescission period | 5 calendar days | 5 calendar days |
| Reserve study required | Yes, if available | Yes, if available |
| Pending litigation disclosure | Mandatory | Mandatory |
| Who prepares it | HOA or management company | HOA or management company |

Both statutes allow the association to charge a fee for preparing these documents. That fee is negotiable between buyer and seller in the purchase agreement.

## What Goes Inside the Resale Certificate and Disclosure Packet

The resale certificate under ORS 100.210 must contain specific items enumerated in the statute. Missing even one element gives the buyer grounds to argue that delivery was incomplete—and the rescission clock never started.

Required contents include: monthly assessment amounts, any special assessments that have been levied or are anticipated, the association's current operating budget, the most recent reserve study, any pending lawsuits involving the association, a statement of whether the unit is in compliance with the governing documents, and the association's insurance summary. If the association has adopted rules restricting rentals, those rules must be disclosed in writing.

**For planned communities under ORS 94.580, the disclosure packet must include substantially the same financial and governance information.** The key difference is that ORS 94 explicitly requires disclosure of any approved but unrecorded amendments to the declaration. Agents representing buyers should verify that the packet reflects the most recent board actions, not just the recorded CC&Rs available through the title company.

The Oregon Association of Realtors (OAR) publishes standard forms that reference these requirements. Form 2112 (Condominium/Planned Community Addendum) is the transactional document most commonly used to memorialize the parties' agreement about who orders and pays for these disclosures. You should confirm you are using the [2026-updated version from OREA's forms page](https://www.oregon.gov/rea).

## Rescission Rights and Timeline Enforcement

Once the buyer receives a compliant resale certificate or disclosure packet, the five-day rescission window begins. If the buyer exercises that right within the statutory period, the purchase agreement is void and the earnest money must be returned.

Here is where agents get into trouble: if the packet is incomplete or never delivered, the buyer's rescission right does not expire. It persists until closing—or in some contested cases, beyond it. Courts have held that delivery of a deficient resale certificate does not trigger the rescission period.

**The practical implication is stark.** A buyer who discovers undisclosed special assessments three days before closing can walk away without penalty if the seller never delivered a compliant packet. Your deal dies, your commission evaporates, and the seller may have a malpractice claim against you if you failed to advise them about their statutory obligation.

| Timeline Event | Action Required | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Listing stage | Advise seller to order resale certificate/packet | Delayed delivery, extended rescission risk |
| Mutual acceptance | Confirm disclosure delivery method in addendum (Form 2112) | Ambiguity about compliance |
| Within 10 days of acceptance (best practice) | Deliver packet to buyer or buyer's agent | Buyer's rescission window never starts |
| 5 days after buyer receipt | Rescission window closes | If deficient, window stays open indefinitely |
| Before closing | Verify buyer signed acknowledgment of receipt | Closing could be challenged post-hoc |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Oregon HOA Disclosures

Three to five errors appear repeatedly in OREA complaints and E&O claims. Avoiding them is not optional; it is baseline competence.

**First, agents confuse the seller's property condition disclosure (ORS 105.465) with the HOA resale certificate.** They are separate obligations. Delivering one does not satisfy the other. The seller disclosure form addresses the physical condition of the unit; the resale certificate addresses the association's financial and legal health. Both must be delivered independently.

Second, listing agents assume the title company will handle HOA document ordering. Title companies pull recorded CC&Rs and plat maps, but they do not order the resale certificate from the management company. That responsibility falls on the seller—and the listing agent should be the one making sure it happens within the first week of the transaction.

Third, buyer's agents fail to document the date of receipt. Without a signed acknowledgment tied to a specific calendar date, proving when the five-day rescission window began becomes a "he said, she said" dispute. Use a date-stamped email delivery confirmation or a signed receipt on your brokerage's letterhead.

Fourth, agents rely on outdated packets. A resale certificate that is more than 30 days old may not reflect a recently approved special assessment or a newly filed lawsuit. ORS 100.210(5) states that the certificate must be current as of a date no earlier than 45 days before the date of delivery to the buyer. If your packet is stale, you are handing the buyer a defective document.

Fifth, dual agents fail to advise both parties of their respective rights and obligations around the disclosure packet. Oregon's [agency disclosure rules](/blog/wisconsin-agency-disclosure-requirements-2026) differ from state to state, but in Oregon, a disclosed limited agent still owes both clients the duty to disclose material facts—including that the rescission clock has not started.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry supervisory liability under ORS 696.805 for the acts of their affiliated licensees. When it comes to HOA disclosures, that means your brokerage's transaction audit process must flag specific checkpoints.

Every condo or planned community file should contain: (1) a copy of the resale certificate or disclosure packet, (2) proof of delivery to the buyer with a date stamp, (3) a signed buyer acknowledgment, and (4) evidence that the five-day rescission window expired without exercise. If any of these four elements is missing, the file is deficient.

**Brokers should also audit the version of Form 2112 in use.** OAR updates forms annually, and using a prior-year version may omit new statutory language or revised timelines. A quarterly form audit—checking that agents are pulling current templates from the OAR library—prevents compliance drift before it becomes a complaint.

Transaction coordinators play a critical role here, and brokerages that have adopted [structured TC workflows](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) report fewer audit failures. The key is building HOA disclosure verification into the compliance checklist at the offer-acceptance stage, not as an afterthought before closing.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When You Get It Wrong

An agent who fails to ensure proper HOA disclosure delivery faces three categories of risk: transaction rescission, civil liability, and regulatory discipline.

Transaction rescission is the most immediate consequence. The buyer walks, and in most cases, they are entitled to a full refund of earnest money under ORS 100.210(7) or ORS 94.580(4). The seller loses a buyer and may have to relist—often at a lower price if market conditions have shifted.

Civil liability arises when the buyer closes without receiving a proper disclosure and later discovers material association problems. Under Oregon common law, the buyer can sue the seller for damages. If the listing agent knew or should have known about the disclosure obligation and failed to act, the agent may be joined as a defendant. E&O carriers report that HOA disclosure claims average between $15,000 and $40,000 in settlement costs in Oregon.

OREA can impose regulatory sanctions ranging from a letter of reprimand to license suspension. Under OAR 863-015-0130, failure to disclose material information or failure to present documents in a timely manner constitutes a violation of the licensee's duties. Repeated violations escalate to revocation proceedings.
