The Missouri Real Estate Commission (MREC) has finalized a package of regulatory updates that take effect on August 1, 2026, and the compliance window is closing fast. If you hold a Missouri license, understanding Missouri new real estate laws 2026 is not optional—it is the difference between clean closings and disciplinary hearings. These changes touch buyer representation, seller disclosures, commission transparency, and supervisory obligations for brokers.

MREC published its final rule amendments in the Missouri Register on March 14, 2026, following a 90-day public comment period that drew more than 400 submissions from licensees statewide. The revisions align Missouri practice with the post-NAR settlement reality while addressing gaps the commission identified in consumer protection.

## Missouri New Real Estate Laws 2026: The Buyer Agency Overhaul

**Missouri's revised Chapter 339 now requires a signed, written buyer representation agreement before an agent conducts any property tour with a prospective buyer.** This goes beyond the previous standard, which only mandated written agreements before submitting an offer. The statutory authority sits in RSMo 339.150(3)(a), as amended by HB 1847 (2026 session).

The agreement must include three elements that were previously optional: a fixed termination date no longer than six months out, a specific disclosure of the compensation the buyer's agent expects, and a statement confirming that compensation is negotiable. MREC Form 1050 (Exclusive Buyer Representation Agreement) has been updated to incorporate all three fields.

Agents who previously relied on oral understandings or vague "let's look at a few homes first" arrangements face a hard stop. The commission's enforcement guidance, published in MREC Bulletin 2026-03, states that a single showing without a signed agreement constitutes a violation subject to investigation.

## Updated Seller Disclosure Requirements Under RSMo 442.606

Missouri's Seller's Disclosure Statement has been expanded. The revised form—Missouri REALTORS Form 2159 (revised 01/2026)—now includes mandatory fields for known flooding history in the past 10 years, prior insurance claims related to structural defects, and any pending special assessments from homeowners' associations or community improvement districts.

Previously, flooding disclosure relied on the narrower language of RSMo 442.606(2), which only required sellers to disclose whether the property was in a designated FEMA floodplain. The 2026 amendment extends the obligation to any known water intrusion event regardless of federal mapping. Sellers cannot simply check "unknown" on a flooding question if they have submitted a prior insurance claim—doing so creates a rebuttable presumption of knowledge.

**Listing agents must deliver the updated form to buyers no later than five calendar days after mutual acceptance of the purchase contract.** Failure triggers an automatic five-business-day rescission window for the buyer, measured from actual receipt. The rescission right cannot be waived by contract.

## Commission Transparency and the New Form 1100

Form 1100—the Missouri Agency Disclosure and Election form—replaces the multi-page pamphlet that agents have handed out (and buyers have ignored) for more than a decade. The new single-page document requires the agent to specify, in dollar amount or percentage, what compensation they will receive and from whom.

| Element | Old Pamphlet | New Form 1100 |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Length | 4 pages | 1 page |
| Compensation Disclosure | Not required | Dollar or percentage, mandatory |
| Timing of Delivery | "At first substantive contact" | Before first showing or listing presentation |
| Signature Required | Buyer or seller signature | Both parties plus agent signature |
| Filing Obligation | Retain in file | Submit copy to broker within 48 hours |

This change mirrors what other states have implemented post-settlement, but Missouri's version carries teeth. Under the amended RSMo 339.100, failure to properly complete and deliver Form 1100 is classified as a Category B violation, carrying fines up to $2,500 per occurrence and mandatory continuing education remediation. Similar shifts in commission disclosure are emerging nationwide—agents familiar with [how commission structures are changing in Washington](/blog/washington-commission-structure-changes-2026) will recognize the pattern.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers carry direct supervisory liability under RSMo 339.120 for their agents' compliance with the new requirements. MREC Bulletin 2026-04 specifically names four audit obligations that brokers must perform or delegate to a compliance officer.

**Every brokerage must implement a system to verify that Form 1050 and Form 1100 are signed, dated, and filed before any showing activity occurs.** MREC has indicated it will conduct random file audits beginning October 2026, focusing on transactions that closed between August and September. Brokerages that cannot produce the required forms during an audit face fines assessed against the broker's license, not just the individual agent.

Brokers should also verify that the revised Form 2159 (Seller's Disclosure) is the correct version in every listing file. Using the pre-2026 version after August 1 does not satisfy the statutory requirement, even if the substantive disclosures are identical. The commission treats outdated forms as incomplete disclosures.

Additionally, brokers must confirm that all buyer representation agreements contain a termination date within the six-month maximum. Any open-ended agreement filed after August 1 constitutes a violation, and the broker is the responsible party under MREC's supervisory framework. ## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make (and How to Avoid Them)

Three months into any regulatory change, MREC complaint files reveal predictable patterns. Based on similar transitions in other states and MREC's own enforcement history, here are the errors most likely to generate disciplinary action.

**First, agents will use the old pamphlet instead of Form 1100.** Many brokerages stockpiled printed copies of the prior agency disclosure pamphlet. After August 1, distributing that document—even alongside the new form—creates confusion and does not satisfy the statutory requirement. Shred the old stock.

Second, agents will forget the "before first showing" timing rule for buyer agreements. The prior standard allowed agents to secure a signature before the first offer, which often meant getting the form signed at the kitchen table during offer preparation. Under the 2026 rules, that is too late. The agreement must precede any in-person or virtual property tour. Agents who show "just one house" without a signed Form 1050 are exposed.

Third, listing agents will deliver the outdated Seller's Disclosure form because their CRM auto-populates the old template. If your transaction management system still has the pre-2026 Form 2159 loaded, you are generating non-compliant documents at scale. Update your templates immediately. Understanding [where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) makes it clear that disclosure failures are among the top causes of post-contract collapse.

Fourth, agents will fail to disclose their compensation in a specific dollar amount or percentage on Form 1100, instead writing vague language like "per MLS" or "as negotiated." The commission's guidance is explicit: a number must appear on the form. Ranges are not acceptable.

Fifth, buyer's agents will neglect to include the mandatory "compensation is negotiable" statement in Form 1050. This single omission renders the agreement non-compliant and exposes the agent to a complaint from any buyer who later feels pressured into a commission structure.

## Liability Exposure: What Happens When Agents Miss the Mark

The consequences extend beyond MREC fines. A buyer who does not receive the updated Seller's Disclosure within the required window gains a statutory rescission right under RSMo 442.606(5). That means a deal can fall apart days after what everyone thought was a binding contract—and the listing agent who failed to deliver the form on time may face a claim for the buyer's inspection costs, appraisal fees, and related damages.

On the buyer side, an unsigned or improperly completed Form 1050 can render a commission claim unenforceable. If a buyer closes on a property and the agent never secured a compliant written agreement, the agent has no contractual basis to collect compensation. Missouri courts have consistently held that oral agreements for real estate brokerage services violate the [Statute of Frauds (RSMo 432.010)](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=432.010) and are unenforceable.

MREC's disciplinary matrix, updated in April 2026, classifies repeated Form 1100 violations as grounds for license suspension after three substantiated complaints within a 24-month period. The commission does not issue warnings for Category B violations—the first substantiated complaint results in a fine and mandatory CE hours.
