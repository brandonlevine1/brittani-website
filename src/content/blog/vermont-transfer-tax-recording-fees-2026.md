## Understanding Vermont Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Vermont's property transfer tax is one of the more complex in New England, and it catches agents off guard more often than it should. If you handle transactions in the Green Mountain State, understanding the Vermont transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 is not optional — it is the difference between a smooth closing and a deal that stalls at the town clerk's window. The rates, forms, and filing obligations have specific statutory roots that every licensed agent and supervising broker must track.

The property transfer tax in Vermont is governed primarily by 32 V.S.A. Chapter 231, Sections 9601 through 9618. Recording fees, meanwhile, fall under 32 V.S.A. Section 1671 and are administered at the municipal level by individual town clerks. These two separate frameworks intersect at the moment of recording, and mistakes in either one can delay title transfer, trigger penalties, or expose an agent to liability claims.

## The Rate Structure: Who Pays What

**Vermont splits its property transfer tax between buyer and seller, which is unusual compared to most states.** The seller is responsible for a flat rate of 0.5% of the property's fair market value. The buyer owes 1.25% — unless the buyer qualifies for the reduced principal residence rate.

The principal residence exemption drops the buyer's rate from 1.25% to 0.5% on the first $200,000 of the purchase price. To qualify, the buyer must occupy the property as a primary residence within a specified period after closing. Any amount above $200,000 is taxed at the full 1.25% rate, even for qualifying principal residence buyers.

Here is a breakdown of how the tax applies at various price points for a qualifying principal residence buyer versus a non-qualifying buyer:

| Purchase Price | Seller's Tax (0.5%) | Buyer's Tax (Principal Residence) | Buyer's Tax (Non-Principal Residence) | Total Tax (Principal Residence) | Total Tax (Non-Principal Residence) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $250,000 | $1,250 | $1,625 | $3,125 | $2,875 | $4,375 |
| $400,000 | $2,000 | $3,500 | $5,000 | $5,500 | $7,000 |
| $550,000 | $2,750 | $5,375 | $6,875 | $8,125 | $9,625 |
| $750,000 | $3,750 | $7,875 | $9,375 | $11,625 | $13,125 |

The buyer's principal residence calculation for a $400,000 purchase, for example, works out as follows: 0.5% on the first $200,000 ($1,000) plus 1.25% on the remaining $200,000 ($2,500), totaling $3,500. That difference of $1,500 compared to the non-qualifying rate is significant enough that agents must confirm residency intent early in the transaction.

## Recording Fees at the Town Clerk Level

Vermont does not have a single statewide recording fee schedule set by statute in the way some states do. Instead, 32 V.S.A. Section 1671 authorizes town clerks to charge fees for recording deeds, mortgages, liens, and other instruments. The standard rate across most Vermont municipalities is $15 per page for recording.

Additional fees may apply for documents that require indexing or that exceed standard page counts. Some towns also charge a separate land records fee or surcharge earmarked for the preservation of historical records. Agents should confirm the exact fee schedule with the specific town clerk's office where the property is located, because a closing prepared with incorrect recording fees will need last-minute adjustments that delay funding.

**Municipal recording fees are paid at the time the deed is presented for recording, and town clerks have the authority to refuse documents that are not accompanied by the correct payment.** This means your title company or closing attorney must have exact figures before the closing table is set.

## Required Forms and Filing Procedures

The centerpiece of Vermont's transfer tax compliance is [Form PT-172](https://tax.vermont.gov/property/transfer-tax), the Property Transfer Tax Return. This form must be completed and filed with the town clerk at the time the deed is recorded. Without it, the clerk will not accept the deed for recording, full stop.

Form PT-172 requires detailed information about both the buyer and seller, the property address, the sale price, the allocation of the transfer tax, and whether the buyer is claiming the principal residence exemption. The form is filed in duplicate — one copy stays with the town clerk, and one is forwarded to the Vermont Department of Taxes.

Agents also encounter Form HS-122, the Homestead Declaration and Property Tax Adjustment Claim, when buyers intend to claim the property as a homestead. While HS-122 is technically a property tax form rather than a transfer tax document, its filing deadline often overlaps with the closing timeline, and failure to file it correctly can affect the buyer's property tax rate in the first year of ownership. Coordinating these forms is precisely the kind of deadline management that trips up busy agents, and it mirrors the [systemic deadline challenges](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that plague transactions in every state.

## What Happens When You Get It Wrong

Noncompliance with Vermont's transfer tax statutes is not a slap on the wrist. Under 32 V.S.A. Section 9610, late filing of the Property Transfer Tax Return triggers a penalty of 5% of the unpaid tax for each month the return is delinquent, up to a maximum penalty of 25%. Interest accrues on top of that at a rate set annually by the Department of Taxes.

If the Department determines that underpayment resulted from negligence or intentional disregard of the rules, an additional penalty of up to 100% of the underpaid amount can be assessed under 32 V.S.A. Section 9612. Fraud carries criminal penalties. These are not theoretical risks — the Department actively audits property transfers and issues assessments.

For agents, the professional exposure goes beyond tax penalties. A buyer who loses the principal residence rate because the agent failed to file PT-172 correctly has a straightforward negligence claim. A seller who faces a delayed closing because recording fees were miscalculated can demand compensation for carrying costs, rate-lock extensions, and other damages. The Vermont Real Estate Commission can also investigate complaints related to agent incompetence in handling transaction documentation, which puts your license at risk.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Vermont

**The first and most frequent error is assuming the principal residence exemption applies without verifying eligibility.** Agents sometimes check the box on PT-172 based on a buyer's verbal statement that they plan to live in the property, without confirming that the buyer meets the statutory requirements. If the buyer does not actually occupy the property as a principal residence, the Department of Taxes will reassess the tax at the full 1.25% rate, plus penalties and interest — and the buyer will look to the agent for an explanation.

The second common mistake is miscalculating the split between buyer and seller. Vermont's division of the transfer tax is not negotiable by contract in the same way closing costs are allocated in many other states. The statutory obligation assigns specific percentages to each party. Agents who draft purchase agreements that shift the tax burden without understanding the statutory framework create confusion at closing and potential disputes after.

Third, agents frequently fail to coordinate with the correct town clerk's office. Vermont has 246 towns and cities, and the recording must happen in the municipality where the property is located. Sending documents to the wrong clerk's office wastes days and can blow past contractual closing deadlines. This is one of those [process breakdowns](/blog/where-deals-break-down) that seems minor until it collapses a timeline.

Fourth, some agents neglect to account for recording fees when preparing estimated closing cost sheets. When the actual fees at the town clerk's window differ from the estimates, buyers and sellers must produce additional funds at the last minute or the recording is refused.

Fifth, agents occasionally overlook the requirement that PT-172 must accompany the deed at the time of recording. Filing the tax return after the deed has been submitted, or submitting an incomplete return, results in rejection by the clerk and a delayed transfer of title.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Supervising brokers in Vermont carry a direct obligation to ensure their agents handle transfer tax and recording fee documentation accurately. The Vermont Real Estate Commission, operating under the Office of Professional Regulation, holds brokers accountable for the acts of their licensees when those acts fall within the scope of real estate practice.

Brokers should audit every closing file for three things. First, confirm that Form PT-172 is fully completed and that the principal residence box is checked only when supporting documentation exists. Second, verify that the transfer tax amounts on the closing disclosure match the statutory rates applied to the actual sale price. Third, confirm that recording fees listed on the estimated and final closing statements reflect the rates charged by the specific town clerk's office handling the recording.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| PT-172 Completion | All fields filled, signatures present, residency box accurate | Missing buyer SSN, unchecked residency box with no documentation |
| Transfer Tax Calculation | 0.5% seller, 1.25% buyer (or split rate for principal residence) | Tax amount does not match sale price at statutory rates |
| Recording Fee Accuracy | Per-page fee matches town clerk schedule | Flat fee estimate used instead of actual per-page calculation |
| Filing Timeliness | PT-172 filed simultaneously with deed recording | Return filed days after deed, or not filed at all |
| Closing Disclosure Match | CD figures match PT-172 and town clerk receipts | Discrepancies between CD, tax return, and actual fees paid |

Brokers who rely on [memory instead of structured checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) for this kind of compliance review are gambling with their brokerage's reputation and their agents' licenses. A quarterly file audit protocol — not just a closing-day review — catches systemic errors before they become systemic liabilities.

## Exemptions and Special Situations

Not every transfer triggers the property transfer tax. Vermont law provides specific exemptions under 32 V.S.A. Section 9603. Transfers between spouses, transfers to or from governmental entities, transfers pursuant to a court-ordered partition, and certain transfers related to foreclosure or bankruptcy proceedings may qualify for partial or full exemptions.

However, claiming an exemption still requires filing PT-172. The form includes checkboxes for the specific exemption category, and the town clerk must receive the completed return even when no tax is due. Agents who assume that an exempt transfer means no paperwork is necessary will find the deed rejected at the recording window.

Transfers involving land trusts, LLCs, or other entities add another layer of complexity. The Department of Taxes scrutinizes entity transfers closely because they are sometimes used to avoid triggering the transfer tax. When a controlling interest in an entity that holds Vermont real estate changes hands, the transfer tax may still apply even though no deed is recorded.
