## Understanding Maryland Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on a settlement statement is not a minor inconvenience—it delays recordings, angers clients, and exposes your brokerage to liability. The Maryland transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 carry specific statutory obligations that agents must meet before a deed reaches the Clerk of the Circuit Court. Missing a single exemption affidavit or miscalculating a county surcharge can hold up a transaction for days.

Maryland's transfer and recordation tax framework sits primarily within the Tax-Property Article (Title 12 and Title 13) of the Annotated Code of Maryland. The State Department of Assessments and Taxation (SDAT) administers these taxes, while each county's Clerk of the Circuit Court handles the physical recording and collection at the window. Agents who understand this two-layer system—state statutes plus county-specific schedules—close faster and protect their clients from surprise charges.

## State Transfer Tax: Rates, Statutes, and Split Obligations

**Maryland imposes a state transfer tax of 0.5% on the consideration paid for real property, codified in Tax-Property Article Section 13-203.** That 0.5% is split equally—0.25% from the buyer and 0.25% from the seller—unless the parties contractually agree to a different allocation on the settlement sheet.

First-time Maryland homebuyers receive a statutory exemption from the buyer's 0.25% share under TP Section 13-203(b). To claim it, the buyer must execute a First-Time Homebuyer Affidavit (often integrated into the intake documents by the title company) attesting they have never held title to residential real property in any state. This affidavit is filed with the deed at recording.

Certain transactions are exempt from the state transfer tax entirely. Transfers between spouses, transfers to or from governmental entities, and transfers incident to a divorce decree fall under the exemptions listed in TP Section 13-207. Agents should not assume an exemption applies—each must be documented with a supporting affidavit or court order attached to the recording package.

## Recordation Tax: The Bigger Number Most Agents Underestimate

The recordation tax is separate from the transfer tax, and it is often the larger line item on the HUD or ALTA settlement statement. Under Tax-Property Article Section 12-103, Maryland imposes a state recordation tax of $7.00 per $500 (or fraction thereof) of consideration. That works out to 1.4% of the sale price—a figure that catches newer agents off guard.

| Fee Component | Rate / Amount | Statutory Authority |
|---|---|---|
| State Transfer Tax | 0.5% of consideration (split 50/50) | TP Section 13-203 |
| State Recordation Tax | $7.00 per $500 of consideration (1.4%) | TP Section 12-103 |
| County Transfer Tax (varies) | 0% to 1.5% depending on jurisdiction | County Code |
| Recording Fee (deed, first page) | $60–$115 (varies by county) | County Clerk schedule |
| Recording Fee (each additional page) | $5–$20 per page | County Clerk schedule |

Counties may impose their own local transfer tax on top of the state amounts. For example, Montgomery County adds 1% for transactions over $500,000 through its own county code provisions, while Howard County imposes 1% on all residential transactions. Baltimore City charges 1.5%—one of the highest in the state. Agents listing properties in border areas between counties must verify which jurisdiction's rate applies based on the property's physical location, not the mailing address.

## County-Level Variations That Catch Agents Off Guard

**Not every county in Maryland charges the same recording fees or surcharges, and relying on a single fee template across jurisdictions is a recipe for settlement-day surprises.** The Clerk of the Circuit Court in each of Maryland's 23 counties and Baltimore City publishes its own recording fee schedule. These schedules cover per-page fees for deeds, deeds of trust, assignments, releases, and other instruments.

Here is a comparison of selected county transfer tax rates as of the 2026 schedule:

| Jurisdiction | Local Transfer Tax Rate | Notable Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Baltimore City | 1.5% | Applies to all residential and commercial |
| Montgomery County | 1.0% | Additional 0.25% on sales over $500K |
| Howard County | 1.0% | Flat rate, residential and commercial |
| Prince George's County | 1.4% | Includes county + special taxing district |
| Anne Arundel County | 1.0% | Standard residential |
| Frederick County | 0% | No local transfer tax imposed |

Frederick County's zero-rate status surprises many agents accustomed to working in the Baltimore-Washington corridor. Conversely, Prince George's County's combined effective rate (state recordation + state transfer + county transfer) can approach 4.3% of the sale price—a number that must appear on buyer cost estimates early in the transaction to avoid sticker shock at the closing table.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Maryland real estate transactions require specific forms for tax payment and recording. The Maryland Real Property Transfer Tax Return is the primary document filed with SDAT to report the transfer tax due. Title companies typically prepare this form, but agents must verify the information matches the contract terms.

The Intake Sheet (sometimes called the Title Order Form) submitted to the title company should accurately reflect whether the buyer qualifies as a first-time homebuyer, whether any exemptions apply, and whether the parties have agreed to a non-standard tax split. Errors on intake cascade into incorrect settlement statements. The ALTA Settlement Statement (formerly the HUD-1 for transactions before 2015) must itemize all recordation taxes, transfer taxes, and recording fees with line-item specificity.

For [FIRPTA-related withholding considerations](/blog/washington-firpta-withholding-rules-2026) that may apply when a foreign seller is involved, agents should coordinate with the settlement attorney early—Maryland transfer taxes are still owed regardless of FIRPTA status.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong: Liability and Consequences

The Clerk of the Circuit Court will reject any deed package that arrives with incorrect tax payments. Rejection means the deed does not record, title does not transfer, and the buyer does not own the property on the expected date. For transactions tied to back-to-back closings or rate-lock expirations, a single-day delay can cost thousands.

**Under Maryland Real Property Article Section 3-104, a deed that is not recorded is not effective against subsequent bona fide purchasers.** If your buyer's deed sits in limbo because the transfer tax calculation was $200 short, and the seller (or seller's creditor) records a lien in the interim, your buyer's priority position is at risk. This is not hypothetical—it happens in contested estates and short sales.

Beyond recording rejection, SDAT can assess penalties and interest on unpaid or underpaid transfer taxes under TP Section 13-207. The agent is not personally liable for the tax itself (that obligation falls on the parties), but a pattern of errors can trigger complaints to the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) under COMAR 09.11.01, potentially resulting in disciplinary action including fines or license suspension.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Maryland Transfer Taxes

The first common error is failing to identify a first-time homebuyer exemption early in the transaction. When the exemption is discovered at the settlement table, the title company scrambles to prepare the affidavit, delaying recording. Agents should ask about homeownership history during the initial buyer consultation—not at the final walkthrough.

The second mistake is assuming the contract's stated allocation of transfer taxes will automatically appear on the settlement statement. Maryland Association of Realtors (MAR) Form 400 (the Residential Contract of Sale) contains a section specifying who pays which taxes, but if the intake sheet sent to the title company omits this detail, the default 50/50 split applies. Agents must confirm in writing that the title company received and applied the contractual allocation.

Third, agents working in multiple jurisdictions frequently apply the wrong county transfer tax rate. Pulling last year's rate from a different county—or worse, from a template used in Frederick County where the rate is zero—creates an underestimate that surfaces at closing. Always verify the current schedule directly with the county Clerk's office or the [Maryland SDAT website](https://dat.maryland.gov/Pages/default.aspx).

Fourth, agents sometimes neglect to account for the agricultural land transfer tax under TP Section 13-301 through 13-305. When farmland or land under an agricultural preservation easement changes use, an additional tax applies. This catches agents off guard in rural counties like Carroll, Garrett, and Allegany.

Fifth, overlooking the deed preparation fee and assuming it is included in the title company's settlement fee leads to line-item disputes at closing. The deed preparation is a separate charge—typically $150–$350—and it must be disclosed on the buyer's Loan Estimate if a lender is involved.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers have a supervisory duty under COMAR 09.11.02.03 to ensure their agents are not providing inaccurate cost information to consumers. A broker's compliance review should include spot-checking settlement statements against the executed contract to verify that tax allocations match. If an agent consistently quotes incorrect closing costs, the broker faces potential liability under the Maryland Consumer Protection Act for unfair or deceptive trade practices.

**Every brokerage operating in Maryland should maintain an updated county-by-county fee schedule and require agents to reference it before distributing any buyer or seller net sheet.** A static PDF from 2024 is not sufficient—recording fees and county surcharges change, and some jurisdictions adjust mid-year. The broker's transaction management system should flag any net sheet that has not been updated within 90 days.

Brokers should also audit whether their agents are properly identifying exempt transactions. Claiming a first-time homebuyer exemption for a buyer who previously owned property constitutes fraud—and the brokerage's name is on the transaction file. Requiring a signed buyer questionnaire at the contract stage provides an audit trail.
