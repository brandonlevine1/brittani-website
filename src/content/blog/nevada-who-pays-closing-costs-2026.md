## Nevada Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Statutory Framework

The question of Nevada who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not answered by a single state statute that assigns every fee to one party. Instead, Nevada law sets firm rules on a handful of specific charges and leaves the rest to contractual negotiation between the parties. Agents who assume local custom equals legal mandate put their licenses at risk.

Nevada Revised Statutes Chapter 113 governs real property disclosures and certain transaction requirements, while NRS Chapter 375 specifically addresses the real property transfer tax. NRS Chapter 645 outlines the duties and liabilities of licensees, including prohibitions on misrepresentation about transaction costs. The Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED) under the Department of Business and Industry enforces these standards and publishes advisory opinions that affect how closing costs are handled.

**Understanding which costs are statutory obligations versus negotiable items is the single most important distinction agents must draw for their clients.** Getting this wrong does not just kill deals — it generates complaints to NRED that land on your license record.

## Statutory Costs: What Nevada Law Actually Mandates

Only a few closing costs in Nevada are assigned by statute to a specific party. The real property transfer tax under [NRS 375.030](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-375.html) is declared the responsibility of the seller. In Clark County, that rate is $2.55 per $500 of value ($1.95 state plus $0.60 local). Washoe County adds its own increment, bringing the combined rate to $2.55 per $500 as well.

NRS 111.312 requires the seller to deliver a marketable title, which functionally means the seller bears the cost of clearing liens, paying off existing encumbrances, and providing a grant, bargain, and sale deed. Recording fees for the deed of conveyance are typically charged to the buyer because the buyer is the party recording, though this remains negotiable in the contract.

NRS 113.130 requires sellers of residential property (1-4 units) to provide a Seller's Real Property Disclosure Form. The cost of preparing that disclosure is nominal, but any inspection or remediation it triggers falls on the seller unless the contract states otherwise.

## The Negotiable Middle Ground: What the Contract Controls

Everything outside those statutory mandates is open territory. Escrow fees, title insurance premiums, home warranties, HOA transfer fees, document preparation fees, and courier charges all default to whatever the purchase agreement says. This is where the forms matter enormously.

In southern Nevada, agents primarily use the Greater Las Vegas Association of Realtors (GLVAR) Residential Purchase Agreement. In northern Nevada, the Reno/Sparks Association of Realtors (RSAR) equivalent applies. Both forms contain a closing costs section where the parties designate responsibility for specific line items including escrow fees, title insurance, and various administrative charges.

**The GLVAR purchase agreement (revised 2025) includes Section 7, which breaks closing costs into buyer-paid, seller-paid, and split categories with checkboxes.** If an agent leaves those boxes blank or defaults to "local custom" without documenting the agreement, they have created an ambiguity that can unravel the transaction during final settlement review.

| Cost Category | Typical Buyer Responsibility | Typical Seller Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Real property transfer tax | — | Required by NRS 375.030 |
| Owner's title insurance | Negotiable (often seller in Clark Co.) | Negotiable (often seller in Clark Co.) |
| Lender's title insurance | Yes (lender requirement) | — |
| Escrow fees | Split 50/50 (common custom) | Split 50/50 (common custom) |
| Recording fees (deed) | Yes (new owner records) | — |
| Recording fees (reconveyance) | — | Yes (clearing old lien) |
| HOA transfer/disclosure fees | Negotiable | Often seller (resale packages) |
| Home warranty | Negotiable | Often seller (as concession) |

Custom is not law. Agents must confirm every allocation in writing within the contract itself.

## Seller Concessions and Lender Limits in 2026

Seller-paid closing cost concessions remain one of the most common negotiation tools in Nevada, particularly in markets where inventory favors buyers. However, agents must stay current on lender-imposed caps that limit how much a seller can contribute toward the buyer's costs.

For conventional loans, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac cap interested party contributions at 3% of the sales price for buyers putting down less than 10%, 6% for 10-25% down, and 9% for more than 25% down. FHA allows up to 6%. VA allows up to 4% in concessions plus the ability to pay certain fees outside that cap. These limits have not changed for 2026 but are frequently misapplied by agents who confuse "closing costs" with "prepaids."

**Agents who draft a contract with a seller concession exceeding the lender's cap will see the loan denied or the concession reduced at the last minute, jeopardizing the close date.** Always verify the buyer's loan program before writing in a specific dollar figure or percentage for seller contributions. This is a recurring source of [deal breakdowns](/blog/where-deals-break-down) that experienced transaction coordinators catch during file review.

## Common Mistakes Nevada Agents Make on Closing Costs

The following five errors generate the most complaints, deal delays, and NRED disciplinary actions related to closing cost allocation in Nevada.

First, agents tell clients that "the seller always pays for the owner's title policy" as if it were law. It is not. Clark County custom favors that arrangement, but in rural Nevada counties the buyer often pays. When an agent from Las Vegas lists a property in Nye County and assumes Clark County norms apply, the settlement statement arrives with an unexpected charge that neither party agreed to in writing.

Second, agents fail to fill in the closing cost allocation section of the GLVAR or RSAR form completely. Blank fields default to nothing — they do not default to custom. The title company or escrow officer will call for clarification, delaying the file and occasionally triggering a dispute when one party claims oral agreement.

Third, agents miscalculate the real property transfer tax or misattribute it to the buyer. Under NRS 375.030, the tax is the seller's obligation. An agent who writes it into the buyer's estimated costs sheet is not just sloppy — they may be misrepresenting material transaction terms under NRS 645.633(1)(h).

Fourth, agents neglect to account for HOA-related closing costs. Nevada's Common-Interest Community provisions under NRS Chapter 116 require specific disclosures and create specific fees (resale packages, status certificates, transfer fees) that vary wildly between associations. Failing to identify these early means a surprise bill at closing that neither party budgeted for.

Fifth, agents promise a net amount to sellers without running accurate closing cost estimates first. When the actual settlement statement differs from the agent's verbal estimate by thousands of dollars, the seller may refuse to close or file a complaint alleging negligence.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers operating under NRS 645.370 bear supervisory responsibility for their agents' transactional conduct. That statute requires brokers to maintain and supervise transaction records, and NRED has consistently held that broker oversight extends to ensuring closing cost representations match contractual terms.

Every brokerage should audit three things on every file before it reaches escrow. First, confirm the closing cost section of the purchase agreement is fully completed — no blank checkboxes, no "TBD" entries. Second, verify that any seller concession written into the contract does not exceed the applicable lender cap for the buyer's loan product. Third, cross-reference the preliminary settlement statement from the title company against the contract terms to ensure allocations match.

| Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Contract Section 7 (GLVAR) | All cost allocations marked | Ambiguity leads to closing delays |
| Seller concession amount | Within lender cap for loan type | Loan denial or last-minute renegotiation |
| Transfer tax attribution | Assigned to seller per NRS 375 | Misrepresentation complaint to NRED |
| HOA fees identification | Resale package and transfer fees noted | Surprise costs derail settlement |
| Preliminary HUD/CD review | Line items match contract | Signing delays, party disputes |

Brokers who run a [systematic weekly file review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) catch these issues before they become grievances. A single NRED complaint about closing cost misrepresentation can trigger a full brokerage audit that pulls resources away from production for months.

## Liability Exposure Under NRS 645

NRS 645.633 enumerates grounds for disciplinary action against licensees. Subsection (1)(h) covers making a material misrepresentation, and subsection (1)(l) covers demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness. Misallocating closing costs or leaving allocations undefined can fall under either provision depending on intent.

Penalties range from a letter of reprimand to a fine of up to $10,000 per violation, license suspension, or revocation. NRED also has authority under NRS 645.6321 to require restitution to harmed parties. If a buyer pays a cost that the contract assigned to the seller because the agent's error propagated through escrow unchecked, the agent and their brokerage face both regulatory action and civil liability.

Deal cancellation is another practical consequence. Under Section 12 of the GLVAR purchase agreement, either party can declare default if the other refuses to pay a cost contractually assigned to them. If the assignment is ambiguous because the agent left the field blank, neither party has clean grounds to compel performance, and the transaction collapses into mediation or litigation.
