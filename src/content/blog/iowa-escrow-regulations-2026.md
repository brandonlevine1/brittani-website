## Why Iowa Escrow Regulations 2026 Demand Your Attention This Year

The Iowa Real Estate Commission tightened its enforcement posture in late 2025, and the ripple effects are hitting agents who thought their trust-account habits were good enough. Understanding Iowa escrow regulations 2026 is no longer optional homework—it is the difference between a clean renewal and a disciplinary hearing. Two consent orders published in the first quarter of 2026 involved brokerages that failed basic deposit-timing rules, resulting in combined penalties exceeding $18,000.

Iowa Code Chapter 543B and the corresponding Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 193E remain the governing authorities for escrow and trust account management. The statutes have not been overhauled in decades, but the Commission's interpretive guidance and audit frequency have shifted. Agents who rely on informal practices passed down from mentors risk running afoul of rules that the Commission now enforces with less tolerance for "honest mistakes."

This post breaks down the statutes, the forms, the penalties, and the operational habits brokers must audit to protect their licenses and their agents.

## Governing Statutes and Regulatory Bodies

**Iowa Code Section 543B.34 is the statutory backbone of escrow compliance for every real estate licensee in the state.** It grants the Iowa Real Estate Commission authority to adopt rules governing the handling of trust funds, mandate record-keeping standards, and initiate disciplinary proceedings. The Commission itself sits within the Iowa Professional Licensing Bureau under the Department of Inspections, Appeals, and Licensing.

Iowa Administrative Code Chapter 193E, specifically Rules 13.1 through 13.5, provides the operational details. Rule 13.1 defines what constitutes a trust account. Rule 13.3 sets deposit timelines. Rule 13.5 addresses disbursement conditions and the procedures when a dispute arises between parties.

Agents should also be aware of Iowa Code Section 543B.29(2), which lists grounds for license suspension or revocation. Failure to account for or remit money belonging to others is explicitly enumerated. The Commission publishes enforcement actions on its [official website](https://plb.iowa.gov/real-estate), and reviewing recent orders is one of the fastest ways to understand current priorities.

## Key Forms and Documentation Requirements

Iowa residential transactions rely on forms published by the Iowa Association of Realtors and approved by the Iowa Real Estate Commission. The primary purchase agreement—IAR Form 101 (Residential Purchase Agreement)—contains provisions in Section 4 specifying which broker holds the earnest money deposit, the amount, and the deadline for delivery.

When disputes arise over escrow disbursement, agents must reference IAR Form 112 (Release of Earnest Money), which requires signatures from both buyer and seller before funds leave the trust account. If one party refuses to sign, the broker cannot unilaterally release the funds without a court order or a signed written agreement from both parties directing disbursement.

**Brokers must also maintain IAR Form 130 (Trust Account Record) or its equivalent ledger system that tracks every deposit, withdrawal, and running balance by transaction.** The Commission does not mandate a specific software platform, but it does require that records be available for inspection within 72 hours of a written request. Failing to produce legible, current records during an audit triggers an automatic investigation.

| Form | Purpose | When Used |
|------|---------|-----------|
| IAR Form 101 | Residential Purchase Agreement | Contract execution |
| IAR Form 112 | Release of Earnest Money | Disbursement after closing or cancellation |
| IAR Form 130 | Trust Account Record | Ongoing—every deposit and withdrawal |
| IAR Form 107 | Addendum to Purchase Agreement | Modifying escrow terms mid-transaction |

## Deposit Timing and the Five-Day Rule

Iowa Administrative Code 193E-13.3 requires that earnest money be deposited into the designated trust account no later than five banking days after receipt, unless the purchase agreement explicitly provides a different timeline. "Banking days" excludes weekends and federal holidays. An agent who receives a check on a Friday afternoon has until the following Friday to deposit it, assuming no holidays intervene.

The rule applies from the moment the broker or any licensee under the broker receives the funds—not from the date of mutual acceptance. This distinction catches agents off guard. If a buyer hands you a check at the time of offer submission and the seller does not accept for another four days, your deposit clock started the day you took possession.

Some brokerages build tighter internal policies, requiring deposit within 48 hours. That is permissible. The statute sets a floor, not a ceiling. Agents should confirm their brokerage's internal policy, because violating the brokerage policy can trigger employment consequences even if the statutory deadline has not yet passed.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

**License discipline is the most immediate threat, but it is not the only one.** Under Iowa Code 543B.29, the Commission can suspend or revoke a license, impose civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation, issue a citation and warning, or place a licensee on probation with conditions. Multiple violations in a single transaction can stack, meaning a single botched escrow situation could generate five-figure exposure.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability. A buyer whose earnest money is lost, misapplied, or untimely returned can sue for breach of fiduciary duty. Iowa courts have awarded consequential damages in cases where a failed escrow deposit caused the buyer to lose the property to a competing offer. The broker is vicariously liable for agent conduct under Iowa Code 543B.33.

Deal cancellation is also a real outcome. If an agent fails to deposit earnest money within the contractual timeframe, the seller may argue that the buyer breached the purchase agreement. The [reasons deals collapse](/blog/where-deals-break-down) frequently trace back to administrative failures that agents thought were minor at the time.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Make With Escrow

The first recurring error is holding checks in a desk drawer or car console past the deposit window. Agents intend to "wait for acceptance" before depositing, but the statute does not condition the deposit obligation on acceptance. The clock starts at receipt. Commission investigators have cited agents who held checks for 10 or more days while negotiations continued.

The second mistake is depositing earnest money into the wrong account. Some agents accidentally direct funds to an operating account or a personal account. Even a same-day correction does not erase the commingling violation. The Commission treats the momentary deposit into a non-trust account as a completed act of commingling, and the corrective transfer is treated as mitigating—not exonerating—evidence.

Third, agents fail to get both-party signatures on Form 112 before disbursing funds after a deal falls apart. Under pressure from an angry buyer demanding an immediate refund, the listing agent verbally consents, and the holding broker releases the check. Without the signed release form, the broker has made an unauthorized disbursement. The [deadline management failures](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) that cause deals to crater often cascade into escrow release disputes.

Fourth, agents neglect to update the trust account ledger when an earnest money amount changes via addendum. If the parties agree to increase the deposit through IAR Form 107, and the additional funds arrive but are never logged as a separate line item, the ledger will not reconcile during an audit.

Fifth, some agents assume that because the title company will ultimately handle closing funds, the initial earnest money deposit does not need rigorous documentation. The Commission audits brokerages—not title companies—for pre-closing trust account compliance. Title company involvement at settlement does not retroactively cure early-stage violations.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry personal liability for the trust-account conduct of every licensee affiliated with their office. Iowa Code 543B.33 makes this explicit. A broker who has never personally mishandled a dollar can still lose their license if they failed to supervise an agent who did.

**Monthly trust-account reconciliation is the single most important audit habit a broker can maintain.** The Commission expects brokers to compare the bank statement, the individual transaction ledgers, and the aggregate trust ledger every 30 days. Discrepancies must be investigated and documented within five banking days of discovery. The Commission's audit checklist—available through the Professional Licensing Bureau—provides a template.

Brokers should also conduct random spot-checks of agent files to verify that deposit receipts match ledger entries and that Form 112 is present in every closed or cancelled file. Building this into a [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) ensures that trust-account hygiene is not a once-a-year scramble before license renewal.

Training documentation matters. If a broker can demonstrate that they provided written escrow policies to every agent at onboarding and conducted annual refresher training, the Commission is more likely to limit sanctions to the individual agent rather than imposing brokerage-wide discipline. Keep sign-off sheets with dates.

## Disputed Earnest Money: The Interpleader Option

When buyer and seller cannot agree on who gets the earnest money, Iowa law does not permit the broker to simply pick a side. Rule 193E-13.5 requires the broker to hold the funds in trust until receiving either a signed mutual release (Form 112), a court order, or a written agreement between the parties.

If no resolution appears forthcoming, the broker may file an interpleader action in Iowa district court. This deposits the disputed funds with the court and releases the broker from further liability. The filing fee and attorney costs are typically deducted from the escrowed amount unless the purchase agreement says otherwise.

Brokers who sit on disputed funds indefinitely without pursuing interpleader risk a Commission complaint from the frustrated party. There is no statutory deadline for filing interpleader, but Commission guidance suggests that holding disputed funds beyond 180 days without documented resolution attempts invites scrutiny.
