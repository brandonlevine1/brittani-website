## Iowa Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement 2026: The Ground Has Shifted

The Iowa commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 represent the single largest operational change Iowa agents have faced in decades. If you closed transactions before August 2024, your muscle memory around compensation is now a liability. The rules that took effect nationally under the NAR settlement agreement have been layered on top of Iowa-specific statutes and Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) regulations, creating a compliance environment that punishes assumption and rewards documentation.

Iowa agents operate under Iowa Code Chapter 543B and the administrative rules in Iowa Administrative Code 193E, both of which govern licensing, disclosure, and agency relationships. The NAR settlement didn't override state law—it added requirements on top of it. Understanding where federal settlement terms end and Iowa-specific obligations begin is the difference between a clean closing and a disciplinary hearing.

## What the NAR Settlement Actually Changed for Iowa Agents

The settlement, finalized in late 2024, imposed two headline requirements that restructured commission flow nationwide. First, offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer appear in any MLS field. Second, buyers must sign a written representation agreement before an agent can show them property.

In Iowa, the [Iowa Association of Realtors](https://www.iowarealtors.com) updated its standard forms to reflect these changes. The IAR 135 (Buyer Representation Agreement) was revised to include explicit compensation terms—amount, payment source, and conditions. The form now requires the agent to disclose whether compensation will come from the buyer directly, from the seller through negotiation, or from a combination of sources.

**The IREC did not adopt a separate rule specific to the NAR settlement, but it issued Guidance Bulletin 2024-03 clarifying that violations of MLS rules adopted pursuant to the settlement can constitute grounds for disciplinary action under Iowa Code Section 543B.29.** This means an MLS fine isn't the end of it—your license is on the line too. The Commission treats MLS compliance as part of your duty to act competently and honestly under Iowa law.

## Required Forms and Documentation in 2026

Iowa agents now work with a tighter stack of mandatory paperwork at the front end of every transaction. Missing any of these creates exposure that compounds as the deal progresses.

| Form/Document | Purpose | When Required | Governing Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| IREC 1 (Agency Disclosure) | Discloses agency relationship to all parties | First substantive contact | Iowa Code 543B.56 |
| IAR 135 (Buyer Representation Agreement) | Establishes compensation terms and scope of representation | Before touring any property | NAR Settlement + IREC Guidance 2024-03 |
| IAR 101 (Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement) | Defines seller compensation obligations | At listing appointment | Iowa Code 543B |
| Purchase Agreement Compensation Addendum (IAR 145) | Documents any seller contribution toward buyer-agent fees | With offer submission | NAR Settlement practice changes |

The IAR 145 addendum is particularly important because it replaced the informal practice of simply noting seller-paid commission in the MLS. If a seller agrees to contribute toward the buyer's agent compensation, that agreement must be memorialized in writing as part of the purchase agreement—not in the MLS, not in a text message, not in a verbal promise relayed through the listing agent.

Agents who skip the IAR 135 before showings aren't just risking an MLS violation. They're potentially violating IREC's requirement under 193E-14.1 that agents document the nature of the agency relationship and compensation arrangement in writing. This administrative rule predates the NAR settlement but now carries fresh enforcement priority.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Non-compliance exposes agents to three distinct layers of consequence: MLS penalties, state regulatory action, and private litigation.** Each operates independently, meaning a single infraction can trigger all three simultaneously.

On the MLS level, Iowa Regional MLS (IRMLS) and Des Moines Area Association of Realtors MLS have updated their rules and fine schedules. Advertising buyer-agent compensation in any MLS field—including agent remarks—can result in fines starting at $1,000 and escalating to MLS suspension for repeat violations.

At the state level, the Iowa Real Estate Commission can pursue disciplinary action under Iowa Code 543B.29, which lists "failing to provide a written agency agreement" and "incompetency" among grounds for license suspension or revocation. The Commission's standard penalty grid, published in its enforcement manual, places documentation failures in the "moderate" category, typically resulting in a civil penalty of $500 to $2,500 plus mandatory continuing education.

Private litigation is the wildcard. Buyers who discover they overpaid relative to agent compensation—or sellers who learn their agent offered compensation in violation of the settlement—may pursue breach of fiduciary duty claims. Iowa courts apply the standard set in *Baebler v. RE/MAX* (Iowa 2019) for evaluating whether an agent's compensation practices harmed a client. The damages in these cases can dwarf any regulatory fine.

## Common Mistakes Iowa Agents Are Making Right Now

Two years into the post-settlement environment, patterns of non-compliance have emerged. These aren't hypothetical risks—they're errors showing up in actual IREC complaints and MLS audits across Iowa.

**First, agents are using the listing remarks field to signal buyer-agent compensation.** Phrases like "generous cooperating compensation available—call listing agent" or "seller motivated to assist with buyer costs" are thinly veiled offers that violate the spirit and letter of the MLS prohibition. IRMLS compliance staff have flagged hundreds of these since January 2025.

Second, buyer agents are touring properties before executing the IAR 135. The excuse is usually some variation of "we were just doing a quick look" or "the client wasn't ready to commit." The settlement doesn't include a casual-viewing exception. If you're acting in a representative capacity and the buyer perceives you as their agent, the obligation attaches regardless of how informal the showing feels.

Third, agents are failing to update compensation terms when circumstances change mid-transaction. If a buyer initially agrees to pay their agent 2.5% but then negotiates a seller contribution of 1.5%, the IAR 135 must be amended to reflect the new arrangement. Many agents close without updating the form, creating a paper trail that contradicts the actual money flow—a red flag in any future audit or dispute.

Fourth, listing agents are verbally communicating compensation offers to buyer agents without any written documentation. Iowa Code 543B.56 and IREC rules require written memorialization of material transaction terms. A phone call saying "my seller will pay your buyer's agent 2%" means nothing without a signed IAR 145 addendum in the file.

Fifth, teams are assuming that one buyer representation agreement covers multiple transactions. Each property showing engagement requires its own IAR 135, or at minimum, the existing agreement must explicitly cover the scope of properties being toured. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers bear supervisory liability under Iowa Code 543B.34, which holds designated brokers responsible for the acts of their licensees performed within the scope of their license. A broker who fails to implement systems for post-settlement compliance isn't just managing risk poorly—they're personally exposed to disciplinary action.

| Audit Item | Frequency | What to Check | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| IAR 135 execution date vs. first showing date | Every buyer transaction | Agreement signed before or on date of first tour | MLS fine + IREC complaint |
| MLS listing language | Weekly | No compensation language in any field | MLS suspension + IREC action |
| IAR 145 addendum in file | Every transaction with seller contribution | Signed addendum matches actual compensation paid at closing | Breach of fiduciary duty claim |
| Compensation amendment documentation | Transaction close | IAR 135 amendments match closing statement | Audit failure + potential fraud allegation |
| IREC 1 delivery confirmation | Every new client relationship | Signed acknowledgment in file within required timeframe | Iowa Code 543B.56 violation |

**Brokers should implement a pre-closing file review that specifically checks for settlement compliance items, separate from the standard transaction checklist.** The old checklist that worked in 2023 doesn't catch the new requirements. [Why checklists outperform memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) isn't just productivity advice—it's a compliance imperative in this regulatory environment.

Iowa's designated broker requirement under 193E-3.1 means someone at every brokerage has their name on the line. That person needs to be reviewing compensation documentation at least as rigorously as they review earnest money handling. The IREC has signaled through its 2025 enforcement priorities memo that post-settlement compliance will be a focus area for audits through at least 2027.

## Compensation Negotiation in Practice: What's Actually Happening

The market has adapted unevenly. In Des Moines metro, seller contributions toward buyer-agent compensation remain common—appearing in roughly 70% of closed transactions according to IRMLS data from Q1 2026. In rural Iowa markets, buyer-paid compensation models have gained more traction, with approximately 35% of transactions now involving buyers paying their agent directly at closing.

This split creates challenges for agents working across markets. A compensation structure that buyers expect in Ames may surprise buyers in Cedar Rapids. The IAR 135's flexibility allows agents to structure compensation in multiple ways—flat fee, percentage, hourly, or hybrid—but the form must be specific. "To be determined" or "market rate" language in the compensation field of the IAR 135 does not satisfy the settlement's requirement that the amount be "objectively ascertainable."

Agents who want to remain competitive need to articulate their value before the compensation conversation happens. The days of commission being an invisible line item buried in the seller's closing costs are over.
