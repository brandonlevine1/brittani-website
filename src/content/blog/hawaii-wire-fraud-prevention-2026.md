## Why Hawaii Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026 Matter More Than Ever

Wire fraud losses in U.S. real estate transactions exceeded $446 million in 2023 according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and Hawaii's high median home prices make the state a prime target. Understanding the Hawaii wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 framework is no longer optional — it is a baseline licensing obligation that the Hawaii Real Estate Commission (REC) actively monitors. Agents who treat wire fraud warnings as a courtesy rather than a mandate are exposing themselves, their brokers, and their clients to catastrophic financial loss.

Hawaii's isolation in the Pacific amplifies the risk. Buyers frequently wire funds from mainland accounts, military relocation transactions involve multiple financial institutions across time zones, and the state's reliance on escrow (rather than attorney closings) concentrates transfer authority in a single point of failure. These structural realities drove the REC to formalize wire fraud disclosure obligations that took effect in late 2025 and carry full enforcement weight into 2026.

## The Legal Framework: HRS Chapter 467 and HAR Title 16

The statutory backbone for wire fraud prevention in Hawaii sits within [HRS Chapter 467](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/hrscurrent/Vol10_Ch0436-0474/HRS0467/HRS_0467-.htm), which authorizes the REC to impose duties of disclosure and consumer protection on all licensees. The administrative rules under HAR Title 16, Chapter 99 further specify how licensees must handle sensitive transaction information, including financial data transmitted during closing.

**The REC issued Bulletin 2025-04 in September 2025, establishing that all licensees must provide a written wire fraud advisory — Form WFA-1 — to every buyer and seller at or before the time of contract execution.** Failure to deliver this form now constitutes a violation of HAR Section 16-99-3(f), which addresses material omissions in licensee disclosures. The bulletin also clarified that verbal warnings alone do not satisfy the requirement.

Form WFA-1 is a single-page document that warns parties about email compromise schemes, instructs them to verify wiring instructions by phone using a number obtained independently (not from the same email containing wire details), and includes a signature acknowledgment line for each party. It is distinct from the escrow company's own wire fraud notice — agents cannot substitute one for the other.

## Form WFA-1: What It Contains and When to Deliver It

The Hawaii REC's Form WFA-1 has four sections: a description of common wire fraud schemes, a set of best practices for verifying instructions, a statement that the brokerage is not liable for losses resulting from a party's failure to follow the advisory, and the acknowledgment signatures. Agents must retain signed copies in the transaction file for a minimum of five years, consistent with HRS 467-14's general recordkeeping requirement.

Timing matters. The REC expects delivery at or before contract execution, meaning agents should include Form WFA-1 in the initial package alongside the Seller's Real Property Disclosure Statement (Form SPD) and the agency disclosure. Delivering it only at the closing table is too late — the wire has often already been sent by that point.

For transactions involving mainland or international buyers who sign remotely, agents must transmit Form WFA-1 electronically and obtain a digital signature before the buyer wires earnest money. This is the first wire transfer in most Hawaii transactions and the moment when fraud risk first materializes.

## Liability If You Skip the Advisory

Ignoring the wire fraud advisory requirement exposes agents to three distinct layers of liability. First, the REC can initiate disciplinary proceedings under HRS 467-14(20), which covers any conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness. Sanctions range from a formal reprimand to license suspension, with fines up to $2,500 per violation.

Second, civil liability is real and growing. Hawaii courts have recognized that a licensee's failure to warn can constitute negligence when the harm — a diverted wire — was foreseeable and the warning was both easy to provide and industry-standard. An agent who skips Form WFA-1 may find their errors and omissions carrier denying coverage on the basis that the loss resulted from a knowing deviation from required practice.

**Third, deal cancellation becomes a live risk.** If a buyer's funds are stolen mid-transaction because wiring instructions were compromised, the closing fails. The seller may refuse to extend deadlines, the buyer may lack liquid funds to re-wire, and the agent faces clawback pressure on any advance commission or referral payments already disbursed.

| Consequence | Who Bears It | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| REC disciplinary action | Agent and supervising broker | Missing Form WFA-1 in transaction file |
| Civil negligence claim | Agent, broker, potentially escrow | Diverted wire after failure to warn |
| E&O coverage denial | Agent individually | Documented non-compliance with REC bulletin |
| Deal collapse | All parties | Stolen funds with no recovery timeline |
| Fine up to $2,500 | Agent | Per-violation assessment by REC |

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Hawaii Wire Fraud Compliance

The first and most frequent error is treating the escrow company's wire fraud notice as a substitute for Form WFA-1. These are separate obligations. The escrow company's notice protects the escrow firm; Form WFA-1 protects the brokerage and satisfies the licensee's independent duty under HAR Title 16.

The second mistake is delivering the advisory too late. Agents who bundle it into the closing package miss the window. By the time a buyer sits at the closing table, earnest money has already been wired — sometimes weeks earlier. The REC's expectation is clear: delivery at or before contract execution.

A third common failure is not retaining a signed copy. Some agents collect signatures on loose paper that never makes it into the digital transaction file. When the REC audits — and they do, particularly after a consumer complaint — the absence of a signed WFA-1 is treated identically to never having provided one.

Fourth, agents forward wire instructions by email without adding a fraud warning in the body of that specific message. Even if Form WFA-1 was signed weeks earlier, transmitting raw wire details without a contemporaneous reminder violates the spirit of Bulletin 2025-04 and creates a discoverable lapse in any subsequent litigation. Every email containing account numbers or routing numbers should include a plain-text warning to verify by phone.

Fifth, some agents rely on their team coordinator or assistant to handle the advisory without confirming it was actually completed. Delegation is fine, but the licensee of record remains responsible. A missed form is the agent's problem, not the assistant's. ## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

**Brokers in Hawaii carry supervisory liability under HRS 467-1.6, which means a principal broker can face discipline for an associated agent's compliance failure if the broker lacked reasonable systems to prevent it.** This is not theoretical — the REC has sanctioned brokers specifically for inadequate transaction file oversight.

At minimum, brokers should audit for the presence of a signed Form WFA-1 in every closed transaction file. Quarterly random audits of open files are even better, catching missing forms before they become violations. The REC's own audit checklist, updated in January 2026, now includes a line item for wire fraud advisory confirmation.

| Broker Obligation | How to Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Signed Form WFA-1 in file | Digital file review or checklist confirmation | Every transaction |
| Timely delivery (at/before contract) | Compare WFA-1 signature date to contract date | Quarterly audit sample |
| Email forwarding policy | Spot-check agent emails for wire instruction handling | Semi-annual review |
| Training documentation | Annual wire fraud training attendance log | Annually |
| Incident response plan | Written plan accessible to all agents | Annual update and review |

Brokers should also maintain a written office policy on wire instruction handling. This policy should prohibit agents from emailing wire instructions without a fraud warning, require callback verification before any funds are released, and establish a reporting protocol if a client suspects a compromised email. The policy itself becomes a defense document if litigation arises — proof that the brokerage exercised reasonable care.

Training is the final pillar. The REC does not currently mandate a specific wire fraud CE course, but Bulletin 2025-04 strongly recommends annual training. Brokers who document annual training for all agents and staff create an audit trail that demonstrates systemic compliance rather than ad hoc reactions.

## How Verification Protocols Work in Practice

The verification process is straightforward but must be followed without exception. When an escrow officer sends wire instructions to a buyer, the buyer should call the escrow company at a phone number obtained independently — from the company's website or business card, not from the email containing the instructions. The buyer confirms account name, routing number, and account number verbally before initiating the transfer.

Agents play a supporting role here. They should remind buyers at least twice — once at contract signing and once within 48 hours of the scheduled wire — to follow verification steps. This is where understanding the [wire instructions verification process](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) in detail separates competent agents from those who assume escrow will handle everything.

> "No legitimate escrow company will ever change wire instructions by email alone. If you receive revised wiring details, stop and call." — Hawaii Real Estate Commission Bulletin 2025-04
