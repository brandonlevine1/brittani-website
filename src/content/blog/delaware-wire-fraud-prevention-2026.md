# Delaware Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: What Every Agent Must Do Before Closing

Wire fraud cost American real estate consumers over $145 million in 2023, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and Delaware is not immune. Understanding Delaware wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 is no longer optional knowledge for licensed agents — it is a direct professional obligation that affects your license, your liability exposure, and your clients' life savings. The state has tightened expectations around disclosure, verification, and broker oversight, and agents who treat these requirements as afterthoughts are playing a dangerous game.

Delaware's regulatory framework places wire fraud prevention squarely within the duties agents owe their clients under 24 Del. C. Chapter 29, the statute governing real estate licensee conduct. The [Delaware Real Estate Commission](https://dpr.delaware.gov/boards/realestate/) has made clear through guidance bulletins and continuing education mandates that wire fraud advisories are a baseline standard of care, not a courtesy.

## How Delaware Law Creates the Obligation

Delaware does not have a single standalone wire fraud prevention statute the way some states do. Instead, the obligation arises from the intersection of several existing legal duties. Under 24 Del. C. Section 2912, agents must act in good faith and with reasonable skill, care, and diligence on behalf of their clients — a standard that the Commission has interpreted to include warning clients about known transaction risks.

The Delaware Professional and Occupational Licensing Act, codified in Title 24, gives the Commission broad authority to discipline agents who fail to meet the standard of care. When wire fraud advisories became industry standard practice around 2020, Delaware effectively incorporated them into what constitutes "reasonable skill and care." Agents who skip the advisory are not just being careless — they are falling below the regulatory floor.

Title 6, Chapter 25 of the Delaware Code also creates potential exposure under the state's consumer fraud provisions. If a court determines that an agent's failure to warn contributed to a client's loss, the agent could face civil liability beyond what the Commission imposes. The financial exposure is real and personal.

## The Forms Delaware Agents Must Use

**The Delaware Association of REALTORS (DAR) Form 80, known as the Wire Fraud Advisory, is the standard disclosure form agents should include in every transaction file.** This form specifically warns buyers and sellers about email compromise schemes, spoofed wire instructions, and the irreversible nature of misdirected wire transfers. It requires signatures from all parties and should be executed at or before the earliest point when wire transfer discussions begin.

Form 80 is not a state-mandated form in the way that the Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition (Form 35) is required by statute. However, the Commission's interpretation of agent duties under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 makes its use — or the use of a substantially similar advisory — a practical necessity. Agents who rely on settlement attorneys or title companies to handle the advisory are making a mistake; the agent's independent duty to the client exists regardless of what the closing team does.

Some brokerages have developed proprietary wire fraud advisory forms that go beyond Form 80, adding callback verification procedures and specific email security instructions. If your brokerage has its own form, use it in addition to Form 80, not as a replacement — the DAR form is what auditors and the Commission will look for first.

## Delaware Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Common Mistakes Agents Make

Even agents who know the rules trip over the execution. Here are five specific errors that keep showing up in Commission complaints and brokerage audits.

**First, agents distribute the wire fraud advisory at the wrong time.** The advisory needs to be signed before any party receives wire instructions, not at the closing table when wires have already been sent. Presenting Form 80 at settlement is like handing someone a seatbelt after the crash. Best practice is to include it with the initial contract package.

Second, agents forward wire instructions via email without any verification protocol. The moment an agent emails wire instructions — even legitimate ones — they create an intercept opportunity. Wire instructions should only be communicated through encrypted portals or verified phone calls. If your settlement attorney sends wire details by email and you forward them to a client, you have just doubled the attack surface.

Third, agents fail to document that verbal callback verification occurred. Telling a buyer to "call and confirm the wiring instructions" is not enough. The file should contain a written note — dated and initialed — confirming that the buyer independently verified wire instructions by calling the settlement office at a number obtained from a reliable source, not from the email containing the instructions.

Fourth, agents assume the title company or settlement attorney has handled everything. Delaware is an attorney-closing state, and agents sometimes defer entirely to the closing attorney on wire procedures. The attorney has their own obligations, but the agent's duty to the client under 24 Del. C. Section 2912 is independent. If the attorney drops the ball, the agent's silence will not be a defense.

Fifth, agents neglect to update clients about evolving scam tactics. A wire fraud advisory signed three months before closing may not cover a new phishing method that emerged during the contract period. Agents should send at least one additional written reminder about wire safety in the week before closing, especially when large earnest money deposits or final settlement funds are being transferred.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Broker liability in Delaware runs through 24 Del. C. Section 2920, which holds brokers responsible for the supervision of their licensees. A broker who fails to implement wire fraud prevention protocols is not just risking individual agent discipline — the brokerage license itself is at stake.

| Broker Responsibility | Minimum Standard | Recommended Best Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Wire fraud advisory in every file | Signed Form 80 or equivalent before wire instructions shared | Form 80 signed with initial contract package |
| Agent training documentation | Annual CE credit in wire fraud awareness | Quarterly in-house training with sign-off sheets |
| Email security protocols | Encrypted email for wire instructions | Dedicated wire verification portal with MFA |
| File audit frequency | Reviewed at closing | Spot-checked at contract, midpoint, and closing |
| Callback verification records | Agent attestation in file | Written buyer confirmation with date and phone number used |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits specifically targeting wire fraud compliance, separate from general transaction reviews.** The Commission does not publish a specific audit cadence requirement, but enforcement actions under Section 2920 consistently cite inadequate supervision when a pattern of missing disclosures emerges across multiple files.

Training records matter as much as the training itself. If the Commission investigates a complaint and the broker cannot produce documentation showing that the offending agent received wire fraud training, the presumption of inadequate supervision becomes very difficult to overcome. Every training session, every policy update, and every email reminder about wire procedures should be archived.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

The consequences of ignoring wire fraud prevention requirements in Delaware break down into three categories: regulatory, civil, and transactional.

| Consequence Category | What Happens | Who Is Affected |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory discipline | License suspension, revocation, or fines up to $5,000 per violation under 24 Del. C. Section 2914 | Agent and supervising broker |
| Civil liability | Negligence claims from defrauded clients; potential liability for full wire loss | Agent, broker, and brokerage entity |
| Transactional fallout | Deal cancellation, delayed closing, or buyer walkaway if funds are compromised | All parties to the transaction |
| Errors & omissions claims | E&O insurer may deny coverage if agent failed to follow standard industry protocols | Agent and brokerage |

Section 2914 gives the Commission authority to impose fines, require additional education, or suspend or revoke a license for failure to meet the standard of care. In practice, first-time violations where no client loss occurred often result in mandatory additional continuing education and a formal reprimand. Repeat violations or cases involving actual client losses escalate quickly to suspension.

The civil exposure is often worse than the regulatory penalty. Delaware courts have recognized that real estate professionals owe a duty to warn clients of known risks. An agent who fails to deliver a wire fraud advisory and whose client subsequently loses $300,000 to a spoofed email could face a negligence judgment for the full amount. E&O carriers are increasingly scrutinizing whether agents followed wire fraud protocols before agreeing to cover claims, which means the agent could end up personally liable. ## Protecting Yourself and Your Clients in Practice

The agents who avoid wire fraud problems are the ones who build verification into their workflow rather than bolting it on at the end. Start by making Form 80 part of your standard contract package — right alongside the agency disclosure and the property condition report. Treat it with the same urgency you give to [meeting contingency deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Create a written protocol for how wire instructions are communicated in your transactions. That protocol should prohibit forwarding wire details by unencrypted email, require callback verification using a phone number obtained independently, and document every step in the transaction file. Share this protocol with your clients in writing at the start of the transaction so they know what to expect and what to be suspicious of.

**If you receive wire instructions by email — even from a known contact — do not act on them until you have verified them by phone.** This single habit stops the vast majority of wire fraud attempts. The few extra minutes it takes to pick up the phone and call the settlement attorney's office directly is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
