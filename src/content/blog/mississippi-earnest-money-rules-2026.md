## Understanding Mississippi Earnest Money & Escrow Laws 2026

If you hold a Mississippi real estate license, your handling of earnest money is one of the fastest paths to disciplinary action—or to a clean, dispute-free closing. Mississippi earnest money & escrow laws 2026 remain governed primarily by the Mississippi Real Estate Brokers License Act of 1954 (Miss. Code Ann. Sections 73-35-1 through 73-35-37) and rules promulgated by the Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC). Misunderstanding even one deposit deadline or disbursement rule can cost you your license, your brokerage's reputation, and thousands in legal fees.

This post breaks down the statutes, required forms, agent liabilities, and compliance traps every Mississippi licensee should know heading into 2026.

## Statutory Framework: Where the Rules Live

**Mississippi's escrow requirements are not optional guidelines—they are codified duties tied directly to your license.** Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21 enumerates the grounds on which MREC can suspend or revoke a license, and "failure to account for or remit any money coming into his possession which belongs to others" is explicitly listed. That single sentence has ended more Mississippi real estate careers than market downturns.

MREC Rule 3.2 further specifies that all earnest money, security deposits, and other trust funds must be deposited into a properly maintained trust (escrow) account at a recognized Mississippi depository. The rule demands that the account be designated as a "trust" or "escrow" account on the bank's records and that the broker's name appear on the account.

Section 73-35-21(l) adds another layer: commingling personal funds with escrow funds is an independent violation. A broker may keep only enough personal funds in the trust account to cover bank service charges—nothing more.

## Deposit Timing and Account Requirements

Timing is where many agents stumble. MREC does not prescribe a rigid statutory clock in the same way some states mandate 24- or 48-hour deposits. Instead, the standard is "reasonable time," which MREC enforcement actions have generally interpreted as no more than five business days from the broker's receipt of the funds. If your purchase agreement sets a tighter deadline, that contractual term controls.

| Requirement | Mississippi Rule | Typical Compliance Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit deadline | "Reasonable time" (MREC Rule 3.2) | Five business days from receipt |
| Account type | Trust or escrow, labeled as such | Separate from operating account |
| Depository | Mississippi bank, S&L, or credit union | Must be FDIC/NCUA insured |
| Commingling allowance | Bank service charges only | Usually under $100 |
| Record retention | Minimum 3 years | Digital or physical copies acceptable |

Mississippi Association of REALTORS (MAR) Form 601—the standard Residential Purchase Agreement—includes blanks for the earnest money amount, the holder's identity, and the deposit deadline. If you leave those blanks empty or ambiguous, you invite a dispute that MREC will not look kindly upon during an audit.

## Disbursement Rules and Dispute Procedures

**Releasing earnest money in Mississippi is never a unilateral decision by the holding broker.** Unless both parties sign a mutual release (MAR Form 611, Release of Earnest Money), the broker must either follow the contract's specific disbursement instructions or hold the funds until a court order resolves the dispute. Releasing funds to one party without proper authorization is a violation of Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21 and can trigger both disciplinary and civil proceedings.

When a transaction falls apart and the parties cannot agree, MREC's position is clear: the broker interpleads the funds with the appropriate court rather than picking a side. The cost of interpleader is modest compared to the exposure of a wrongful disbursement claim. Agents who skip this step because they "know who deserves the money" are gambling with their license.

Mississippi does not have a statutory escrow dispute resolution procedure like Florida's escrow disbursement order system. The practical path is: attempt a mutual release, and if that fails within a reasonable time (often 15–30 days), file the interpleader action in chancery court. Your brokerage's policy may set an even shorter internal deadline—check your office procedures manual.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make

Even experienced licensees trip over these recurring errors. If you recognize yourself in any of the following patterns, correct course now rather than after MREC opens an investigation.

**First: treating earnest money as the agent's money.** Agents sometimes deposit checks into their personal accounts or hold physical checks in a desk drawer for weeks. Both actions violate Rule 3.2. The funds belong to the parties—not to you, not to your brokerage—until properly disbursed.

Second: failing to document changes to the escrow holder. When parties agree mid-transaction to move funds from the listing broker's trust account to a title company's escrow, that change must be memorialized in writing. A verbal agreement leaves no audit trail, and MREC auditors will treat the absence of documentation as a red flag.

Third: releasing earnest money on only one party's instruction after a contract falls through. Even if the buyer clearly defaulted, the [contract's termination provisions](/blog/where-deals-break-down) dictate the process. A buyer's agent calling to say "just release it to my client" is not a signed mutual release.

Fourth: not reconciling the trust account monthly. MREC Rule 3.2 requires that the broker reconcile the trust account at least monthly and maintain records showing the balance attributable to each transaction. Agents who manage their own escrow without regular reconciliation are one bookkeeping error away from an unintentional shortage—which MREC treats identically to intentional misappropriation during an audit.

Fifth: ignoring the MREC's annual trust account certification. Brokers must certify compliance annually on their license renewal application. Submitting a false certification is a separate violation that compounds the underlying escrow issue.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry vicarious liability for their agents' escrow handling under Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21. A broker who pleads ignorance when an agent mishandles funds will not find sympathy at MREC. The Commission's enforcement history shows that brokers are routinely disciplined alongside—or instead of—the individual agent when supervisory failures are evident.

| Broker Audit Item | Frequency | Source of Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account bank reconciliation | Monthly | MREC Rule 3.2 |
| Transaction-level ledger review | Per closing or quarterly | MREC Rule 3.2 |
| Verification of deposit timing | Per transaction | MREC Rule 3.2 / contract terms |
| Annual trust account certification | At renewal | MREC renewal application |
| Written escrow policy acknowledgment | At agent onboarding | Best practice / E&O insurer requirement |

**Brokers should require every agent to submit a copy of the earnest money receipt within 48 hours of contract execution.** This gives the office time to confirm the deposit hits the trust account within the five-business-day window and flags any agent who is holding checks. A simple internal tracking system—or an automated [deadline-management workflow](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines)—removes the human memory variable from compliance.

E&O insurance carriers in Mississippi increasingly require brokers to demonstrate documented escrow procedures. If your brokerage has no written policy, your insurer may deny coverage on a claim involving mishandled funds. That gap can turn a $5,000 earnest money dispute into a six-figure personal liability event for the broker.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

MREC's enforcement powers include license suspension, license revocation, fines up to $5,000 per violation, and mandatory restitution. Under Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21, the Commission may also require the respondent to pay investigation costs. Disciplinary orders are public records, searchable on the [MREC website](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/), and they follow you permanently.

Civil liability compounds the regulatory exposure. A buyer who loses earnest money due to an agent's unauthorized release can sue for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and potentially fraud. Mississippi courts have awarded actual damages plus attorney's fees in multiple reported cases involving escrow mishandling by licensees.

Criminal prosecution is rare but not impossible. Miss. Code Ann. Section 97-23-19 (embezzlement by fiduciary) applies when an agent intentionally converts trust funds. Convictions carry imprisonment of up to 10 years and fines up to $25,000, depending on the amount involved.

## Mississippi-Specific Forms You Must Know

The Mississippi Association of REALTORS publishes the standardized forms most agents use statewide. For earnest money and escrow purposes, three forms matter most.

MAR Form 601 (Residential Purchase Agreement) includes the earnest money provisions in Section 3. This is where the amount, holder, deposit deadline, and default remedy are specified. Leaving any field blank invites a dispute that [breaks deals down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at the worst possible moment.

MAR Form 611 (Release of Earnest Money) is the mutual release both parties must sign before a broker can disburse contested funds. Agents should keep pre-filled copies accessible in every transaction file so that releases can be executed quickly when a deal collapses.

MAR Form 621 (Amendment to Contract) is used when parties agree to modify the earnest money amount, change the escrow holder, or extend a deposit deadline mid-transaction. Every such change must be in writing—verbal modifications to earnest money terms are unenforceable and create audit exposure.
