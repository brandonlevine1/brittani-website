## Understanding the Arkansas FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every year, Arkansas agents lose time, money, and occasionally entire deals because someone at the table misunderstands the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act. The Arkansas firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 have not changed dramatically from the federal framework, but the state-level procedural details—particularly around the Arkansas Real Estate Commission (AREC) and the closing practices governed by Arkansas Code Annotated Title 18—create unique compliance pressure for licensees who list or sell property involving non-U.S. persons.

FIRPTA is not optional. It is not something the title company "handles" while agents look the other way. Under 26 U.S.C. Section 1445, the buyer is the withholding agent, and the buyer's representative has a fiduciary interest in making sure the correct amount reaches the IRS. When a listing agent fails to identify a foreign seller or a buyer's agent fails to advise their client about withholding obligations, the fallout lands on everyone.

## Who Qualifies as a "Foreign Person" and Why Arkansas Agents Get Confused

**A "foreign person" under FIRPTA includes nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, foreign partnerships, foreign trusts, and foreign estates.** Green card holders are not foreign persons—they are treated as U.S. residents for tax purposes. The confusion arises in Arkansas when agents encounter sellers who hold valid U.S. visas but are not permanent residents, or sellers who are Canadian snowbirds owning vacation property in places like Bella Vista or Hot Springs Village.

Arkansas has no separate state-level withholding statute for foreign sellers. Unlike states such as California (with its Form 593) or Hawaii (with HARPTA), Arkansas relies entirely on the federal FIRPTA framework. That means the forms, deadlines, and withholding percentages are IRS-governed, but the agent's compliance duty is still enforced by AREC under the general competency and fiduciary standards in Arkansas Administrative Code 130.00.1 through 130.00.7.

The practical impact: an Arkansas agent cannot claim ignorance of FIRPTA because the state has no "additional" statute layered on top. The federal obligation is your obligation as a licensed professional advising a client on a real estate transaction.

## Withholding Rates, Forms, and Deadlines That Agents Must Track

The current withholding rate under FIRPTA is 15% of the gross sales price for dispositions where the amount realized exceeds $300,000. A reduced rate of 10% applies when the property will be used by the buyer as a residence and the amount realized is between $300,001 and $1,000,000. If the buyer will use the property as a residence and the price is $300,000 or less, the withholding is zero.

| Scenario | Amount Realized | Buyer's Intended Use | Withholding Rate |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Standard sale | Over $1,000,000 | Any | 15% |
| Mid-range residential | $300,001–$1,000,000 | Buyer's residence | 10% |
| Lower-priced residential | $300,000 or less | Buyer's residence | 0% |
| Investment property | Any amount | Not buyer's residence | 15% |
| Seller obtains withholding certificate | Any | Any | Reduced per IRS approval |

The key IRS forms involved are IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and IRS Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both must be filed within 20 days of closing. If the seller wants a reduced withholding, IRS Form 8288-B must be submitted before or at closing—and agents should know that processing typically takes 90 days or longer.

The affidavit of non-foreign status, often called a "FIRPTA affidavit" or "seller's certification," is not a numbered IRS form but rather a document prepared pursuant to [Treasury Regulation 1.1445-2(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subject-group-ECFRb467872627553f1/section-1.1445-2). Every seller in an Arkansas transaction should be presented with this affidavit. If the seller refuses to sign or cannot truthfully certify U.S. person status, withholding applies.

## What Happens When an Agent Fails to Comply

**The IRS does not distinguish between intentional evasion and accidental oversight.** If the required 15% withholding is not remitted and the foreign seller leaves the country without paying capital gains tax, the IRS pursues the buyer for the full withholding amount, plus interest and penalties under 26 U.S.C. Section 1461.

From a licensing perspective, AREC can take disciplinary action against agents who demonstrate incompetence in handling material transaction requirements. Under Arkansas Code Annotated 17-42-311, the Commission may suspend or revoke a license for "failure to exercise reasonable skill and care" or for "conduct that constitutes improper, fraudulent, or dishonest dealing." A FIRPTA violation—particularly one that exposes a buyer-client to IRS penalties—fits squarely within these grounds.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. If a buyer pays $450,000 for a property and the listing agent knew (or should have known) the seller was a nonresident alien but failed to disclose this to the closing agent, the buyer could pursue a negligence claim for the $67,500 withholding amount they now owe the IRS. Deal cancellation is also common: buyers who discover mid-transaction that a foreign seller has not applied for a withholding certificate and refuses to accept a reduced net proceed will often terminate under inspection or financing contingencies rather than absorb the compliance risk.

This is one of many areas [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) not because of property condition or financing, but because of paperwork failures that could have been caught weeks earlier.

## Common Mistakes Arkansas Agents Make With FIRPTA

The first mistake is assuming the title company will catch everything. Arkansas closings are typically handled by title companies or attorneys, and while most will request a FIRPTA affidavit, they are not your compliance backstop. If the listing agent knows the seller lives in another country and never mentions it until closing day, the title company's last-minute scramble may delay or kill the deal.

The second mistake is confusing visa status with tax residency. An agent in Fayetteville listing a home for a University of Arkansas professor on an H-1B visa might assume the seller is a U.S. person. But if that professor has not met the substantial presence test under IRC Section 7701(b), they may still be classified as a nonresident alien for FIRPTA purposes. The affidavit is the seller's sworn certification—not the agent's guess.

**The third mistake is failing to calendar the 20-day filing deadline for Forms 8288 and 8288-A.** This is a hard deadline from the date of closing, and [missing deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in real estate transactions carries consequences that compound fast. If the forms are late, the IRS assesses penalties against the withholding agent—which, again, is technically the buyer.

The fourth mistake is not advising foreign sellers to file Form 8288-B early enough. A seller who waits until two weeks before closing to request a reduced withholding will not receive their withholding certificate in time. The agent should raise this at listing appointment, not at the final walkthrough.

The fifth mistake is overlooking entity sellers. A domestic LLC owned by a foreign person is still a U.S. entity, and FIRPTA withholding does not apply to it in the same way—unless the LLC is disregarded for tax purposes. Agents listing properties held by LLCs must ask about the membership structure and recommend that the seller consult a tax professional.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents in Arkansas have a direct responsibility under AREC Administrative Rule 130.00.2 to ensure that transactions under their brokerage comply with applicable law. This includes FIRPTA compliance, even if the broker never meets the foreign seller personally.

| Broker Audit Point | What to Verify | Frequency |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| FIRPTA affidavit in file | Signed seller certification or documented withholding | Every transaction |
| Foreign seller identification | Agent notes on seller residency/citizenship at listing | At listing intake |
| Form 8288-B filing timeline | Evidence of early filing if seller seeks reduced withholding | When applicable |
| Closing agent coordination | Written confirmation that withholding was remitted | Post-closing review |
| Agent training documentation | Annual FIRPTA training or CE credit | Annually |

**Brokers should build FIRPTA into their transaction checklist at the listing stage, not the closing stage.** A simple question on the listing intake form—"Is the seller a U.S. citizen or permanent resident?"—catches 90% of potential issues before they become emergencies. The [checklist-based approach](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) prevents reliance on individual agent memory, which fails under the pressure of multiple concurrent transactions.

If a broker discovers after closing that withholding was not properly handled, the brokerage's errors and omissions insurance may not cover the resulting IRS liability—particularly if the failure stemmed from a known lack of internal controls. Document your processes. Train your agents. Audit your files.

## Practical Steps for Arkansas Agents Representing Foreign Sellers

Start at the listing appointment. Ask directly about citizenship and tax residency status. If the seller is a foreign person, discuss the 15% withholding immediately—before the property hits the MLS. Many foreign sellers are surprised by the withholding amount and need time to decide whether to pursue a reduced withholding certificate via Form 8288-B.

Coordinate with the closing agent within the first week of a ratified contract. Confirm that the title company or attorney handling the closing understands a foreign seller is involved and will prepare the appropriate IRS forms. Send written communication—email is fine—documenting this coordination. Your transaction file should contain evidence that you flagged the FIRPTA issue and confirmed the closing agent's awareness.

For agents representing the buyer side, your obligation is equally clear. Your buyer-client bears the withholding liability. Advise them in writing that if the seller is a foreign person, funds will be withheld from the seller's proceeds and remitted to the IRS on the buyer's behalf. If the seller refuses to cooperate with withholding, your buyer should seriously consider whether to proceed with the transaction.
