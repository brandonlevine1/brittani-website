## Understanding Mississippi FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

Every Mississippi agent who lists property for a foreign national faces a federal compliance obligation that can blow up a closing faster than a title defect. The Mississippi firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 require agents to understand both the federal Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (IRC Section 1445) and Mississippi's own nonresident withholding statute. Getting either wrong exposes you, your brokerage, and the buyer to joint and several liability with the IRS.

FIRPTA mandates that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sales price and remit it to the IRS. Mississippi layers an additional 5% state withholding for nonresident sellers under Miss. Code Ann. Section 27-7-308. That means a foreign seller in Mississippi could see 20% of the gross sales price held back at closing before any exemptions or reductions apply.

## Federal Withholding Rates and Exemptions That Apply in Mississippi

**The standard FIRPTA withholding rate is 15% of the amount realized, but two buyer-use exemptions can reduce or eliminate it.** If the buyer signs an affidavit stating they will use the property as a personal residence and the sales price does not exceed $300,000, the withholding drops to zero. For residence-use purchases priced between $300,001 and $1,000,000, the rate falls to 10%.

These exemptions only apply when the buyer is an individual who will personally reside in the property for at least 50% of the days it is used during each of the first two 12-month periods after closing. Investment buyers, LLCs purchasing rentals, and corporate purchasers cannot trigger the residence-use exemption regardless of price.

| Scenario | Sales Price Range | Buyer Use | Federal Rate |
|----------|------------------|-----------|--------------|
| Residence use | $300,000 or less | Personal | 0% |
| Residence use | $300,001–$1,000,000 | Personal | 10% |
| Residence use | Over $1,000,000 | Personal | 15% |
| Non-residence | Any amount | Investment/Business | 15% |

Foreign sellers who believe the withholding exceeds their actual tax liability can apply for a Withholding Certificate using IRS Form 8288-B before or after closing. The IRS typically processes these in 90 days, but the transaction cannot wait—funds are held at closing regardless.

## Mississippi's Separate State Withholding Under Section 27-7-308

Mississippi does not piggyback on federal FIRPTA. The Mississippi Department of Revenue enforces its own nonresident withholding requirement independently. Under Miss. Code Ann. Section 27-7-308, any buyer purchasing real property from a nonresident seller (foreign or domestic out-of-state) must withhold 5% of the gross sales price for Mississippi income tax purposes.

The closing attorney or title company files Mississippi Form 89-350 (Nonresident Income Tax Withholding) and remits the withheld amount to the Mississippi Department of Revenue within 30 days of closing. The seller receives credit for this withholding when filing their Mississippi nonresident income tax return.

**A foreign seller in Mississippi therefore faces a combined 20% withholding on the gross sales price when both federal and state obligations apply—not the net proceeds, but the full contract price.** Agents must communicate this reality during listing presentations with foreign nationals. A seller expecting $400,000 in proceeds on a $500,000 sale will actually see $100,000 withheld before any mortgage payoff, commissions, or other closing costs.

## Forms and Filing Deadlines Agents Must Track

The paperwork burden falls primarily on the buyer as the "withholding agent," but Mississippi closing attorneys handle the mechanical filing. Agents need to know [which forms trigger which deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) because missed deadlines create penalties that can claw back into the transaction.

| Form | Purpose | Filed With | Deadline |
|------|---------|-----------|----------|
| IRS Form 8288 | Withholding tax return | IRS | 20 days after closing |
| IRS Form 8288-A | Statement to foreign seller | IRS (copy to seller) | 20 days after closing |
| IRS Form 8288-B | Application for reduced withholding | IRS | Before or after closing |
| MS Form 89-350 | State nonresident withholding | MS Dept. of Revenue | 30 days after closing |
| IRS Form W-7 | ITIN application (if seller lacks TIN) | IRS | With Form 8288 |

The 20-day federal deadline is measured from the date of transfer, which in Mississippi is typically the recording date of the deed. If the closing attorney misses this window, the IRS assesses penalties plus interest on the buyer—who may then pursue the listing agent or brokerage for contribution if they failed to disclose the seller's foreign status.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

**Agent liability under FIRPTA is not theoretical—it is codified.** Under IRC Section 1445(d), any agent who knew or had reason to know that the seller was a foreign person and failed to notify the buyer can be held liable for the withholding amount. The IRS interprets "agent" broadly to include listing agents, buyer's agents, and their brokerages.

If the required 15% is never withheld, the IRS can assess the full amount against the buyer. The buyer can then seek indemnification from the agent who failed to flag the issue. Mississippi courts have recognized contribution claims in negligence actions tied to closing failures, and the Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC) treats FIRPTA compliance failures as potential violations of Miss. Code Ann. Section 73-35-21(1)(n), which covers incompetence or negligent conduct.

The practical fallout looks like this: deal cancellation when a buyer discovers last-minute that $75,000 must come out of proceeds; MREC complaints leading to license suspension; E&O claims that spike your premiums for years; and IRS notices that name your brokerage as a responsible party.

## Common Mistakes Mississippi Agents Make With FIRPTA

The first common mistake is assuming the closing attorney will handle everything without any input from the agent. Mississippi is an attorney-close state, which creates a false sense of security. The attorney handles filing, but the agent has an independent duty to identify foreign sellers and inform all parties. If you list a property and know the seller holds a Canadian passport, you cannot simply stay silent and hope the attorney catches it.

The second mistake is confusing residency with citizenship. A foreign national who lives in Mississippi full-time, holds a green card, and files U.S. taxes is generally not subject to FIRPTA. But a Canadian snowbird who owns a vacation home in Ocean Springs and files no U.S. returns likely qualifies as a "foreign person." Agents routinely conflate physical presence with tax residency, and the distinction matters enormously.

The third mistake is failing to request a FIRPTA affidavit (also called a non-foreign affidavit or [Certificate of Non-Foreign Status](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026)) from the seller early in the transaction. Under Treasury Regulation 1.1445-2(b)(2), if the buyer receives a properly executed affidavit stating the seller is not a foreign person, the buyer is relieved of withholding obligations. Waiting until closing day to discover the seller cannot sign this affidavit is a preventable disaster.

The fourth mistake is miscalculating the withholding base. FIRPTA withholding applies to the "amount realized," which is the full contract price—not the net after commissions, repairs, or mortgage payoffs. An agent who tells a foreign seller "you'll only owe 15% of your profit" has created a material misrepresentation that [breaks down the deal](/blog/where-deals-break-down) at the closing table.

The fifth mistake is ignoring Mississippi's separate 5% state withholding. Agents who study FIRPTA but forget about Miss. Code Ann. Section 27-7-308 leave another 5% gap in the seller's net sheet. Both withholdings must appear on the preliminary closing disclosure.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents who work with foreign sellers carry vicarious liability under both IRC Section 1445 and MREC regulations. The broker's compliance obligation begins at listing intake—not at closing.

Every brokerage operating in Mississippi should maintain a written FIRPTA policy that requires agents to ask sellers about citizenship and tax residency status during the listing agreement phase. This question should appear on the seller intake form alongside standard disclosure questions. The MREC does not mandate a specific form for this purpose, but brokers who build it into their [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) process create an auditable paper trail.

Brokers should also verify that the agent has communicated the withholding obligation in writing before the property goes under contract. A simple email confirming "your transaction is subject to 15% federal and 5% state withholding at closing" gives the brokerage defensible documentation if a complaint arises later.

Finally, brokers must confirm that the closing attorney has received notice of the seller's foreign status no later than the title order date. Waiting until the closing package is assembled invites last-minute chaos. The Mississippi Department of Revenue publishes guidance on Form 89-350 filing procedures at [dor.ms.gov](https://www.dor.ms.gov), and brokers should ensure their agents reference this resource when questions arise about state withholding calculations.
