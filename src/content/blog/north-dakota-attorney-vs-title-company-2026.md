## North Dakota Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026

The question of North Dakota attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 trips up agents more than almost any other compliance issue in the state. North Dakota is not an attorney-mandatory state, which means licensed title companies can — and regularly do — handle the entire settlement process. But that simple fact obscures a web of statutes, licensing requirements, and unauthorized-practice-of-law traps that agents must understand before they walk into any closing room.

Unlike neighboring Minnesota, where attorneys play a more prominent role in residential closings, North Dakota leaves the choice largely to the parties involved. The North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC) expects agents to know the boundaries of their own authority, especially when questions about legal documents arise at the table. Getting this wrong does not just create awkward moments — it creates liability exposure that can end a career.

## The Legal Framework: What North Dakota Statutes Actually Say

**North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 26.1-20 governs the title insurance industry in the state and establishes who may issue title insurance policies, conduct title searches, and perform closings.** Title insurance companies must be licensed by the North Dakota Insurance Department, and their agents must hold individual licenses as well. Abstractors, separately licensed under NDCC Chapter 43-01, may also prepare title opinions and facilitate closings when working alongside a title insurer.

Attorneys admitted to the North Dakota bar have an inherent right to conduct closings and prepare real estate documents. Under NDCC Section 27-11-01, the "practice of law" is broadly defined, and any non-attorney who drafts legal instruments, renders legal opinions, or advises parties on their legal rights may be prosecuted for unauthorized practice. This statute is not theoretical — the [North Dakota Supreme Court](https://www.ndcourts.gov/) has disciplined non-lawyers for crossing this line in real estate transactions.

The practical result is a two-track system. Title companies handle the mechanical settlement work — ordering title commitments, preparing closing disclosures, collecting funds, recording documents. Attorneys handle legal interpretation, document drafting, and dispute resolution. Agents who blur these roles put themselves and their brokers at risk.

## Who Handles What at the Closing Table

Understanding the division of labor keeps agents out of trouble. The table below breaks down which tasks belong to which party in a standard North Dakota residential closing.

| Task | Title Company | Attorney | Agent |
|------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Issue title commitment/policy | Yes | No (unless also a title agent) | No |
| Prepare deed or mortgage documents | No | Yes | No |
| Explain legal effect of documents | No | Yes | No |
| Prepare ALTA settlement statement | Yes | Yes (if conducting closing) | No |
| Hold earnest money in escrow | Yes | Yes | Only if broker trust account is used |
| Advise on title defects or liens | Yes (factual disclosure) | Yes (legal advice) | No |
| Record documents at county recorder | Yes | Yes | No |
| Recommend specific contract terms | No | Yes (for their client) | Within NDREC guidelines only |

Agents sometimes assume that because they sit at the closing table, they share authority with the title officer. They do not. NDREC administrative rules make clear that licensees may fill in blanks on pre-approved forms — specifically the North Dakota Association of Realtors (NDAR) standard forms — but may not draft custom clauses, modify legal descriptions without direction, or interpret contract language for a client. The approved forms include the NDAR Purchase Agreement (Form 30-07) and the NDAR Addendum to Purchase Agreement (Form 30-14), among others.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in North Dakota Closings

**Three to five recurring errors account for the majority of NDREC complaints related to closing procedures.** Each one is preventable, and each one can trigger consequences ranging from license suspension to personal civil liability.

The first mistake is explaining legal documents to clients. When a buyer asks, "What does this easement mean for my property?" and the agent answers with a legal interpretation, the agent has just practiced law. The correct response is to direct the question to the closing attorney or to suggest the buyer retain independent counsel. Agents do not lose credibility by saying, "That question is best answered by an attorney." They lose credibility — and their license — by guessing.

The second mistake is selecting or steering clients toward a specific title company in exchange for referrals or business favors. RESPA Section 8 prohibits kickbacks and unearned fees, and the NDREC has adopted this standard into its own enforcement framework. An agent who consistently directs all business to one title company without disclosure, or who receives any compensation for the referral, risks federal and state penalties. This issue overlaps significantly with [understanding where deals commonly break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) because undisclosed affiliated business arrangements create trust failures that can unravel transactions.

The third mistake is handling earnest money improperly at or near closing. Under NDCC Section 43-23-11, all earnest money must be deposited into a trust account within three business days of acceptance. Agents who hold checks "until after the inspection" or who deposit funds into a personal account violate state law. Brokers bear primary responsibility for trust account compliance, but individual agents face discipline too.

The fourth mistake is failing to verify closing figures independently. Agents who never review the ALTA settlement statement before closing sometimes discover errors only after funding — when correcting them requires new signatures, re-recording, or even deal rescission. A simple pre-closing audit of the numbers against the contract terms catches most of these problems. Agents who rely on [memory instead of structured checklists](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) are the ones most likely to miss discrepancies.

The fifth mistake is notarizing documents without proper authority. North Dakota requires notaries public to hold a current commission under NDCC Chapter 44-06. An agent whose notary commission has lapsed — or who notarizes a document for a signer not physically present — commits a violation that can void the document and expose the agent to liability.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers in North Dakota carry direct supervisory liability under NDCC Section 43-23-11.1 and NDREC administrative rules. When an agent makes a closing-related error, the commission's first question is whether the broker had systems in place to prevent it.

**Brokers should audit trust account reconciliations monthly, at minimum.** The NDREC requires that trust accounts be reconciled and that records be available for inspection. A broker who cannot produce a current reconciliation during an audit faces potential license suspension. This is not a theoretical risk — NDREC conducts routine and complaint-triggered audits and has publicly disciplined brokers for deficient records.

Beyond trust accounts, brokers need a written policy specifying which forms agents may use and under what circumstances agents should recommend legal counsel. The policy should explicitly prohibit agents from drafting custom contract language, interpreting legal documents, or accepting compensation from title companies or attorneys for referrals. Annual training on these policies — documented with sign-off sheets — creates an audit trail that protects the brokerage if a complaint arises.

Brokers should also verify that every agent in the office who notarizes documents holds a current North Dakota notary commission. A spreadsheet tracking commission expiration dates is a minimal step that prevents a surprisingly common problem. The North Dakota Secretary of State's office maintains the notary database and can confirm active status.

North Dakota's non-mandatory approach gives agents and their clients flexibility, but it also places a higher burden on agents to know when a situation requires legal counsel. In an attorney-mandatory state, the attorney is always there to catch legal issues. In North Dakota, no one may be watching unless the agent flags the need.

## When an Attorney Is Still the Right Call

Not every North Dakota closing requires an attorney, but some demand one. Any transaction involving boundary disputes, mineral rights complications, or estate sales should involve legal counsel on at least one side. Mineral rights in North Dakota are particularly complex because the state has extensive oil and gas extraction, and severed mineral estates are common in western counties.

**If a title commitment reveals exceptions, liens, or encumbrances that the parties do not understand, an attorney should review those items before closing.** A title company can disclose the existence of a defect, but interpreting its legal impact on the buyer's intended use crosses into legal advice. Agents who attempt to bridge that gap — even with good intentions — risk an unauthorized practice complaint.

Short sales, foreclosure purchases, and transactions involving trusts or LLCs also warrant attorney involvement. The documentation in these deals often includes custom provisions that fall outside NDAR standard forms. Attempting to handle them with fill-in-the-blank templates is a recipe for post-closing disputes and potential [missed deadlines that kill deals](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).
