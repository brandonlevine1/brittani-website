## Why Oregon Teams & Supervision Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The phrase Oregon teams & supervision rules 2026 should be on every whiteboard in every brokerage from Portland to Bend. The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) has sharpened enforcement around team advertising, supervisory responsibilities, and consumer disclosure requirements over the past two years, and the consequences of non-compliance have teeth. Agents who joined or formed teams without reading ORS Chapter 696 and OAR Division 863 are running on borrowed time.

Oregon's regulatory framework treats a "team" differently than a solo agent operating under a broker. A team is not a separate legal entity—it functions within the brokerage and under the principal broker's license. That distinction matters because every document, advertisement, and client interaction must reflect the brokerage relationship first, team branding second.

Failing to understand this hierarchy is not just an academic problem. It results in fines, voided transactions, and license actions that follow agents for years.

## How Oregon Defines a Real Estate Team

**Oregon Administrative Rule 863-015-0260 defines a team as two or more licensees affiliated with the same registered business who market themselves collectively under a team name.** The team name must be registered with OREA as a "registered business name" on the principal broker's license file. Without that registration, the team name is illegal to use in any consumer-facing context.

The statute does not require a team to be a separate LLC or corporation. In fact, OREA discourages structures that obscure the consumer's understanding of who holds supervisory authority. The principal broker of the brokerage—not the team leader—remains the supervising broker under ORS 696.020(1).

Team leaders who hold a principal broker license can supervise their own team members, but only if they are the designated principal broker for the brokerage itself. A broker-level licensee leading a team within another principal broker's office does not gain supervisory authority simply by having a team.

## Advertising and Disclosure Rules for Oregon Teams

OAR 863-015-0275 governs how teams may advertise. Every advertisement, including social media posts, yard signs, and print mailers, must include the licensed brokerage name in a manner that is "at least as prominent" as the team name. This is the rule most frequently violated, and OREA has issued civil penalties ranging from $1,000 to $5,000 per offense since early 2025.

The required disclosure form in Oregon is the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet, which must be presented at first substantive contact. Team members must identify their individual license status (broker or principal broker) and clarify that the team operates under the supervising brokerage. OREA Form 520.009 remains the standard Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet template agents should reference.

| Requirement | Solo Agent | Team Member |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage name on advertising | Required | Required (at least as prominent as team name) |
| Registered business name filing | Not applicable | Required under OAR 863-015-0260 |
| Supervisory broker identified | Principal broker | Principal broker (not team leader unless also PB) |
| Initial Agency Disclosure timing | First substantive contact | First substantive contact |
| Liability for advertising violations | Agent + broker | Agent + team leader + broker |

The layered liability structure means that when a team member violates advertising rules, the Oregon Real Estate Agency may pursue action against the individual, the team leader, and the principal broker simultaneously.

## Supervision Standards Under ORS 696

**ORS 696.805 places the duty of supervision squarely on the principal broker, requiring "reasonable supervision" of all licensees associated with the brokerage.** The statute does not define "reasonable" with a bright-line test, but OREA enforcement actions make clear that passive oversight is insufficient.

Reasonable supervision includes maintaining systems to review transaction files, ensuring advertising compliance, confirming that agency disclosures are delivered timely, and verifying that team members do not operate outside the scope of their license level. A broker-level licensee in Oregon may write offers and conduct negotiations. An activity-licensed agent (formerly "salesperson") must perform all licensed activities under direct supervision.

OREA's [guidance documents](https://www.oregon.gov/rea) specify that principal brokers must have written policies addressing team operations. These policies should cover file retention, communication protocols, complaint handling, and the chain of command when the principal broker is unavailable.

The failure to maintain written policies does not carry a standalone penalty, but it becomes devastating evidence during disciplinary hearings. OREA investigators routinely request policy manuals, and their absence supports findings of inadequate supervision.

## Common Mistakes Oregon Teams Make

**Three to five errors account for the vast majority of OREA enforcement actions against teams, and most are entirely preventable.**

The first and most common error is advertising the team name without the brokerage name or displaying the brokerage name in significantly smaller font. OREA auditors regularly screen social media accounts and websites, and agents are often surprised to receive violation notices for Instagram posts they assumed were informal.

The second mistake is failing to register the team name as a registered business name with OREA. Agents form teams, design logos, order signs, and begin marketing before filing the paperwork. Every day of operation without registration is a separate violation under OAR 863-015-0260.

Third, team leaders frequently assume supervisory authority they do not legally hold. Unless a team leader is the designated principal broker of the brokerage, they cannot sign off on trust account reconciliations, approve disclosure forms on behalf of the brokerage, or handle consumer complaints as the responsible party. Agents who want to understand why these administrative gaps cause deals to collapse should read about [where deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) in the transaction process.

Fourth, teams often share transaction files informally—passing documents via text message or personal email—without routing files through the brokerage's official document management system. This creates gaps in the principal broker's ability to supervise, and it violates OAR 863-015-0135's record-keeping requirements.

Fifth, teams neglect to update OREA when members join or leave. Changes in team composition require updated registered business name filings, and the departing agent's name must be removed from all active advertising within a reasonable time frame.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers who oversee one or more teams should build their audit protocols around four pillars: advertising compliance, file integrity, disclosure timing, and trust account procedures.

| Audit Category | What to Check | Frequency Recommended | Governing Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising compliance | All team social media, websites, signs, and print materials for brokerage name prominence | Monthly | OAR 863-015-0275 |
| File integrity | Transaction files for completeness, proper signatures, and agency disclosures | Quarterly (minimum) | OAR 863-015-0135 |
| Disclosure timing | Confirmation that Initial Agency Disclosure was delivered at first substantive contact | Per transaction | ORS 696.805 |
| Trust account procedures | Earnest money deposits, timely remittance, reconciliation records | Monthly | ORS 696.241 |

Brokers should not rely on team leaders to self-police. The entire point of ORS 696.805's supervisory standard is that responsibility cannot be delegated away from the principal broker. Even when a team leader performs daily oversight, the principal broker must verify that oversight is actually happening.

A practical approach many Oregon brokerages adopt is requiring all team advertising to pass through a compliance review before publication. This single step eliminates the most common violation category entirely. For brokers managing multiple teams, automated [pipeline review systems](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) help track file completeness without requiring manual checks on every document.

## Penalties and Enforcement Actions

OREA's enforcement arm has broad authority under ORS 696.301 to impose civil penalties, suspend licenses, revoke licenses, and require additional education. For team-related violations, the penalty structure escalates quickly.

A first advertising violation typically results in a warning letter or a civil penalty of $1,000 to $2,000. Repeated violations or willful non-compliance can reach $5,000 per instance. Supervision failures by a principal broker—especially those resulting in consumer harm—can trigger license suspension and mandatory restitution.

Beyond regulatory penalties, agents face civil liability when team compliance failures cause transaction harm. If a buyer's agent on a team fails to deliver the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet and the buyer later claims they misunderstood the representation structure, the resulting lawsuit names the agent, the team leader, the principal broker, and potentially the brokerage's E&O carrier. Understanding [why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) helps illustrate how small oversights create outsized legal exposure.

Deal cancellation is another real consequence. Oregon buyers who discover material disclosure violations within the rescission period may terminate purchase agreements. Sellers who learn their listing was marketed under an unregistered team name may terminate listing agreements for cause.

## Building a Compliant Team Structure in 2026

Start with the registration. File the registered business name with OREA before printing a single business card. Confirm that the brokerage's principal broker has signed off on the team name and understands their supervisory obligations.

Next, establish a written team operations manual that addresses advertising approval workflows, file submission deadlines, agency disclosure delivery protocols, and the procedure for adding or removing team members. This document protects the team leader and the principal broker during any future OREA inquiry.

Finally, invest in systems that create audit trails automatically.
