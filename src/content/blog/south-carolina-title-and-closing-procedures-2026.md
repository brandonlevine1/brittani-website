## Understanding South Carolina Title & Closing Procedures 2026

South Carolina stands apart from most states in one fundamental way: attorneys run closings, not title companies. If you hold an active license in this state, understanding **South Carolina title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional—it directly determines whether your transactions close cleanly or collapse under compliance failures. The South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC) and the South Carolina Supreme Court both enforce strict boundaries around what agents can and cannot do during the closing process.

The state's Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL) rules, codified under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-5-310, make it a criminal misdemeanor for anyone other than a licensed attorney to conduct a real estate closing. This means preparing closing documents, disbursing funds, or even explaining title commitment exceptions to a buyer without attorney involvement can expose you to prosecution.

Agents who operated in other states before moving to South Carolina often stumble here. The attorney-supervised closing model affects everything from how you schedule your pipeline to how you communicate with clients about what to expect at the table.

## The Attorney-Supervised Closing Model

**Every residential real estate closing in South Carolina must be conducted under the direct supervision of a South Carolina-licensed attorney.** This requirement stems from a series of South Carolina Supreme Court opinions, most notably the Doe v. McMaster ruling and subsequent UPL opinions, which defined real estate closings as the practice of law.

The closing attorney performs title examination, prepares the closing disclosure, handles escrow and trust account disbursements, records the deed and mortgage at the county Register of Deeds office, and issues the title insurance policy as an agent of a title insurance underwriter. Your role as the real estate agent ends at the closing table—you do not prepare documents, handle funds, or issue title commitments.

This model differs significantly from states like Wisconsin or Wyoming where title companies independently conduct closings. If you are working with out-of-state buyers, you will need to explain this distinction early and often so they understand why an attorney fee appears on their closing disclosure.

## Key Statutes and Regulatory Bodies Agents Must Know

The regulatory framework governing South Carolina closings involves multiple bodies. The [South Carolina Real Estate Commission](https://llr.sc.gov/res/) under the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation enforces agent conduct. The South Carolina Supreme Court governs attorney conduct and UPL enforcement. The South Carolina Department of Insurance regulates title insurance rates and underwriter practices.

Here are the statutes agents encounter most frequently:

| Statute / Rule | What It Governs | Agent Impact |
|---|---|---|
| S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-145 | Trust account handling and earnest money | Defines when and how agents can hold earnest money |
| S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-5-310 | Unauthorized Practice of Law | Prohibits agents from performing closing functions |
| S.C. Code Ann. Section 38-75-10 et seq. | Title Insurance Act | Governs policy issuance and premium rates |
| SC Commission Regulation 105-1 through 105-9 | License law rules | Covers disclosure, forms, and supervision |
| S.C. Code Ann. Section 30-5-30 | Recording requirements | Establishes deed and mortgage recording standards |

Agents must also understand that SCREC requires use of approved forms for contracts. The South Carolina Association of Realtors (SCR) produces the standard contract forms including SCR Form 310 (Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate), SCR Form 320 (Addendum), and SCR Form 390 (Closing Attorney Selection). Using non-approved forms without broker authorization creates immediate compliance exposure.

## Forms and Deadlines That Directly Affect Closing

**SCR Form 390, the Closing Attorney Selection form, is where many agents first engage with the closing process.** This form documents which party selects the closing attorney—a decision that carries financial and logistical consequences. In South Carolina, the party who pays for the owner's title insurance policy traditionally selects the closing attorney, though this is negotiable.

The contract form SCR 310 contains specific contingency deadlines that feed directly into the closing timeline. Section 4 covers the financing contingency with defined dates for loan application and commitment. Section 8 addresses the title examination period, typically set at 20 days from the binding agreement date, during which the closing attorney completes the title search and delivers the title commitment to the buyer.

If the title commitment reveals exceptions or defects, the buyer must object in writing within the title examination period specified in SCR Form 310. Failure to object within this window typically constitutes acceptance of the title as-is. This deadline discipline is where experienced agents distinguish themselves from those who [miss critical deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and lose deals.

The TRID (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure) rules still apply federally, requiring the Closing Disclosure to be delivered to the buyer at least three business days before consummation. The closing attorney handles this delivery, but agents need to coordinate scheduling around this federally mandated waiting period.

## Common Mistakes South Carolina Agents Make at Closing

Five errors appear repeatedly in SCREC disciplinary actions and malpractice claims against agents involved in closings. Each one is preventable with proper systems.

**First, agents draft or modify contract addenda that constitute legal documents without attorney review.** Writing custom language into addenda—particularly around title cure provisions, easement exceptions, or boundary disputes—crosses into UPL territory. SCREC has issued cease-and-desist orders and fines ranging from $1,000 to $5,000 for agents who draft legal provisions outside of approved SCR forms.

Second, agents mishandle the earnest money timeline under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-145. The statute requires earnest money to be deposited into a trust account within 48 hours of the binding agreement (excluding weekends and holidays). Agents who hold checks in desk drawers, forget to deposit on time, or deposit into operating accounts face license suspension. SCREC audits trust accounts and the consequences are severe—this is one of the top three reasons for disciplinary action in South Carolina.

Third, agents provide opinions on title commitment exceptions. When a buyer asks "is this easement a problem?" the agent often answers. That answer constitutes legal advice. The correct response is always to direct the buyer to consult with their attorney or the closing attorney about the implications of specific title exceptions.

Fourth, agents fail to verify that the closing attorney is actually licensed and in good standing with the South Carolina Bar. In rare but documented cases, agents have directed clients to individuals whose licenses were suspended. The South Carolina Bar's online directory should be checked if you are working with an unfamiliar attorney.

Fifth, agents neglect to track the title examination period expiration date, allowing buyers to lose their right to object to title defects. This is a [systematic pipeline failure](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) that costs clients their negotiating position and exposes agents to negligence claims.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers-in-charge carry personal liability under S.C. Code Ann. Section 40-57-137 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. This means every closing-related compliance failure by an agent reflects directly on the supervising broker's license.

| Audit Item | Frequency | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly minimum | License revocation, fines up to $10,000 |
| Earnest money deposit timing (48-hour rule) | Every transaction | SCREC disciplinary action |
| SCR form version verification | Quarterly | Contract enforceability challenges |
| Closing attorney license verification | Per transaction | Liability for unauthorized closings |
| File completion with all required disclosures | Per transaction | E&O exposure, Commission complaints |

**Brokers must implement a documented review process for every transaction file before closing.** This review should confirm that the correct version of SCR Form 310 was used, that all addenda are on approved forms, that earnest money deposit receipts show compliance with the 48-hour rule, and that the closing attorney selection form (SCR 390) is properly executed.

SCREC conducts random office audits as well as complaint-driven investigations. During an audit, investigators will pull transaction files and verify that trust account records reconcile with deposit timelines. Brokers who cannot produce documentation face immediate trust account restrictions and potential emergency license suspension under SC Commission Regulation 105-7.

The broker's duty extends to ensuring agents do not [cross compliance boundaries](/blog/where-deals-break-down) that would constitute unauthorized practice of law. Written office policies should explicitly state what agents cannot do: prepare deeds, explain title exceptions, calculate prorations, or disburse any closing funds.

## Liability Exposure When Compliance Fails

The consequences of non-compliance in South Carolina are not abstract. SCREC publishes disciplinary actions quarterly, and the penalties escalate quickly. A first-offense trust account violation typically results in a formal reprimand and a fine between $1,000 and $5,000. Repeated violations or willful misconduct can trigger license revocation and referral to the Attorney General for criminal prosecution under the UPL statute.

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. If a transaction fails because an agent provided incorrect information about title status, or if a buyer suffers financial loss because the agent failed to track a title examination deadline, the agent's errors and omissions insurance will be tested. Many E&O policies contain exclusions for acts that constitute unauthorized practice of law, meaning the agent may have no coverage at all.

Deal cancellation is the most common immediate consequence. When a title defect surfaces after the examination period has expired because the agent failed to calendar the deadline, the buyer often has no contractual remedy. The deal either proceeds with the defect or collapses entirely, and the agent's commission disappears along with their professional reputation.
