Maryland wire fraud prevention requirements 2026 now carry real teeth for licensed agents who fail to comply. The state lost over $28 million to real estate wire fraud schemes in 2024 alone, according to FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center data for the Baltimore field office. That number forced the Maryland General Assembly and the Maryland Real Estate Commission (MREC) to tighten disclosure obligations, and the consequences for noncompliance land squarely on the agent's license.

This is not a matter of best practice anymore. It is a legal requirement with documented enforcement.

## The Statutory Foundation: Maryland Real Property Section 10-505.1

Maryland codified wire fraud prevention obligations under Real Property Article Section 10-505.1, which was amended during the 2025 legislative session (HB 1134) and took effect October 1, 2025. The statute requires every licensee involved in a residential real estate transaction to deliver a written wire fraud advisory to their client before the client transmits funds electronically to any party.

The advisory must contain specific language warning that email accounts of real estate agents, title companies, and attorneys are frequent targets of compromise. It must instruct the recipient to independently verify all wiring instructions by calling a known phone number — not one provided in the email containing the instructions. The statute explicitly states that verbal warnings alone do not satisfy the requirement.

**Failure to deliver this written advisory creates a rebuttable presumption of negligence if the client subsequently loses funds to a wire fraud scheme.** That presumption shifts the burden to you, the agent, to prove you acted reasonably. Under Maryland tort law, that is an extremely difficult position to defend from.

## Required Forms and When to Deliver Them

The [Maryland Association of Realtors](https://www.mdrealtor.org/) publishes Form 1801 (Wire Fraud Advisory), which was updated in January 2026 to conform with the amended statute. This form must be signed by the client and retained in the transaction file. MREC auditors specifically look for this document during brokerage audits.

Timing matters. The statute says "before the client transmits funds," but MREC guidance (Bulletin 2025-07) recommends delivery at two points: once at the signing of the agency agreement and again within 48 hours of the scheduled settlement date. The dual-delivery approach protects agents from claims that the advisory was buried in early paperwork and forgotten by closing.

| Document | Form Number | Delivery Timing | Recipient |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Wire Fraud Advisory | MAR Form 1801 | At agency agreement signing | Buyer or Seller client |
| Wire Fraud Reminder | MAR Form 1801-R | 48 hours before settlement | Buyer (primarily) |
| Settlement Agent Verification Acknowledgment | Brokerage-specific | At contract ratification | All parties |
| Broker File Compliance Checklist | MREC Audit Form 7B | Ongoing | Broker of record |

Some brokerages require a third form — an internal acknowledgment confirming the agent personally explained the advisory rather than simply handing it over for signature. Check your office policy manual because MREC holds brokers accountable for ensuring their agents comply, which means your broker may impose additional documentation requirements beyond the statutory minimum.

## Maryland Wire Fraud Prevention Requirements 2026: Penalties and Liability Exposure

The penalty structure operates on multiple levels. MREC can issue a reprimand, impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, suspend a license, or revoke it entirely under Code of Maryland Regulations (COMAR) 09.11.01.18. These administrative sanctions apply even if no client actually lost money — the violation is the failure to disclose, not the occurrence of fraud.

**Civil liability is where the financial exposure becomes severe.** If a buyer wires $400,000 to a fraudster's account and you never delivered MAR Form 1801, the rebuttable presumption of negligence under Section 10-505.1(d) means you start the lawsuit already behind. Plaintiffs' attorneys in Maryland have already filed at least six cases in 2025-2026 citing this specific statutory presumption.

Your errors and omissions insurance policy likely contains a "regulatory compliance" exclusion or sub-limit. Many E&O carriers will deny coverage for claims arising from a licensee's failure to follow a mandatory disclosure requirement. That means you could personally face a six-figure or seven-figure judgment with no insurance backstop.

Deal cancellation is also on the table. Settlement attorneys in Maryland have begun refusing to proceed when they discover the wire fraud advisory is missing from the file. They view the absence as a risk indicator, and their own malpractice carriers are pressuring them to document that all parties received proper warnings before funds move.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Wire Fraud Compliance

The first and most frequent error is treating the advisory as a one-time checkbox. Agents hand over Form 1801 buried in the initial paperwork stack, the client signs without reading it, and by the time settlement arrives three months later, the warning has evaporated from memory. The 48-hour reminder delivery exists to solve this exact problem, yet many agents skip it.

The second mistake is failing to document the method of delivery. MREC Bulletin 2025-07 states that electronic delivery is acceptable only if the agent can produce a timestamp and confirmation of receipt. Sending the form via an untracked email or uploading it to a portal without verifying the client accessed it does not satisfy the documentation standard. Use a system that logs opens and signatures with timestamps.

**Third, agents routinely forward wire instructions from settlement companies to their clients via email.** This is the single most dangerous thing you can do. Even with a disclaimer attached, forwarding wire instructions by email puts you in the chain of communication that fraudsters exploit. If a hacker intercepts your email and modifies the routing number, your involvement in transmitting those instructions creates direct liability. Never forward wire instructions. Direct your client to contact the settlement agent independently.

Fourth, some agents assume the settlement attorney's wire fraud warning absolves them of responsibility. It does not. The statute places an independent obligation on each licensee. The attorney's disclosure protects the attorney. Your disclosure protects you. These are separate legal duties that cannot be delegated.

Fifth, agents working [wire instruction verification](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) into their process sometimes verify instructions once at contract ratification but never re-verify before closing. Hackers frequently wait until the final days before settlement to strike because that is when urgency overrides caution. Verification must happen within 24 hours of the wire being sent.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers of record carry supervisory liability under COMAR 09.11.01.21 for their agents' compliance failures. MREC has made clear through enforcement actions in late 2025 that "I didn't know my agent skipped it" is not a viable defense. The broker's obligation is to create systems that prevent omissions and to audit those systems regularly.

| Broker Responsibility | Audit Frequency | Evidence Required |
|----------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Verify Form 1801 in every transaction file | Every closed file within 30 days | Signed, dated copy with timestamp |
| Confirm 48-hour reminder was delivered | Every file where buyer wired funds | Delivery receipt or e-signature log |
| Train agents annually on wire fraud protocols | Once per calendar year minimum | Signed training acknowledgment |
| Review agent email practices for forwarding violations | Quarterly spot-checks | Email audit log or brokerage system records |
| Report suspected wire fraud to MREC and FBI IC3 | Immediately upon discovery | Written incident report retained 5 years |

Brokers should also confirm that their transaction management systems flag missing wire fraud forms before a file can be marked as compliant. If your brokerage still tracks compliance through spreadsheets or memory, you are one missed form away from an MREC audit finding. The agents in your office who handle [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) already understand this principle — now apply it to wire fraud specifically.

MREC conducted 147 brokerage audits in fiscal year 2025 and cited wire fraud advisory deficiencies in 23 percent of them. That is nearly one in four offices failing to meet the standard. Brokers who cannot produce documentation within 10 business days of an audit request face escalating penalties starting at $1,000 per missing file.

## Practical Steps for Agents Between Now and Your Next Closing

Build the advisory delivery into your first client meeting workflow, not your closing preparation workflow. The moment a buyer signs your agency agreement, Form 1801 should be the next document they see. Explain it verbally for 60 seconds. This protects you and actually protects them — clients who understand the threat early are far less likely to fall for a spoofed email later.

Set a calendar reminder for 48 hours before every settlement to send Form 1801-R. This is the reminder version of the advisory, and it should include the specific instruction: "Call [settlement company name] at [known phone number] to verify wire instructions before sending any money." Do not include the wire instructions themselves. Do not attach any banking details. Your job is to warn, not to transmit.

**If you discover that a client received suspicious wiring instructions, you are obligated under Maryland law to report it.** Contact the settlement attorney immediately, instruct your client not to send any funds, and file a report with the FBI's IC3 portal. MREC expects agents to act within hours, not days. Document every step in writing.
