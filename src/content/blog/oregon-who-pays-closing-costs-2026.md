## Oregon Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Allocation Framework

The question of **Oregon who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026** is not answered by a single statute or bright-line rule. Oregon law leaves most cost allocation to negotiation between the parties, which means agents bear the responsibility of getting it right in the contract. Errors here do not just irritate clients — they trigger license complaints, blown deals, and E&O claims.

Oregon's regulatory framework sits under the Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA), which enforces ORS Chapter 696 and OAR Chapter 863. Unlike states that mandate specific cost splits by statute, Oregon treats closing cost allocation as a contractual matter governed by whatever the parties agree to in the Oregon Real Estate Forms (OREF) purchase agreement. That flexibility is simultaneously the agent's greatest tool and greatest liability exposure.

The practical reality in 2026 is that market conditions, lender requirements, and local custom all influence who pays what. Agents who rely on "how we've always done it" instead of writing precise contractual language are inviting problems that no relationship can fix once they land on a broker's desk.

## Standard Cost Allocation: What Custom Dictates vs. What the Contract Controls

**Oregon has no statutory requirement dictating that sellers must pay any particular closing cost.** Every allocation must be spelled out in the sale agreement, primarily through OREF Form 001 (Residential Real Estate Sale Agreement), Section 10 — Closing Costs and Prorations. If a cost is not addressed in the contract, the default is that each party pays their own customary fees, but "customary" is not a legal defense if a dispute arises.

## The Forms That Govern: OREF 001 and Addenda

OREF Form 001 is the backbone of residential transactions in Oregon. Section 10 specifically addresses title insurance, escrow fees, tax prorations, and other closing costs. Agents must fill this section out completely — leaving blanks is not the same as "following custom." A blank field on Form 001 creates ambiguity that title companies will flag and buyers' lenders may reject.

When buyers request seller-paid closing cost credits, agents should use OREF Form 001's designated fields or attach OREF Form 030 (Addendum to Sale Agreement) to specify the exact dollar amount or percentage. The language matters: writing "seller to pay up to $8,000 toward buyer's closing costs" is different from "seller to pay buyer's closing costs," and the latter invites a dispute over what constitutes "closing costs" versus prepaid items.

**Agents representing buyers with FHA, VA, or USDA loans must know the lender-imposed caps on seller concessions.** FHA limits seller contributions to 6% of the sale price, VA caps them at 4% (with specific rules on what counts), and conventional loans vary from 3% to 9% depending on down payment and occupancy type. Writing a seller concession that exceeds lender limits will cause the appraisal or underwriting to flag the file, delaying or killing the deal.

## Oregon-Specific Costs Most Agents Overlook

Oregon does not impose a statewide real estate transfer tax, which distinguishes it from neighboring Washington's excise tax. However, that does not mean transfers are fee-free. Washington County's Documentary Transaction Tax (WC Code 3.08) applies at $1 per $1,000 of consideration. Agents working in that county who fail to disclose this cost to sellers are setting up a closing-day surprise.

Escrow fees in Oregon are typically split equally unless the contract specifies otherwise. The Oregon Escrow and Title Insurance Board (part of the Department of Consumer and Business Services) regulates escrow agents under ORS Chapter 696, and escrow companies will follow the contract's instructions to the letter — they will not default to "custom" if the contract is silent.

Property tax prorations deserve special attention. Oregon's fiscal year runs July 1 through June 30, and property taxes are paid in arrears. The proration method (calendar-day basis is standard) should be stated in Section 10 of OREF Form 001. ## Common Mistakes Oregon Agents Make on Closing Costs

**Mistake 1: Leaving Section 10 of OREF Form 001 partially blank.** When an agent does not specify who pays for owner's title insurance or how escrow fees split, the escrow company will call both agents for clarification — often days before closing. If the parties disagree at that point, the deal stalls. OREA considers incomplete contract preparation a violation of the competency standards in OAR 863-015-0125.

**Mistake 2: Writing seller concessions that exceed lender limits.** This is not just a lender problem. If the concession is rejected during underwriting and the buyer cannot cover the gap, the transaction fails. The listing agent may face a claim for failing to advise the seller that the concession structure was unrealistic. Agents should verify current lender limits before presenting any offer with a concession request.

**Mistake 3: Confusing "closing costs" with "prepaids" in concession language.** Lenders distinguish between actual closing costs (title fees, recording, escrow) and prepaid items (homeowner's insurance, property tax reserves, per diem interest). If the contract says "seller pays buyer's closing costs" but the buyer's lender allocates the credit to prepaids, the numbers will not align. Specific dollar caps with broad application language ("toward buyer's closing costs and prepaids") solve this.

**Mistake 4: Failing to disclose the Washington County transfer tax to sellers in listing presentations.** Sellers in Beaverton, Hillsboro, Tigard, and unincorporated Washington County expect a net sheet that reflects this cost. Agents who omit it — even accidentally — face credibility damage and potential ORS 696.301 disciplinary complaints for negligent misrepresentation.

**Mistake 5: Not updating net sheets when contract terms change.** Every addendum that modifies closing cost allocation (repair credits, extended closing dates that shift proration amounts, concession adjustments) must trigger a revised net sheet. Agents who [miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) or fail to update numbers risk delivering inaccurate financial information to their clients.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Oregon's principal broker supervision requirements under ORS 696.022 and OAR 863-015-0260 require that brokers maintain oversight of their agents' transaction files. When it comes to closing cost allocation, brokers should verify several specific items before any file moves to pending status.

First, confirm that OREF Form 001 Section 10 is completed in full — no blanks, no "per custom" shorthand that escrow companies cannot enforce. Second, verify that any seller concession amount falls within the buyer's lender guidelines, which the buyer's agent should confirm in writing. Third, ensure that net sheets match the contract terms and have been updated after every addendum.

Brokers who discover a pattern of incomplete Section 10 entries should treat it as a training deficiency, not a one-off mistake. OREA's complaint process under [ORS 696.301](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors696.html) allows consumers to file against both the agent and the supervising broker if inadequate supervision contributed to the harm. A single complaint may result in a letter of reprimand; a pattern can trigger license suspension or conditions on the broker's license.

File audits should also check that agents are providing the Closing Disclosure comparison against the original contract terms. While this is technically the lender's responsibility under TRID, agents who spot discrepancies protect their clients and reduce post-closing disputes. Brokers who implement [weekly pipeline review systems](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-system) catch these issues before they become complaints.

## Liability Exposure When Cost Allocation Goes Wrong

If an agent fails to properly document closing cost responsibilities, multiple consequences can follow. Under ORS 696.301(1), the Oregon Real Estate Commissioner can impose civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation for conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness. Repeated violations may result in license revocation under ORS 696.301(2).

Beyond regulatory action, agents face civil liability. If a seller's net proceeds are materially lower than represented because the agent failed to account for costs the seller would bear, the seller has a claim for negligent misrepresentation. Oregon courts have recognized that real estate agents owe fiduciary duties including accurate financial disclosure — failure is measured against the standard of care for licensed professionals.

Deal cancellation is the most immediate risk. When a buyer's lender rejects a concession structure that violates their guidelines, and no alternative is available within the contract timeline, the buyer may exercise their financing contingency. The seller loses time on market, potentially misses a move-up purchase, and the listing agent's relationship — and referral pipeline — suffers damage that no apology can repair.
