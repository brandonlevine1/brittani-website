## Alabama 1031 Exchange State Tax Implications 2026: The Rules That Trip Up Agents

A federal 1031 exchange defers capital gains at the IRS level, but Alabama adds a separate layer of state tax obligations that can blindside agents and their clients. Understanding Alabama 1031 exchange state tax implications 2026 is not optional—it directly affects whether your seller walks away clean or faces a surprise state tax bill months after closing. The Alabama Department of Revenue enforces these rules aggressively, and the liability often falls on the parties closest to the transaction.

Alabama does not automatically piggyback on the federal deferral. The state has its own withholding statute, its own forms, and its own enforcement mechanisms that operate independently of IRC Section 1031. Agents who assume federal compliance equals state compliance are making a costly mistake.

## How Alabama Taxes Real Estate Gains Differently

**Alabama imposes a mandatory withholding tax on real estate dispositions by nonresident sellers under Section 40-18-86 of the Code of Alabama 1975.** The withholding rate is 3% of the sale price for individuals and 4% for corporations, partnerships, and other entities. This withholding is collected at closing and remitted to the Alabama Department of Revenue within 30 days.

The withholding obligation falls on the "transferee" (buyer) or the closing agent handling the transaction. If neither party collects the tax, both can be held jointly and severally liable. This is where real estate agents enter the picture—not as the liable party themselves, but as the professional who should be flagging the requirement to all parties.

For 1031 exchanges, Alabama recognizes the federal deferral but requires documentation proving the transaction qualifies. Without proper certification at closing, the withholding must still be collected regardless of the seller's intent to complete an exchange.

## The NRA-1 Form: Alabama's Gatekeeper for Exchange Transactions

The critical form in any Alabama 1031 exchange involving a nonresident seller is **Form NRA-1**, the Nonresident Seller Withholding Exemption Certificate. This form must be completed and delivered to the closing agent before or at closing to exempt the transaction from the standard withholding requirement.

Form NRA-1 includes a specific checkbox for Section 1031 exchanges. The seller certifies under penalty of perjury that the disposition is part of a like-kind exchange under IRC Section 1031 and that a qualified intermediary has been engaged. The closing agent retains this form and is relieved of withholding liability once a properly executed NRA-1 is on file.

If the seller is an Alabama resident, the withholding requirement under Section 40-18-86 does not apply in the first place—but the gain itself remains taxable on the seller's Alabama income tax return unless deferred by a valid exchange. Resident sellers report their exchange on Alabama Form 40, Schedule D, and attach [federal Form 8824](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8824) to their state return.

## Federal vs. Alabama: Key Differences Agents Must Track

The interplay between federal and state treatment creates traps for the unwary. Here is how the two systems compare on the elements that matter most at closing:

| Requirement | Federal (IRS) | Alabama (ADOR) |
|---|---|---|
| Deferral statute | IRC Section 1031 | Recognized via conformity; no separate state statute |
| Withholding on nonresidents | FIRPTA (15% or 10%) | Section 40-18-86 (3% or 4%) |
| Exchange exemption form | Form 8288-B (application) | Form NRA-1 (certificate at closing) |
| Filing deadline for exemption | Before closing (ideally) | At or before closing |
| Penalty for non-collection | Buyer liable for FIRPTA amount | Buyer/closing agent liable + 25% penalty + interest |

Notice that Alabama's exemption mechanism is simpler than FIRPTA's—it does not require IRS approval before closing. But it is also less forgiving if missed, because the penalty assessment is automatic and does not require an audit to trigger.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Flag Withholding

The downstream consequences of missing Alabama's withholding requirements hit multiple parties. The closing agent faces direct liability under Section 40-18-86 for failing to collect. The buyer becomes the statutory backstop—if the closing agent does not pay, the Alabama Department of Revenue pursues the buyer for the full withholding amount plus a penalty of up to 25% and statutory interest.

**For agents, the exposure is professional rather than statutory, but no less damaging.** A seller who gets hit with an unexpected tax bill after closing will point directly at the agent who failed to raise the issue. Errors and omissions claims, regulatory complaints to the Alabama Real Estate Commission (AREC), and loss of referral business all follow from a single missed form.

Deal cancellations also occur when the issue surfaces late. If a nonresident seller learns at the closing table that 3% of the sale price will be withheld—and no one arranged the 1031 exchange paperwork in advance—the seller may refuse to close. That delayed closing cascades into [missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on the buy-side, potential earnest money disputes, and commission losses for everyone involved.

## Common Mistakes Alabama Agents Make on 1031 Exchanges

Three patterns account for the majority of 1031-related state tax problems in Alabama transactions.

**First, agents assume that engaging a qualified intermediary is sufficient to satisfy Alabama's requirements.** The QI handles the federal mechanics—holding proceeds, facilitating identification, and ensuring the 180-day timeline. But the QI does not automatically file Form NRA-1 with the closing agent. Someone must ensure that form reaches the right hands before closing.

Second, agents confuse resident and nonresident rules. Alabama residents do not face withholding under Section 40-18-86, so agents working primarily with local sellers may never encounter the form. When a nonresident seller appears—a landlord who moved out of state, an inherited property owner in another jurisdiction—the agent defaults to familiar procedures and misses the withholding trigger entirely.

Third, agents fail to verify the exchange timeline against closing dates. Alabama's conformity with federal Section 1031 means the 45-day identification period and 180-day exchange period apply at the state level too. If the replacement property closing slips past day 180, the exchange fails for both federal and Alabama purposes. The original withholding exemption under Form NRA-1 becomes invalid retroactively, and the Alabama Department of Revenue will assess the tax against the closing agent or buyer.

A fourth error deserves mention: agents sometimes advise clients that Alabama "does not have a state-level 1031 rule." This is technically true—Alabama does not have a standalone state exchange statute—but dangerously misleading. Alabama conforms to the Internal Revenue Code through Section 40-18-2, Code of Alabama 1975, which incorporates federal gross income definitions. The exchange deferral flows through that conformity provision, not through any separate state law.

Fifth, agents neglect to document the exchange structure in the listing agreement or purchase contract. Without written evidence that the transaction is intended as a 1031 exchange, the closing agent has no basis to accept a Form NRA-1 exemption and may withhold by default to protect themselves from liability.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents who handle investment property transactions in Alabama should build specific checkpoints into their [transaction review process](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Every listing involving a nonresident seller of investment or rental property should trigger a withholding analysis memo in the file. The memo does not need to be lengthy—it should identify whether the seller is a resident or nonresident, whether the sale qualifies for any NRA-1 exemption, and who is responsible for delivering the form to the closing agent.

| Broker Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Red Flag If Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Seller residency determination | Alabama address on listing agreement or W-9 | Out-of-state address with no NRA-1 in file |
| QI engagement letter | Executed agreement with qualified intermediary | Seller claims exchange but no QI documentation |
| Form NRA-1 execution | Signed and dated before or at closing | Form missing or dated after closing date |
| Exchange timeline tracking | 45-day and 180-day deadlines documented | No calendar entries or deadline notifications |
| Post-closing confirmation | Replacement property acquisition confirmed | File closed without verifying exchange completion |

Brokers should also confirm that their agents are not providing tax advice. AREC's licensing standards require agents to stay within their scope of practice. Recommending a specific exchange structure, advising on tax consequences, or guaranteeing deferral outcomes crosses into territory reserved for CPAs and tax attorneys. The agent's role is to identify the issue, connect the client with qualified advisors, and ensure the transaction paperwork reflects whatever structure those advisors recommend.

## The Role of Closing Agents and Title Companies

Alabama is not an attorney-only closing state, but many transactions—especially those involving 1031 exchanges—benefit from attorney involvement at the closing table. Title companies handling the settlement are typically the party that collects and remits the Section 40-18-86 withholding. They are also the entity that accepts or rejects the Form NRA-1 exemption certificate.

**Agents should confirm with the title company or closing attorney at the earliest possible stage that the 1031 exchange documentation is on their radar.** A call or email during the contract-to-close phase prevents the last-minute scramble that leads to withheld funds or blown closings.

The Alabama Department of Revenue publishes [guidance on nonresident withholding](https://www.revenue.alabama.gov/individual-corporate/nonresident-requirements/) through its website, including fillable versions of Form NRA-1 and instructions for closing agents. Agents should bookmark this resource and share it with clients early in the listing process when a 1031 exchange is anticipated.

## Planning for 2026: What May Change

Alabama's legislature periodically updates its tax code conformity provisions. As of early 2026, Alabama remains in full conformity with IRC Section 1031 through its adoption of federal adjusted gross income as the starting point for state tax calculations under Section 40-18-15. Any federal changes to Section 1031—such as the proposals that have circulated in Congress to cap or eliminate like-kind exchange deferral—would flow through to Alabama automatically unless the state legislature enacts a decoupling provision.

Agents should monitor the Alabama Department of Revenue's administrative rulings for any changes to withholding rates or NRA-1 procedures. The current 3%/4% rates have been stable, but rate adjustments require only administrative action, not legislative approval, under certain circumstances.
