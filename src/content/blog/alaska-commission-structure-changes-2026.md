# Alaska Commission Structure Rules After the NAR Settlement: What Every Agent Must Know in 2026

The Alaska commission structure rules after the NAR settlement 2026 have fundamentally changed how agents on both sides of a transaction discuss, disclose, and earn their compensation. If you hold a license issued by the Alaska Real Estate Commission (AREC) under Alaska Statute 08.88, you are directly affected—regardless of whether your brokerage is a NAR member. The ripple effects of the August 2024 settlement have now fully matured into enforceable MLS policies and evolving state-level expectations that demand your attention right now.

## How the NAR Settlement Changed Commission Practices in Alaska

The settlement resolved antitrust claims alleging that NAR's rules artificially inflated buyer-agent commissions by requiring sellers to offer compensation through the MLS. Two practice changes now apply to every MLS participant operating in Alaska.

First, MLS listings can no longer include offers of buyer-agent compensation in any data field. The Alaska MLS, which serves the majority of the state's residential market, updated its rules to strip commission-offer fields from listing input screens. Second, buyer agents must execute a written buyer representation agreement before providing any home-touring services.

These changes are not optional, even if your brokerage is not affiliated with NAR. Most Alaska agents access listings through NAR-affiliated MLS systems, which means compliance with these participation rules is a condition of doing business. AREC has also signaled increased scrutiny of commission-related disclosures during routine audits conducted under 12 AAC 64, the administrative code governing real estate licensees.

## Alaska-Specific Forms and Disclosure Requirements

**Alaska agents rely on a specific set of forms that must now be used with heightened precision.** The Consumer Disclosure form, required under AS 08.88.396, must be presented to all parties at the initial meeting—and it now carries more weight than ever because it establishes who you represent and how you will be compensated.

The Alaska MLS Buyer Representation Agreement is the document that satisfies the settlement's requirement for a written agreement before touring. This form must spell out the exact amount or rate of compensation the buyer's agent will earn. Vague language like "customary commission" or "to be determined" does not satisfy the requirement. The form must state a specific dollar figure or percentage.

For listing agents, the Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement remains the standard contract, but its commission section has new implications. Because the MLS no longer broadcasts what a seller is willing to pay a buyer's agent, listing agents must ensure their clients understand that any buyer-side concession will be negotiated outside the MLS—often through direct outreach or in the purchase agreement itself. The Alaska Association of Realtors updated its [standard forms guidance](https://www.prior.prior.prior) in late 2025, and agents should reference the most current versions available through their brokerage.

| Form / Document | Purpose | When Required |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Disclosure (AS 08.88.396) | Discloses agency relationship and compensation | First substantive contact with any party |
| Buyer Representation Agreement | Written agreement for compensation before touring | Before showing any property |
| Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement | Establishes listing terms and seller-side commission | Before marketing a property on MLS |
| Purchase and Sale Agreement (commission terms) | Documents any buyer-agent compensation from seller | At offer stage |
| HUD-1 / Closing Disclosure | Itemizes all compensation paid at settlement | At closing |

## What Happens If an Agent Fails to Comply

Noncompliance carries real consequences across three separate enforcement tracks. Understanding each track helps clarify why cutting corners on commission disclosures is a career-level risk.

**The Alaska Real Estate Commission can suspend or revoke your license under AS 08.88.311 for violations of disclosure requirements.** AREC treats failure to provide the Consumer Disclosure form or misrepresenting compensation terms as grounds for disciplinary action. Fines issued by the commission can reach $25,000 per violation under AS 08.88.071, and the commission publishes disciplinary actions on its public records.

MLS-level penalties are a second layer. The Alaska MLS imposes fines starting at $500 for first-time violations of its participation rules, with escalating penalties for repeat offenses. Persistent noncompliance can result in suspension of MLS access—effectively shutting down your ability to list or search properties.

The third track is civil liability. If a buyer or seller can demonstrate that an agent failed to disclose commission terms, manipulated compensation discussions, or steered clients toward higher-commission listings, that agent faces exposure in private lawsuits. The NAR settlement itself arose from this exact type of claim. Alaska courts recognize breach of fiduciary duty under AS 08.88.396(b), and damages in such cases can include the full commission amount plus attorney fees.

| Enforcement Body | Penalty Type | Maximum Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Alaska Real Estate Commission (AREC) | License discipline, fines | $25,000 per violation; license revocation |
| Alaska MLS | Participation fines, suspension | Loss of MLS access |
| Civil courts | Damages, breach of fiduciary duty | Full commission disgorgement plus legal fees |

## Common Mistakes Alaska Agents Are Making Right Now

Even well-intentioned agents are tripping over the new rules. Here are five specific errors showing up in Alaska transactions.

**Mistake one: failing to execute the Buyer Representation Agreement before the first showing.** Some agents treat the agreement as something to "get signed later" or assume a verbal understanding is sufficient. It is not. The written agreement must be signed before you open a lockbox, not after.

Mistake two: using ambiguous compensation language. Agents who write "buyer's agent commission per MLS" or "standard 3%" on the buyer representation agreement are referencing a system that no longer exists. The agreement must specify compensation in a way that does not depend on MLS data fields that have been eliminated.

Mistake three: embedding buyer-agent compensation into MLS remarks or showing instructions. Some listing agents have tried to work around the MLS field prohibition by typing commission offers into the private or public remarks sections. MLS compliance teams are actively monitoring for this behavior and issuing violations.

Mistake four: neglecting to document seller authorization for concessions. When a seller agrees to contribute toward the buyer's agent compensation, that agreement must appear in writing—ideally in the purchase agreement or a separate addendum. Verbal authorizations create audit exposure and closing disputes.

Mistake five: dual agents failing to disclose both sides of the compensation structure. Alaska allows limited dual agency under AS 08.88.396(d), but dual agents face especially strict disclosure obligations. Both the buyer and seller must understand in writing exactly how the agent is being compensated by each party. Failure here is the fastest path to a commission complaint.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under AS 08.88.345, which means AREC holds the supervising broker responsible for the conduct of their licensees. If an agent in your office mishandles commission disclosures, the investigation does not stop with that agent.

Start with a file audit of every active transaction. Verify that each buyer file contains a signed Buyer Representation Agreement with specific compensation terms dated before the first showing. Confirm that every listing file includes a signed Consumer Disclosure form and that the Exclusive Right to Sell agreement addresses how buyer-side compensation will be handled outside the MLS.

Review your office's advertising materials and social media posts. Agents who publicly promise "full-service, no cost to buyers" may be making misleading representations about how compensation works under the new rules. AREC's advertising standards under 12 AAC 64.110 apply to digital content just as they do to print.

**Implement a quarterly compliance review for your entire office.** This is not optional diligence—it is the standard of care that AREC will expect your brokerage to meet if a complaint arises. ## How Alaska Agents Should Structure Compensation Going Forward

The practical reality is that commission negotiations now happen in at least three separate conversations: between the listing agent and seller, between the buyer's agent and buyer, and between the buyer's agent (or buyer) and the listing side during offer negotiations.

Listing agents should discuss buyer-side concessions with sellers during the listing appointment. Document the seller's willingness—or unwillingness—to contribute to the buyer's agent fee, and outline how that concession will be communicated to cooperating agents. Direct broker-to-broker outreach, seller concession language in the purchase agreement, or external marketing channels are all permissible methods.

Buyer's agents should approach the Buyer Representation Agreement as a value conversation, not a formality. Explain what services you provide, what your fee will be, and under what circumstances the seller may cover part or all of that fee. Agents who [miss deadlines or skip steps](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in this process are the ones most likely to face disputes at the closing table.
