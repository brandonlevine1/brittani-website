## Understanding Montana HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026

Montana's condominium and planned community markets have expanded rapidly across the Gallatin Valley, Flathead Lake corridor, and Missoula metro area. With that growth comes heightened scrutiny of **Montana hoa & condo disclosure rules 2026** and the agent's role in ensuring buyers receive mandated association documents before closing. Failing to manage these disclosures properly exposes both listing and buyer agents to rescission claims, commission clawbacks, and disciplinary action from the Montana Board of Realty Regulation.

The governing statutes sit primarily in the Montana Unit Ownership Act (Title 70, Chapter 23, MCA) for condominiums and the Montana Planned Community Act (referenced in parts of Title 70, Chapter 23, Part 4). Agents who work HOA-attached properties in any Montana market need to know exactly which documents trigger which rights—and when those rights expire.

## The Statutory Framework Agents Must Know

Montana's disclosure obligations for condominium resales are codified under [Montana Code Annotated Section 70-23-613](https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0700/chapter_0230/part_0060/section_0130/0700-0230-0060-0130.html). This section requires the seller (or the association on the seller's behalf) to deliver a resale certificate to the prospective buyer. The certificate must contain the association's current financial statements, budget, reserve disclosures, pending assessments, and any litigation involving the association.

For planned communities governed by recorded covenants but not organized under the Unit Ownership Act, the disclosure obligations differ. These communities typically rely on contractual provisions in the CC&Rs rather than a statutory resale certificate mandate. Agents must verify whether the property falls under the Unit Ownership Act or merely under private covenant governance—a distinction that determines whether the 15-day rescission right applies.

**The Montana Board of Realty Regulation (under the Department of Labor and Industry) enforces licensee conduct through administrative rules in ARM 24.210.** When an agent mishandles a disclosure obligation, the Board can impose sanctions ranging from mandatory education to license suspension.

## What the Resale Certificate Must Include

MCA 70-23-613 specifies the minimum contents of a condominium resale certificate. Agents should verify that every certificate delivered to a buyer includes all required elements, because an incomplete certificate may not trigger the rescission clock.

| Required Element | Statutory Reference | Common Omission Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly assessment amount and frequency | MCA 70-23-613(1)(a) | Low |
| Pending special assessments | MCA 70-23-613(1)(b) | Moderate—boards delay approval |
| Current reserve fund balance | MCA 70-23-613(1)(c) | High in self-managed HOAs |
| Association financial statement | MCA 70-23-613(1)(d) | High—small associations lack audits |
| Pending litigation disclosure | MCA 70-23-613(1)(e) | Moderate—boards downplay disputes |
| Insurance coverage summary | MCA 70-23-613(1)(f) | Moderate |

Agents should not rely on a one-page summary letter from the HOA manager. The statute requires substantive documentation, not a cover sheet that references attached exhibits. If those exhibits are missing from the buyer's package, the certificate is arguably incomplete.

## Rescission Rights and Timing Deadlines

**Buyers have a statutory 15-day rescission period after receiving the resale certificate under MCA 70-23-613.** This right cannot be waived by contract. If the buyer does not receive the certificate at all, the rescission right remains open indefinitely until delivery occurs—or until closing effectively moots it through merger doctrine arguments that courts have not uniformly accepted.

The 15-day clock creates a timing problem agents must manage carefully. If a seller waits until five days before closing to deliver the certificate, the buyer technically retains rescission rights that extend beyond the scheduled closing date. This can result in last-minute deal collapses that neither party anticipated.

Listing agents should order the resale certificate within 48 hours of going under contract. Most Montana property management companies (like Associa, FirstService, or local firms such as Edgewater Management in the Flathead area) charge between $150 and $400 for certificate preparation and require 7 to 14 business days to produce it.

## Montana HOA & Condo Disclosure Rules 2026: Forms and Documentation

Montana does not prescribe a state-issued form specifically for the resale certificate—unlike some states that publish a fill-in-the-blank template through their real estate commission. The Montana Association of Realtors (MAR) publishes transactional forms that address the process, however.

The MAR Residential Purchase Agreement (Form No. 21) contains provisions referencing HOA and condominium disclosures in its contingency section. Agents should also be familiar with MAR Form No. 33 (the Property Disclosure Statement), which includes a section asking sellers to identify whether the property is subject to HOA governance and assessments. Failure to check the correct box on Form 33 creates a separate seller disclosure violation under [MCA 70-23-302](/blog/wyoming-seller-disclosure-requirements-2026), which addresses material fact disclosure broadly.

Additionally, agents representing buyers should confirm that their buyer advisory documentation (MAR Form No. 22, Buyer Advisory) references the buyer's right to review HOA documents during the inspection or due diligence period. Many brokerages in Montana use a standalone HOA contingency addendum that sets a separate deadline—typically 10 to 14 days—for buyer review and acceptance of HOA documents apart from the statutory rescission period.

## Common Mistakes Montana Agents Make

Five errors repeatedly surface in Montana transactions involving HOA or condominium properties. Each one can trigger rescission, delay closing, or result in a complaint to the Board.

**First, agents conflate the inspection contingency deadline with the statutory rescission period.** These are two separate clocks. A buyer who removes the inspection contingency on day 10 still retains the statutory 15-day rescission right if the resale certificate was delivered on day 8. Agents who treat waiver of one as waiver of the other are wrong—and they expose their clients to risk.

Second, listing agents fail to order the resale certificate promptly. Waiting until a buyer requests it—or worse, until the title company asks for it—burns calendar days that cannot be recovered. The resulting time crunch forces buyers to waive review periods informally, which does not extinguish their statutory rights.

Third, agents in planned communities (non-condominium HOAs) assume no disclosure obligation exists because the Unit Ownership Act technically applies only to condominiums. While the statutory resale certificate is indeed limited to condo units, the agent's duty to disclose material facts about the property still encompasses HOA fees, rules, and restrictions under MCA 37-51-313 (licensee duties). Ignoring CC&R disclosures in a planned community creates liability even without a statutory certificate requirement.

Fourth, buyer agents accept the seller's representation that "there's no HOA" without verifying through the title commitment or county records. Dormant associations and expired-but-still-recorded CC&Rs catch agents off guard—especially in older subdivisions outside Billings and Great Falls that were platted decades ago with covenants that technically still run with the land.

Fifth, agents allow the resale certificate to be delivered electronically without confirming the buyer actually received and accessed it. Montana law requires delivery, not merely transmission. An email that bounces or lands in spam does not start the 15-day clock.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokerage compliance officers and designated brokers in Montana should build specific audit checkpoints into their transaction oversight process. The [why checklists beat memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) principle applies directly here—relying on agents to remember timing deadlines across multiple concurrent deals is a recipe for errors.

| Audit Point | What Broker Should Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Resale certificate ordered | Confirmation email from HOA/management company in file | Within 3 days of mutual acceptance |
| Certificate delivered to buyer | Signed receipt or confirmed electronic delivery log | Before day 10 of contract |
| 15-day rescission timeline tracked | Calendar entry or system flag with expiration date | Ongoing per transaction |
| MAR Form 33 HOA section completed | Seller initialed the correct box re: association membership | At listing intake |
| Buyer advisory delivered | MAR Form 22 with HOA review language acknowledged | At offer presentation |

Brokers should also verify that their agents are not inadvertently practicing law by advising buyers on the legal significance of specific CC&R provisions. Montana's unauthorized practice of law standards (enforced by the Montana Supreme Court) prohibit licensees from interpreting covenant language for clients. The agent's job is to deliver the documents and recommend legal counsel for interpretation—nothing more.

Firms that handle significant condominium volume in markets like Big Sky, Whitefish, or downtown Missoula should consider requiring agents to complete a [transaction coordination workflow](/blog/what-is-a-transaction-coordinator) that tracks HOA-specific milestones separately from general contingency dates. The cost of one missed deadline far exceeds the cost of building this process into your brokerage operations.

## Liability When Agents Fail to Comply

The consequences of noncompliance break into three categories: deal-level, disciplinary, and civil.

At the deal level, a buyer who never received the resale certificate retains the right to rescind. If the deal has already closed, Montana courts may still entertain a rescission action if the buyer can demonstrate they were deprived of material information that would have changed their purchase decision. This is not theoretical—Montana district courts in Gallatin County have addressed post-closing rescission claims in association-heavy developments.

**Disciplinarily, the Montana Board of Realty Regulation can sanction a licensee under ARM 24.210.641 for failure to exercise reasonable skill and care.** Violations related to disclosure failures typically result in a stipulated consent order requiring additional continuing education, though repeat offenders face suspension. The Board publishes disciplinary actions publicly, which can damage an agent's reputation in Montana's relatively small professional community.

Civil liability extends beyond the Board. Buyers can pursue damages under MCA 70-23-613(4), which provides that a purchaser may recover actual damages and reasonable attorney fees if the seller (or the seller's agent) fails to deliver the required certificate. Listing agents who are contractually responsible for facilitating delivery may find themselves named as co-defendants in these actions.
