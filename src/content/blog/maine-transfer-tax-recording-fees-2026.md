## Understanding Maine Transfer Tax & Recording Fee Schedules 2026

Getting the numbers wrong on a transfer tax declaration or miscalculating recording fees in Maine does not just delay closings—it can kill deals and expose agents to liability claims. The Maine transfer tax & recording fee schedules 2026 remain governed by Title 36, Chapter 711 of the Maine Revised Statutes, and the rates have not changed from prior years, but the procedural requirements continue to trip up agents who treat them as afterthoughts. Every licensed agent operating in the state needs to know exactly what is owed, who owes it, and which forms must be filed before a deed hits the registry counter.

Maine's transfer tax is formally called the Real Estate Transfer Tax, administered jointly by Maine Revenue Services and the county registries of deeds. The statutory authority sits in 36 M.R.S. § 4641-A through § 4641-N. Unlike states that impose a flat per-transaction fee, Maine calculates its tax on the actual consideration exchanged, which means agents must verify contract price accuracy on every declaration filed.

## Current Tax Rates and Recording Fee Breakdown

**The Maine Real Estate Transfer Tax rate is $4.40 per $1,000 of consideration, split equally between buyer and seller at $2.20 each.** This rate applies statewide with no county-level variation. On a $400,000 sale, the total transfer tax is $1,760—$880 from each party unless the purchase and sale agreement shifts the burden.

Recording fees are set by the individual county registries under the authority of 33 M.R.S. § 751. The base structure is consistent across all sixteen counties, though minor surcharges can differ.

| Fee Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| First page of any document | $38.00 |
| Each additional page | $5.00 |
| Maine Housing Fund surcharge | $1.00 per page |
| Transfer tax (buyer share) | $2.20 per $500 of consideration |
| Transfer tax (seller share) | $2.20 per $500 of consideration |
| Withholding form (REW-1) | No separate recording fee |

A typical two-page warranty deed on a $350,000 property will cost $43 in recording fees plus $1,540 in total transfer tax. Agents who quote closing costs without calculating these figures accurately create problems that surface at the settlement table.

## Required Forms and Filing Procedures

Maine requires the Real Estate Transfer Tax Declaration (RETTD) to accompany every deed presented for recording. This declaration is the mechanism by which Maine Revenue Services tracks taxable transfers and enforces collection. Without a properly completed RETTD, the registry will reject the deed outright.

For transactions involving nonresident sellers, agents must also account for the Maine withholding requirement under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A. The applicable form is the **REW-1** (Withholding of Maine Income Tax on the Sale of Real Property), which must be filed with Maine Revenue Services. A companion form, **REW-3** (Withholding Certificate), may be used when the seller claims an exemption or reduced withholding.

**The RETTD form requires the full legal description of the property, the total consideration, the type of deed, and the exemption code if applicable.** Agents frequently delegate this to closing attorneys or title companies, but the data originates from the purchase and sale agreement the agent helped draft. Errors in consideration amount on the RETTD versus the contract price create audit flags with Maine Revenue Services.

The [Maine Revenue Services Real Estate Transfer Tax page](https://www.maine.gov/revenue/taxes/real-estate-transfer-tax) provides current forms, rate tables, and exemption code lists that agents should bookmark.

## Exemptions That Agents Must Recognize

Not every transfer triggers the full $4.40 per $1,000 tax. Under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C, several categories of transactions qualify for partial or full exemption. The most common exemptions agents encounter include transfers between spouses, transfers to trusts where the grantor is the beneficiary, and transfers by operation of law such as foreclosure deeds.

However, claiming an exemption incorrectly on the RETTD can result in penalties assessed by Maine Revenue Services under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-H. The penalty is the unpaid tax plus interest from the date of recording. Agents who advise clients that a transfer is "probably exempt" without consulting the statute or the client's attorney are stepping into unauthorized practice of law territory.

| Common Exemption | Statute Reference | Exemption Code |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer between spouses | 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C(1) | Code 3 |
| Transfer to/from revocable trust | 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C(6) | Code 8 |
| Transfer by foreclosure | 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C(5) | Code 7 |
| Transfer to government entity | 36 M.R.S. § 4641-C(2) | Code 4 |
| Gift between family members | Not exempt | N/A |

That last row catches agents off guard regularly. Gifts between parents and children are **not** exempt from the Maine transfer tax unless they meet the narrow criteria of another applicable exemption. The tax is calculated on the fair market value of the property when no monetary consideration is exchanged.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with Transfer Taxes and Recording Fees

**First, agents misquote the split.** The default statutory split is 50/50, but agents sometimes tell clients the seller pays the full amount or the buyer pays nothing. Unless the purchase and sale agreement explicitly states a different allocation, both parties owe $2.20 per $500. Misrepresenting this at the offer stage leads to renegotiation pressure at closing.

Second, agents submit inaccurate consideration figures. When seller concessions, repair credits, or personal property allocations reduce the taxable consideration, the RETTD must reflect the actual amount subject to tax—not the gross contract price. Overstating the consideration means the client overpays. Understating it triggers an audit and potential penalty under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-H.

Third, agents forget the nonresident withholding requirement entirely. If the seller's primary residence is outside Maine, the 2.5% withholding under 36 M.R.S. § 5250-A applies unless a REW-3 exemption certificate is filed. Failing to withhold makes the buyer liable for the tax, which is a problem agents rarely explain during the offer stage. This issue parallels [similar FIRPTA-style requirements in other states](/blog/wisconsin-firpta-withholding-rules-2026) that agents must track.

Fourth, agents assume recording fees are uniform across document types. Mortgage discharges, easements, and liens each have specific page-count implications. Quoting a single flat fee for "recording" without knowing the document length results in shortage at closing.

Fifth, agents neglect to confirm that prior-recorded documents—like a satisfaction of mortgage—have actually been filed before the new deed records. A title that appears clear but has an unrecorded discharge creates a chain-of-title issue the registry will not catch but a future buyer's title search will.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers have a supervisory obligation under the Maine Real Estate Commission's rules (02-039 C.M.R. Chapter 10) to ensure their agents comply with disclosure and procedural requirements. Transfer tax accuracy falls squarely within this duty because errors create client harm and potential trust account discrepancies.

**Brokers should audit every closed transaction file for a completed RETTD that matches the contract consideration.** If the file shows a $425,000 sale but the RETTD lists $415,000 because of a negotiated credit, the broker needs documentation explaining the discrepancy—typically a signed addendum or closing attorney's worksheet.

Brokers must also verify that their agents are not providing tax advice when discussing transfer tax exemptions. Telling a client "you qualify for the spousal exemption" without directing them to legal counsel crosses the line. The Maine Real Estate Commission has disciplined agents for unauthorized practice of law in far less ambiguous situations. Maintaining clear documentation that exemption determinations came from an attorney or CPA protects the brokerage.

Transaction file reviews should confirm that nonresident sellers have either a REW-1 on file or a REW-3 exemption certificate. Brokerages that handle [multiple closings with complex compliance timelines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) benefit from systematic checklists rather than relying on individual agent memory. The [relationship between checklists and deal integrity](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is well-documented—transfer tax compliance is one more item that belongs on the pre-closing audit list.

## Liability When Agents Get It Wrong

The consequences of transfer tax and recording fee errors range from inconvenient to career-threatening. At the mild end, a miscalculated fee means someone writes an additional check at closing and the deal proceeds fifteen minutes late. At the severe end, an unrecorded deed leaves a buyer without marketable title.

Under Maine law, if the transfer tax is not paid, the deed cannot be recorded. An unrecorded deed is still valid between the parties, but it offers no protection against subsequent bona fide purchasers or judgment liens. If an agent's negligence caused the recording failure—say, by providing incorrect consideration figures that led to underpayment—the agent and their brokerage face professional liability exposure.

Maine Revenue Services can also pursue the unpaid tax directly against both parties to the transaction under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-H, with interest accruing from the original transfer date. In cases where the RETTD was intentionally falsified—reporting a lower consideration to reduce tax—criminal penalties under 36 M.R.S. § 4641-I apply, including fines up to $1,000 per violation.

The Maine Real Estate Commission can impose license discipline for conduct that demonstrates incompetence or untrustworthiness under 32 M.R.S. § 13187. Repeated transfer tax errors, especially those that cause client financial harm, fit comfortably within that standard.

## Practical Steps for Staying Compliant in 2026

Build the transfer tax calculation into your listing presentation and buyer consultation. On a $500,000 property, each party owes $1,100 in transfer tax—state that number clearly and early so it never surprises anyone at the settlement table.

Confirm with your closing attorney or title company that the RETTD is prepared from the final, executed purchase and sale agreement—not a draft or verbal estimate. If the contract price changes via amendment, the RETTD must reflect the amended figure.
