## Nevada Attorney vs. Title Company — Who Must Be at Closing 2026: The Definitive Breakdown

The question of Nevada attorney vs. title company — who must be at closing 2026 matters because agents who misunderstand the answer risk both their commission and their license. Nevada is an escrow-closing state, meaning closings are conducted by title companies and licensed escrow agents — not attorneys. That single fact shapes everything from how you advise clients to how you structure your transaction timelines.

Yet confusion persists. Buyers relocating from attorney-closing states like New York or Georgia frequently expect a lawyer at the table. Agents who fail to set clear expectations upfront end up fielding panicked calls at the eleventh hour, and those who inadvertently drift into legal guidance risk a complaint to the Nevada Real Estate Division (NRED).

## What Nevada Law Actually Requires at the Closing Table

**Nevada Revised Statutes Chapter 692A governs title insurance and escrow operations in the state.** Under NRS 692A.117, title insurers and their agents are authorized to conduct closings, hold escrow funds, and disburse proceeds. There is no statutory mandate requiring attorney presence at a residential real estate closing.

The Nevada Division of Insurance regulates title companies and escrow agents, while the State Bar of Nevada governs the practice of law. NRS 7.285 defines the unauthorized practice of law, which means any agent or title officer who drafts legal documents beyond standard transactional forms crosses a statutory line. The practical effect: title companies handle the mechanical settlement process, and attorneys enter the picture only when a party voluntarily retains one.

## Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

Nevada agents work within the standardized forms published by the Nevada REALTORS association (formerly Nevada Association of REALTORS). The Residential Purchase Agreement (Form 500) and its associated addenda form the backbone of every transaction. Agents do not draft closing documents — that responsibility falls squarely on the title company.

The key forms agents touch during the closing phase include the Estimated Buyer Costs worksheet, the Seller's Net Sheet, and any contractual addenda such as the Contingency Removal form (Form 564) or the Extension of Time Addendum. The Settlement Statement itself — now typically a standardized Closing Disclosure under TRID rules — is prepared by the escrow officer. Agents review it for accuracy but do not author it.

**If a title company asks you to prepare or modify a deed, trust document, or legal description, refuse.** That request crosses into unauthorized practice of law territory regardless of how routine it seems. Direct the client to retain private counsel for any document that requires legal drafting.

## Common Mistakes Nevada Agents Make at Closing

The first error is advising clients they do not need to review the Closing Disclosure in advance. Federal TRID regulations require the buyer to receive the CD at least three business days before closing. Agents who push for same-day review create re-disclosure risk, which triggers a new three-day waiting period and blows up the closing date.

The second mistake involves agents interpreting title commitment exceptions for their clients. A Schedule B-II exception on a preliminary title report might flag an easement, CC&R restriction, or boundary overlap. When an agent explains the legal implications of that exception rather than referring the client to counsel or the title officer, they expose themselves to liability under NRS 7.285.

Third, agents frequently fail to confirm escrow instructions match the purchase agreement terms. The escrow officer drafts instructions based on information received, but discrepancies in prorations, repair credits, or seller concessions slip through when no one cross-references the executed contract. This creates post-closing disputes that generate NRED complaints.

Fourth, some agents attend the signing appointment and pressure clients to sign documents they have questions about. An agent's role at closing is observational and supportive — not directive. Instructing a client to "just sign it" when they express concern about a document has generated formal complaints and civil suits in Nevada.

Fifth, agents occasionally recommend a specific attorney or discourage attorney involvement entirely. While Nevada does not require attorney presence, discouraging a client from seeking legal counsel — or steering them to a particular attorney in exchange for referral fees — violates both NRS 645.252 (licensee duties) and RESPA Section 8 prohibitions on kickbacks.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

**Brokers bear supervisory liability under NRS 645.635 for the actions of their agents during closings.** A broker who fails to implement compliance checks may face license discipline alongside the offending agent. NRED has authority to suspend or revoke a broker's license for inadequate supervision.

Brokers should audit three specific areas quarterly. First, verify that agents are not drafting or modifying any documents beyond the NRED-approved standardized forms. Pull a random sample of closed transaction files and confirm every document in the file was either a standard form or was prepared by the title company or an attorney.

Second, review communication logs for language that could constitute legal advice. Emails or texts where agents interpret contract clauses, explain lien priority, or advise on tax consequences of closing create discoverable liability. Training agents on the boundary between factual information and legal interpretation is not optional — it is a broker obligation.

Third, confirm that every transaction file contains evidence of timely Closing Disclosure delivery. If your brokerage uses a [transaction coordination system that tracks deadlines automatically](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines), this audit becomes a five-minute spot check rather than a half-day dig through email threads.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Look For | Risk if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized document drafting | Deeds, legal descriptions, or trust docs prepared by agent | NRS 7.285 violation, license discipline |
| Legal advice in communications | Agent interpreting contract law, lien priority, tax impact | Civil liability, NRED complaint |
| Closing Disclosure timing | CD delivered 3+ business days before closing | TRID violation, deal delay, federal penalty |
| Escrow instruction verification | Instructions match executed purchase agreement | Post-closing disputes, E&O claims |
| Referral fee compliance | No kickback arrangements with title or attorney | RESPA Section 8 violation, federal fine |

## The Role of Attorneys When Parties Choose to Hire One

Nothing in Nevada law prevents a buyer or seller from retaining a real estate attorney. Complex transactions — commercial deals, 1031 exchanges, trust-held property, or short sales — often benefit from legal counsel even though counsel is not required. The attorney may review documents, attend the signing, or simply advise in the background.

When an attorney is involved, communication protocols shift. The title company will typically require a written authorization before sharing documents with the attorney. Agents should confirm early in the transaction whether either party has retained counsel and adjust timelines accordingly — attorney review periods can add three to five days to the closing schedule.

Agents sometimes worry that attorney involvement signals a difficult transaction. In practice, attorney participation often smooths the closing because legal questions get resolved before the signing appointment rather than surfacing as last-minute objections. The key is building the review period into your timeline from the start, which is easier when you use a [structured checklist system rather than relying on memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory).

## Liability Exposure for Non-Compliance

NRED can impose fines up to $10,000 per violation under NRS 645.990 for licensees who engage in conduct that violates the chapter. Unauthorized practice of law carries separate penalties under NRS 7.285, including potential misdemeanor charges. Civil liability compounds the regulatory risk — clients who receive bad legal advice from an agent can sue for damages, and E&O carriers may deny coverage for claims arising from UPL.

Deal cancellation is the other practical consequence. If a buyer discovers mid-closing that the agent provided incorrect legal guidance — say, about how title vesting affects community property rights — the buyer may demand rescission or refuse to close. The seller then has a potential claim against the buyer's agent for tortious interference or negligent misrepresentation.

The [Nevada Real Estate Division's administrative code](https://red.nv.gov/) at NAC 645.605 lists specific grounds for discipline, including making false promises likely to influence a transaction and failure to disclose material facts. Agents who blur the line between transactional facilitation and legal counsel fall squarely within these provisions.

## How to Protect Yourself and Your Clients in 2026

**Build your pre-closing workflow around one principle: the title company runs the settlement, you run the relationship.** Your value at closing is ensuring all contract terms translated correctly into escrow instructions, confirming the numbers on the CD match what was negotiated, and being present to answer procedural questions — not legal ones.

When clients ask legal questions, your response should be a referral, not an answer. Document that referral in writing. A simple email stating "I recommend you discuss this question with a real estate attorney" creates a paper trail that protects you if a complaint arises later.
