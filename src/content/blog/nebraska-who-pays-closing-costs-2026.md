## Nebraska Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Definitive Allocation Guide for Agents

The question of Nebraska who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 is not merely academic. It shapes net sheets, determines whether deals survive appraisal gaps, and—when mishandled—exposes agents and brokers to regulatory action from the Nebraska Real Estate Commission (NREC). Closing cost allocation in Nebraska is governed by a patchwork of statute, contract language, and deep-rooted county custom, and agents who rely on assumptions instead of documentation are playing a dangerous game.

This post breaks down the specific statutes, forms, and compliance risks you need to master before your next transaction closes.

## Statutory Framework: What Nebraska Law Actually Requires

Nebraska does not have a single statute that assigns every closing cost to a particular party. Instead, agents must work with several overlapping authorities. The most consequential is Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-901, which imposes a documentary stamp tax of $2.25 per $1,000 of the total consideration on all conveyances of real property. By statute and longstanding practice, the seller pays this tax unless the parties contractually agree otherwise.

**Title 298 of the Nebraska Administrative Code governs title insurance rates and agent licensing, while the Nebraska Uniform Conveyancing Blanks Act (Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-213 et seq.) standardizes deed and conveyance forms.** These statutes don't allocate costs to buyer or seller per se, but they define the fees that must appear on settlement statements. When a fee exists by law, someone has to pay it—and the purchase agreement is where that allocation lives.

The [Nebraska Real Estate Commission](https://nrec.nebraska.gov/) enforces license law under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2401 through 76-2450. Section 76-2422 specifically prohibits licensees from paying or receiving undisclosed compensation, which means any agent contribution toward closing costs must be documented in the contract and on the settlement statement.

## The Standard Split: How Nebraska Custom Allocates Fees

Custom varies slightly between Lancaster, Douglas, and rural counties, but the table below reflects the predominant allocation you will see on NR Form 1 (the Nebraska REALTORS Association Residential Purchase Agreement) and most title company settlement statements in 2026.

| Fee | Typically Paid By | Statutory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Documentary stamp tax ($2.25/$1,000) | Seller | Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-901 |
| Owner's title insurance policy | Seller | County custom, not statute |
| Lender's title insurance policy | Buyer | Lender requirement |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer | County custom |
| Recording fees (mortgage release) | Seller | County custom |
| Transfer/conveyance prep (deed) | Seller | Custom; NR Form 1, Para. 13 |
| Closing/escrow fee | Split 50/50 | Custom; negotiable per contract |
| Survey | Buyer (unless seller-obligated by contract) | Negotiable |

Agents should not assume this table is self-executing. If a cost allocation is not written into the purchase agreement or addendum, neither party is legally bound to pay it regardless of "how we always do it." Paragraph 13 of NR Form 1 is the primary battleground—if you leave a blank or check the wrong box, the title company has no authority to override your contract language with custom.

## NR Form 1 and Other Key Forms

The Nebraska REALTORS Association publishes forms used in roughly 80 percent of residential transactions statewide. Agents transacting outside the NR form ecosystem still need to address the same allocation issues, but do so at higher compliance risk because generic contracts often omit Nebraska-specific provisions.

**NR Form 1 (Residential Purchase Agreement) Paragraph 13 specifically addresses closing costs, documentary stamp tax, and title evidence.** Within that paragraph, agents must select whether the seller provides an abstract of title continued to date or an owner's title insurance commitment—and which party pays. Failure to make a selection does not void the contract, but it creates an ambiguity that can stall closing or trigger a dispute requiring broker mediation.

NR Form 7 (Counter Proposal) is where renegotiated closing cost terms typically appear. NR Form 13 (Amendment to Purchase Agreement) is used post-acceptance to shift costs—common when an appraisal comes in low and the parties restructure the deal by reallocating fees instead of adjusting purchase price. If you need to add a seller credit toward buyer closing costs, that credit must be memorialized on either NR Form 13 or a separate addendum, and it must appear on the Closing Disclosure under applicable TRID rules.

Understanding how these forms interact with deadline management is critical. Agents who [miss contractual deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on amendments or counter-proposals can inadvertently leave cost allocation unresolved at the point of no return.

## What Happens When Agents Get It Wrong: Liability and Consequences

A misallocated closing cost might seem like a rounding error until it isn't. The NREC has authority under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2435 to impose disciplinary sanctions including license suspension, revocation, civil fines up to $10,000 per violation, or required completion of additional education.

Three scenarios create the most agent exposure in Nebraska:

First, undisclosed agent contributions. If you credit part of your commission toward a buyer's closing costs without documenting it on both the purchase agreement and the Closing Disclosure, you violate Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2422. The NREC treats this as a material misrepresentation.

Second, verbal promises about cost allocation. An agent who tells a buyer "the seller always pays for the survey in this county" without confirming that obligation in writing has created a potential breach-of-duty claim. If the seller refuses and the buyer loses the survey cost, the agent's E&O carrier is the next phone call.

Third, failure to reconcile the Closing Disclosure against the contract. Under TRID and Nebraska practice, the title company prepares the CD, but the listing and buyer's agents have a professional obligation to review it for accuracy. If a contractual seller credit is missing from the CD and the deal closes anyway, the buyer may have a claim against both the title company and the buyer's agent for failing to catch the discrepancy.

## Common Mistakes Nebraska Agents Make on Closing Cost Allocation

**Mistake one: relying on "county custom" without putting it in writing.** Custom is not a contract term. If Paragraph 13 of NR Form 1 is left incomplete or ambiguous, neither party can enforce custom as a binding obligation. Always fill in every blank.

Mistake two: failing to distinguish between a seller credit and a seller concession. A seller credit applied toward buyer closing costs reduces the seller's net proceeds but does not reduce the purchase price. A price reduction does lower the basis for documentary stamp tax. Agents who conflate the two may create tax reporting issues or appraisal complications.

Mistake three: overlooking HOA transfer fees. In Omaha-metro subdivisions with homeowner associations, transfer fees and capital contribution fees can range from $200 to $1,500. These fees are frequently omitted from the purchase agreement because they are not part of the traditional NR Form 1 checklist. If no party is assigned responsibility, the title company will hold the file until someone agrees to pay.

Mistake four: assuming the lender will allow unlimited seller concessions. Conventional, FHA, and VA loans each cap the percentage of seller-paid closing costs differently. An agent who negotiates a six percent seller credit on a conventional loan with five percent down has exceeded the three percent concession limit—meaning the CD will not balance, and closing will be delayed or killed.

Mistake five: using outdated form versions. The Nebraska REALTORS Association periodically revises NR Forms. Agents who pull last year's Form 1 from a file cabinet instead of downloading the current version from the NR website risk using language that has been superseded, which can create enforceability questions.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers in Nebraska carry supervisory responsibility under Neb. Rev. Stat. 76-2417. This is not optional oversight—it is a statutory duty. When a closing cost error triggers a complaint, the NREC will investigate both the agent and the supervising broker.

**Brokers should implement a pre-closing audit that confirms three things: (1) Paragraph 13 of NR Form 1 is fully completed with no blanks, (2) any seller credits or concessions are within lender guidelines, and (3) the final Closing Disclosure matches the contract terms.** This three-point check takes less than ten minutes per file and eliminates the majority of post-closing complaints.

Brokerages with high transaction volume should also track documentary stamp tax calculations across their files. A recurring pattern of incorrect calculations—especially on new-construction deals with lot premiums or builder incentives—can attract NREC audit attention.

| Broker Audit Checkpoint | What to Verify | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| NR Form 1, Para. 13 complete | All cost allocations selected | Contract ambiguity; closing delay |
| Seller credit within lender caps | Credit percentage vs. LTV/loan type | CD rejection; deal cancellation |
| Documentary stamp tax accuracy | $2.25/$1,000 of actual consideration | County recorder rejection; seller liability |
| Agent commission credits disclosed | Written in contract and on CD | NREC violation under 76-2422 |
| Current form version used | Check NR revision date | Enforceability challenge |

Brokers who use [systematic checklists rather than memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) catch these issues before they become complaints. The cost of prevention is trivial compared to the cost of a disciplinary proceeding.

## Negotiation Strategies Agents Should Actually Use in 2026

Rate buydowns remain a popular vehicle for seller-paid closing costs in Nebraska's 2026 market. A seller who contributes $6,000 toward a temporary rate buydown effectively pays a portion of the buyer's closing costs while creating a marketing advantage for the listing. This must be documented on NR Form 13 or a buydown addendum and must remain within lender concession limits.

Another tactic gaining traction: splitting the closing/escrow fee in a way that reflects negotiation leverage rather than the default 50/50 custom. In a seller's market, listing agents push for zero seller contribution to escrow fees. In a buyer's market, buyer's agents request the seller cover the full escrow fee plus the owner's policy. Either outcome is permissible—but only if the contract says so explicitly.

Agents should also be aware that Nebraska's documentary stamp tax is calculated on the total consideration, which includes any personal property conveyed as part of the sale unless it is separately itemized and excluded. Allocating value to appliances or fixtures in a separate bill of sale can reduce the documentary stamp tax owed, but aggressive allocations invite scrutiny from the county assessor.
