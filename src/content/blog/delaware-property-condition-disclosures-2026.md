## Understanding Delaware Property Condition & Environmental Disclosures 2026

Getting disclosure paperwork wrong in Delaware does not just embarrass you at closing—it can unwind an entire transaction and expose you to personal liability. **Delaware property condition & environmental disclosures 2026 carry specific statutory requirements that differ from neighboring states, and the forms themselves have nuances that catch even experienced agents off guard.** The stakes are high enough that brokerages should treat disclosure compliance as a standing audit item, not a once-a-year training topic.

Delaware's disclosure framework sits primarily within Title 6, Chapter 25 of the Delaware Code, commonly referred to as the Seller's Disclosure Act. The Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC), housed under the Division of Professional Regulation, enforces licensing standards that intersect directly with disclosure obligations. Agents who fail to understand both the statutory and regulatory layers risk disciplinary action from DREC on top of civil liability.

This post breaks down the specific forms, statutes, common agent errors, and broker-level enforcement steps relevant to residential transactions in the state right now.

## The Statutory Framework: What Delaware Law Actually Requires

The Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition Report is the backbone document. Required under [6 Del. C. § 2572](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title6/c025/index.html), it mandates that sellers of residential real property provide a written disclosure covering the condition of structural components, mechanical systems, environmental hazards, and more. The form must be delivered to the buyer before or at the time the purchase agreement is executed.

**Delaware recognizes specific exemptions from the disclosure requirement, but they are narrower than many agents assume.** Exempt transactions include sales by a fiduciary during estate administration, court-ordered transfers, foreclosure sales, and transfers between spouses or co-owners. New construction sold by a builder who has never occupied the property is also exempt, though builders face separate warranty obligations.

The statute requires sellers to disclose conditions "of which the seller has actual knowledge." This is not a warranty of the property's condition—it is a knowledge-based standard. But agents should understand that Delaware courts have interpreted "actual knowledge" broadly enough to include facts the seller clearly should have known given their occupancy and experience with the property.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Track

Delaware residential transactions involve more than a single disclosure form. The table below outlines the primary disclosure documents agents should have on their compliance checklist for every applicable deal.

| Document | Triggering Requirement | Responsible Party | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition Report (6 Del. C. § 2572) | All residential sales unless exempt | Seller (agent ensures delivery) | Before or at contract execution |
| Lead-Based Paint Disclosure (42 U.S.C. § 4852d) | Pre-1978 residential properties | Seller and listing agent jointly | Before contract ratification |
| EPA "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home" Pamphlet | Pre-1978 residential properties | Listing agent ensures delivery | With lead disclosure form |
| Property Transfer Tax Affidavit (Form 5402) | All transfers subject to realty transfer tax | Buyer and seller | At closing |
| Radon Disclosure (if applicable) | Recommended but not state-mandated | Seller, if known results exist | With property disclosures |

Agents handling transactions involving properties near Superfund sites, underground storage tanks, or coastal flood zones should also verify whether supplemental disclosures are needed. Delaware's Department of Natural Resources and Environmental Control (DNREC) maintains databases of known contaminated sites, and a property's proximity to one can trigger buyer inquiries that demand accurate, documented responses.

The [federal lead-based paint disclosure rules](/blog/wisconsin-lead-paint-disclosure-2026) apply identically in Delaware as in every other state, but DREC has been explicit that listing agents bear co-responsibility for ensuring the form and pamphlet reach the buyer. This is not a task you can fully delegate to the seller and walk away from.

## Environmental Disclosures: Where Delaware Gets Specific

Delaware's coastal geography and industrial history create environmental disclosure issues that agents in landlocked states rarely encounter. Properties in Sussex and Kent counties frequently sit in flood zones or wetland buffer areas regulated by DNREC. Sellers with knowledge of wetland delineations, prior flooding, or contamination from agricultural runoff must disclose these conditions on the state form.

**Underground storage tanks (USTs) deserve particular attention.** Delaware requires registration of USTs with DNREC, and the Seller's Disclosure form asks directly whether the seller is aware of any underground tanks on the property. An honest "unknown" is acceptable, but a seller who removed a tank five years ago and checks "no" is making a false statement. Agents who know about a prior UST removal—perhaps from a prior listing or neighborhood knowledge—have an independent duty not to conceal material facts under 24 Del. C. § 2912(a)(1).

Radon is a gray area in Delaware. The state does not mandate radon testing or radon-specific disclosure the way some neighboring states do. However, if a seller has had a radon test performed and the results are available, those results should be disclosed. Withholding known test results, particularly elevated readings, creates obvious liability exposure even without a specific radon disclosure statute.

Agents working properties near the former Fort DuPont, Delaware City refinery area, or any DNREC-listed brownfield site should flag environmental concerns early and recommend buyers conduct Phase I environmental assessments. Your role is not to diagnose contamination—it is to ensure the buyer has every known fact and a reasonable opportunity to investigate further.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Delaware Disclosures

Five errors come up repeatedly in DREC complaints and civil litigation related to disclosure failures.

**First, agents treat the Seller's Disclosure form as optional when the seller resists completing it.** The statute does allow a seller to elect not to disclose, but this must be documented in writing and does not eliminate the seller's liability for known material defects. Agents who simply skip the form because the seller "doesn't want to deal with it" are creating a compliance gap that DREC takes seriously.

Second, listing agents fail to review the completed disclosure form before delivering it to the buyer's side. A seller who checks "unknown" on every line, or who leaves sections blank, is either uninformed about what the form asks or being evasive. Neither scenario protects you. Your job is to walk the seller through each section and ensure every response is complete and consistent with what you know about the property.

Third, agents miss the timing requirement. The disclosure must be delivered before or at the time the purchase agreement is signed—not after. Delivering a disclosure form three days after contract execution violates the statutory timeline and gives the buyer a potential rescission argument. This timing mistake is [one of the most common reasons deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) across multiple states.

Fourth, agents fail to document delivery. There is no statutory requirement for a specific delivery method, but agents who hand a disclosure form to a buyer without getting a signed acknowledgment of receipt are playing a dangerous game. Email with read receipt, signed cover page, or transaction management system timestamp—pick one and use it every time.

Fifth, agents neglect to update the disclosure when new information emerges. If a seller discovers a roof leak between disclosure delivery and closing, the disclosure must be amended. Delaware courts have held that a disclosure is a living document through the close of the transaction, not a one-time snapshot that the seller can forget about.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory responsibility under 24 Del. C. § 2912 for the conduct of their affiliated licensees. A broker who discovers—or should have discovered—that agents in the office routinely skip disclosure steps faces potential DREC disciplinary action, including fines and license suspension.

| Audit Item | What to Verify | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure form in file | Completed form or documented seller opt-out present | Every transaction |
| Lead-based paint compliance | Form, pamphlet delivery, and 10-day testing window documented for pre-1978 homes | Every applicable transaction |
| Timing of delivery | Disclosure delivered before or at contract execution (check timestamps) | Every transaction |
| Buyer acknowledgment | Signed receipt or electronic confirmation on file | Every transaction |
| Post-disclosure amendments | Any material changes communicated and documented before closing | As needed, verify at final review |

**Brokers should conduct quarterly file audits rather than relying on year-end reviews.** A systematic quarterly check catches problems while transactions are still active and corrections are still possible. Waiting until a file is closed and archived means any deficiency becomes a latent liability rather than a fixable oversight.

Training matters, but systems matter more. Agents who rely on memory to manage disclosure timelines [will inevitably miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines). Brokerages should implement transaction management workflows that automatically flag missing disclosure documents and alert both the agent and the supervising broker when a file is incomplete past the contract execution date.

DREC has the authority under 24 Del. C. § 2913 to impose civil penalties up to $5,000 per violation, issue reprimands, suspend licenses, or revoke licenses for serious or repeated offenses. Beyond DREC enforcement, a buyer who suffers damages from a disclosure failure can pursue civil remedies including rescission of the contract and compensatory damages. The cost of a single undisclosed environmental issue—a leaking UST, for example—can dwarf the commission earned on the transaction many times over.

## Liability Exposure When Agents Fail to Comply

Agent liability in Delaware disclosure cases falls into three categories: statutory violations, breach of fiduciary duty, and common law fraud or misrepresentation. A statutory violation under the Seller's Disclosure Act can result in the buyer voiding the contract and recovering damages. Breach of fiduciary duty claims arise when an agent knew or should have known about a material defect and failed to ensure it was disclosed.

Fraud and misrepresentation claims carry the heaviest consequences. If a court finds that an agent actively concealed a known defect—or made affirmative statements contradicting what the agent knew—the agent faces compensatory damages, potential punitive damages, and near-certain DREC disciplinary proceedings. Errors and omissions insurance will cover some exposure, but most E&O policies exclude intentional misrepresentation.

The practical takeaway is straightforward: document everything, disclose everything known, and when in doubt, disclose anyway. The legal risk of over-disclosure is virtually nonexistent. The legal risk of under-disclosure can be career-ending. Agents who build [strong checklist habits](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) around disclosure obligations protect themselves, their brokers, and their clients simultaneously.
