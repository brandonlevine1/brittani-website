## Understanding Delaware Title & Closing Procedures 2026

Delaware's closing process differs from neighboring states in ways that trip up even experienced agents. Getting a handle on **Delaware title & closing procedures 2026** is not optional — it is the difference between a smooth settlement and a derailed transaction. Every residential deal in the state runs through a framework shaped by specific statutes, mandatory attorney involvement, and forms published by the Delaware Association of Realtors (DAR).

The state's attorney-close requirement is the single biggest structural difference agents encounter when working across the Mid-Atlantic. Unlike Pennsylvania or Maryland, where title companies routinely handle closings independently, Delaware law requires a licensed attorney to examine title, prepare settlement documents, and supervise the closing itself. This requirement traces back to advisory opinions from the Delaware Supreme Court interpreting the unauthorized practice of law under the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of Professional Conduct, Rule 5.5.

Agents who ignore or misunderstand this structure risk more than a delayed closing. They risk practicing law without a license — a serious regulatory and civil liability issue.

## The Attorney-Close Requirement and What It Means for Agents

**Delaware is one of a handful of states where an attorney must be physically or virtually present at settlement.** Title companies can issue policies, but they cannot conduct closings without attorney oversight. The distinction matters because agents sometimes assume that working with a title company alone satisfies the requirement.

The Delaware Department of Justice and the Office of Disciplinary Counsel have both taken the position that non-attorney settlement activity constitutes the unauthorized practice of law. Agents who direct clients to close without proper attorney involvement expose themselves and their brokerages to liability under 24 Del. C. Chapter 29, which governs real estate licensing, and potentially under Delaware's criminal unauthorized-practice statutes.

From a practical standpoint, the attorney's role includes certifying the title search, preparing the deed, reviewing the settlement statement, handling the escrow disbursement, and recording documents with the appropriate county Recorder of Deeds office — whether that is New Castle County, Kent County, or Sussex County.

## Key Forms and Documents Agents Must Know

DAR publishes the standard forms used in virtually every residential transaction in Delaware. Agents should be fluent in the current versions of these documents, as outdated forms are a frequent source of compliance problems.

| Form Number | Document Name | Primary Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| DAR Form 10 | Standard Agreement of Sale | Governs the terms of the residential purchase contract |
| DAR Form 35 | Seller's Disclosure of Real Property Condition | Mandatory disclosure under 6 Del. C. Section 2572 |
| DAR Form 13 | Lead-Based Paint Disclosure | Required for homes built before 1978 per federal law |
| DAR Form 306 | Buyer Agency Agreement | Formalizes the buyer-agent relationship post-2024 practice changes |
| DAR Form 12 | Counter-Proposal | Used for offer negotiations modifying DAR Form 10 |

DAR Form 10 is the backbone of the transaction. It contains the contingency deadlines, the earnest money requirements, and the default provisions that govern what happens when a party fails to perform. Agents who freelance addenda instead of using the approved DAR forms open their brokerages to errors-and-omissions claims.

The Seller's Disclosure (DAR Form 35) must comply with the Delaware Residential Property Disclosure Act, codified at 6 Del. C. Chapter 25, Subchapter II. Sellers of residential property with four or fewer units must provide this disclosure before the buyer is bound. Agents who allow a contract to go fully executed before the disclosure is delivered are creating a problem that can unwind the deal.

## Transfer Tax, Recording Fees, and the Cost Split

Delaware's realty transfer tax is one of the highest on the East Coast. The state imposes a 4% tax on the value of real property transferred, codified under [30 Del. C. Chapter 54](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title30/c054/index.html). By custom and often by contract, this is split 2% to the buyer and 2% to the seller.

First-time homebuyers purchasing a principal residence may qualify for a reduced total rate of 3%, split 1.5% each. Agents need to confirm eligibility early in the transaction because the attorney handling the closing will require documentation — typically an affidavit — before applying the reduced rate.

| Fee Type | Typical Amount | Paid By |
|---|---|---|
| State Realty Transfer Tax | 4% of sale price (3% for qualifying first-time buyers) | Split equally unless negotiated otherwise |
| County Recording Fee | $33 for first page, $10 each additional page | Buyer (for deed); Seller (for releases) |
| Title Search Fee | $200 - $400 | Buyer |
| Attorney/Settlement Fee | $500 - $1,200 | Varies by negotiation |
| Title Insurance (Owner's Policy) | Varies by sale price and underwriter | Buyer |

Recording fees are set by county and follow the fee schedule under 9 Del. C. Section 9605. Agents who estimate closing costs without verifying current county fee schedules cause problems at the settlement table when numbers do not match the preliminary figures.

This cost-splitting structure is one area where agents often struggle to advise clients clearly. If you are looking at how [other states handle the buyer-seller cost divide](/blog/west-virginia-who-pays-closing-costs-2026), the contrast with Delaware's high transfer tax environment is stark.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make in Delaware Closings

Five errors show up repeatedly in Delaware transactions, and each one carries real consequences.

**First, agents miss earnest money deposit deadlines.** DAR Form 10 specifies the timeframe for depositing earnest money into escrow. Missing this deadline is a contractual breach. Sellers can terminate the agreement and pursue damages, and the listing agent's brokerage may face a complaint with the Delaware Real Estate Commission (DREC) for failing to ensure compliance.

Second, agents fail to confirm the buyer's attorney selection early enough. Because Delaware requires an attorney at closing, waiting until the final week to identify and engage settlement counsel creates bottlenecks. Title searches take 10 to 14 business days under normal conditions. A late start compresses every downstream deadline.

Third, agents submit outdated or incorrect DAR forms. DAR updates its forms periodically, and using a prior-year version can introduce clause language that conflicts with current law or regulatory guidance. DREC expects agents to use current forms, and using outdated versions is a finding in audits.

Fourth, agents neglect to verify the seller's disclosure delivery timing. Under 6 Del. C. Section 2573, the buyer has the right to rescind the contract if the disclosure is not provided before the agreement becomes binding. Agents who treat the disclosure as a closing formality rather than a pre-contract requirement risk deal cancellation with no recourse.

Fifth, agents provide settlement cost estimates without coordinating with the closing attorney. In Delaware, the attorney controls the final settlement figures. Agents who hand buyers or sellers informal spreadsheets with inaccurate numbers undermine client trust and can trigger RESPA concerns if those estimates look like official loan disclosures. Understanding [why deals break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) often comes back to these types of miscommunications.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under 24 Del. C. Section 2912, which requires designated brokers to supervise all licensed activity conducted under their brokerage. DREC audits examine specific areas of closing-related compliance, and brokers who do not proactively monitor these areas are exposed.

**Escrow account management is the single highest-risk area for brokerages in Delaware.** DREC requires that earnest money deposits be placed in a properly maintained escrow account within the timeframe specified in the contract. Brokers must reconcile escrow accounts monthly and maintain records for at least five years. Violations can result in license suspension, fines up to $5,000 per infraction, and civil liability to harmed parties.

Brokers should also audit form usage across their agents at least quarterly. Confirm that every active transaction file contains the current version of DAR Form 10, that DAR Form 35 was delivered on the correct timeline, and that any counter-proposals use DAR Form 12 rather than informal emails or texts treated as binding amendments.

Transaction file completeness is another audit priority. DREC requires brokerages to maintain a complete file for every transaction, including all signed agreements, disclosures, correspondence, and closing documents. A missing file during a DREC audit is treated as a supervision failure, and the broker — not the individual agent — bears primary responsibility.

> "The broker shall exercise reasonable and adequate supervision over the activities of all licensees affiliated with the broker." — 24 Del. C. Section 2912(a)(3)

Brokers managing high transaction volumes should consider whether their current tracking systems actually flag deadline misses and form version errors in real time, or whether they are relying on agents to self-report problems after the fact. That distinction is often where compliance gaps hide.

## Wire Fraud and Escrow Security in Delaware Transactions

Wire fraud targeting real estate closings continues to escalate nationally, and Delaware is not immune. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported over $145 million in real estate wire fraud losses in 2024 alone. Delaware's attorney-close model adds a layer of protection — the closing attorney controls disbursement — but agents remain a common point of compromise.

Agents should never send wire instructions via email without a verification callback to a known phone number. DREC has not yet issued a standalone wire fraud rule, but the general duty of care under 24 Del. C. Chapter 29 and the fiduciary obligations in agency relationships create liability exposure for agents who handle wire details carelessly. For a deeper look at verification protocols, [wire fraud prevention practices](/blog/west-virginia-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) apply across state lines with minor procedural differences.

Brokerages should have a written wire fraud prevention policy that every agent acknowledges annually. This is not just best practice — it is becoming a baseline expectation in E&O underwriting.

Agents are required to complete 21 hours of continuing education every two years, and at least three of those hours must cover Delaware law topics, including closing procedures and fiduciary duties.

The rules governing Delaware closings are not static. Legislative sessions can adjust transfer tax rates, modify disclosure requirements, or change escrow handling standards with relatively little advance notice.
