## Understanding New Hampshire FIRPTA Withholding Rules for Foreign Sellers 2026

The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act remains one of the most misunderstood compliance obligations in residential real estate, and New Hampshire transactions are no exception. Agents working under the New Hampshire firpta withholding rules for foreign sellers 2026 framework need to understand that while FIRPTA is federal law (26 U.S.C. Sections 1445 and 897), the practical mechanics of compliance fall squarely on the parties at the closing table—including you. New Hampshire does not impose a separate state-level withholding tax on foreign sellers, but that does not eliminate the federal obligation or your exposure if it goes unfulfilled.

**FIRPTA requires that when a foreign person sells U.S. real property, the buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sales price and remit it to the IRS.** This is not optional. It is not negotiable between the parties. The withholding rate stands at 15% for properties sold above $1,000,000, drops to 10% for properties between $300,001 and $1,000,000 where the buyer intends to use the property as a residence, and can be zero for sales at or below $300,000 with a buyer who will use it as a primary residence.

New Hampshire's closing process typically involves an attorney or title company handling settlement, and that entity will expect you to have flagged foreign seller status well before the closing date. The New Hampshire Real Estate Commission (NHREC) does not publish FIRPTA-specific guidance, but its general standards of practice under RSA 331-A require licensees to act with reasonable skill, care, and diligence—which includes recognizing a FIRPTA-triggering transaction when one lands on your desk.

## The Federal Framework Applied to New Hampshire Closings

Unlike states such as California or Hawaii that impose their own withholding on non-resident sellers, New Hampshire has no state income tax on earned income and no capital gains tax. This simplifies things slightly—you are only dealing with the federal FIRPTA withholding, not a layered state requirement. But it also means agents sometimes forget FIRPTA entirely because there is no state analog reminding them.

The core forms involved are IRS Form 8288 (U.S. Withholding Tax Return for Certain Dispositions by Foreign Persons) and IRS Form 8288-A (Statement of Withholding on Dispositions by Foreign Persons of U.S. Real Property Interests). Both must be filed within 20 days of the closing date. The buyer is the "transferee" responsible for this filing, but in New Hampshire practice the closing attorney almost always handles the preparation and submission on the buyer's behalf.

If the seller wants a reduced withholding, they must file IRS Form 8288-B (Application for Withholding Certificate for Dispositions by Foreign Persons) in advance of closing. Processing time for 8288-B applications currently runs 90 days or longer, so this is not a last-minute solution. Agents representing foreign sellers should initiate this discussion at listing intake, not at the inspection contingency deadline.

## Identifying Foreign Sellers: What Counts and What Does Not

A "foreign person" under FIRPTA includes any non-resident alien individual, a foreign corporation, a foreign partnership, a foreign trust, or a foreign estate. Permanent residents holding a green card are generally treated as U.S. persons for tax purposes and fall outside the FIRPTA withholding requirement. Dual citizens may or may not trigger FIRPTA depending on their residency status and tax elections.

**The standard method for a seller to certify non-foreign status is the FIRPTA affidavit—also called a non-foreign affidavit or transferor's certification—provided under Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-2(b).** This document, which is not a standardized IRS form but rather a closing-document affidavit, must include the seller's name, U.S. taxpayer identification number, and home address, along with a declaration under penalty of perjury that the seller is not a foreign person. If the seller provides this certification and the buyer has no actual knowledge that it is false, the buyer's withholding obligation is relieved.

New Hampshire closing attorneys routinely include this affidavit in their standard closing packages. The risk for agents arises when the seller refuses to sign, cannot provide a U.S. TIN, or when you have reason to believe the certification is inaccurate. In those situations, you cannot simply proceed as if everything is fine.

## Agent Liability: What Happens When You Fail to Comply

Many agents assume FIRPTA liability rests entirely with the buyer and the closing attorney. That assumption is dangerously wrong. Under 26 U.S.C. Section 1445(d), any agent of the transferor or transferee who has actual knowledge that a FIRPTA certification is false can be held liable for the tax that should have been withheld.

| Scenario | Primary Liable Party | Agent Exposure |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Buyer fails to withhold, no agent knowledge | Buyer | None |
| Agent knows seller is foreign, closing proceeds without withholding | Buyer + Agent | Up to 15% of gross sales price |
| Seller provides false affidavit, agent has no knowledge | Seller (criminal penalties) | None |
| Agent facilitates false affidavit knowingly | Seller + Agent | Full withholding amount plus potential fraud charges |

The IRS can assess the full withholding amount against an agent found liable, plus interest and penalties. Beyond IRS exposure, the NHREC could investigate a licensee under RSA 331-A:26 for conduct demonstrating incompetence or untrustworthiness—a violation that can result in license suspension, revocation, or fines up to $2,000 per violation as set forth in RSA 331-A:28.

Deal cancellation is also a practical risk. If a buyer discovers at or after closing that FIRPTA should have been withheld but was not, the buyer faces a potential 15% liability on the full purchase price. Some buyers have pursued rescission claims against sellers and negligence actions against agents in these situations. The cost of defending such a claim dwarfs the effort of getting it right up front.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make with FIRPTA in New Hampshire

**The first and most frequent error is failing to ask about foreign status at listing intake.** Many agents treat seller nationality as something that will "come out at closing," but by then it may be too late to apply for a reduced withholding certificate or structure the transaction properly. Your listing agreement discussions should include a direct question about the seller's U.S. person status.

The second mistake is confusing residency with citizenship. A seller who lives in New Hampshire full-time, pays property taxes, and speaks fluent English may still be a non-resident alien for federal tax purposes. Immigration status and tax status are separate legal concepts, and agents who assume otherwise create serious liability exposure.

Third, agents routinely underestimate the timeline for Form 8288-B processing. Telling a foreign seller that "we'll handle it at closing" is a recipe for disaster when the IRS takes three months to issue a withholding certificate. By then, the buyer has either had to withhold the full 15% or the deal has collapsed. Similar [deadline management failures](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) plague transactions across multiple compliance areas.

Fourth, some agents in New Hampshire incorrectly believe that the absence of a state withholding tax means there is no withholding obligation at all. This confusion between state and federal requirements has led to closings where no withholding occurs and the buyer later receives an IRS notice.

Fifth, listing agents sometimes advise foreign sellers to simply "sign the affidavit anyway" to avoid complicating the deal. This is not just bad advice—it constitutes facilitating fraud and can expose the agent to criminal liability under 26 U.S.C. Section 7206.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Designated brokers and managing brokers in New Hampshire carry supervisory responsibility under RSA 331-A:16. If an agent in your office mishandles FIRPTA compliance, your brokerage's reputation and license are on the line alongside theirs.

| Audit Point | What to Check | Frequency |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Listing intake forms | Does the form include a foreign seller status question? | Every listing |
| Transaction file review | Is a FIRPTA affidavit or withholding documentation present? | Before closing |
| Agent training records | Has the agent completed FIRPTA training in the past 24 months? | Annually |
| Closing attorney coordination | Did the file confirm withholding was addressed at settlement? | Post-closing audit |
| Escalation protocols | Is there a documented process for flagging foreign seller transactions? | Policy review annually |

Brokers should require that every listing file contain either a signed non-foreign affidavit or documentation showing that FIRPTA withholding was properly handled at closing. This is not excessive—it is the same standard you would apply to [wire fraud prevention protocols](/blog/wisconsin-wire-fraud-prevention-2026) or earnest money documentation.

Consider implementing a policy that requires agents to notify the broker or transaction coordinator within 48 hours of identifying a foreign seller. This gives the brokerage time to involve tax counsel, coordinate with the closing attorney, and ensure the 8288-B timeline is addressed before it becomes a closing emergency. Firms that rely on [checklists rather than memory](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) catch these issues far more reliably than those depending on individual agent awareness.

## Practical Steps for Agents Representing Foreign Sellers

When you identify a foreign seller, your immediate next step is to refer them to a tax attorney or CPA who specializes in international tax. You are not qualified to provide tax advice, and attempting to do so violates both common sense and NHREC standards of practice. Your role is identification, disclosure, and coordination—not tax planning.

> "The transferee's agent, the transferor's agent, or the settlement officer ... shall not be liable for failure to comply with the requirements of section 1445(e) if such person relies on the written statement of the transferor that the transferor is not a foreign person." — Treasury Regulation Section 1.1445-2(b)(2)

That regulatory protection only applies when you have no reason to doubt the certification. If you know the seller moved to New Hampshire from abroad two years ago and holds a work visa, relying on a signed affidavit claiming U.S. person status is not a defense—it is willful blindness.

Coordinate early with the closing attorney. In New Hampshire, attorneys at firms handling real estate settlements are accustomed to FIRPTA issues, but they need lead time. Provide them with the seller's status information as soon as you have it confirmed. Make sure the purchase and sale agreement addresses FIRPTA explicitly, either through a standard addendum or through contract language specifying the withholding allocation and timeline.

## The Intersection of FIRPTA and New Hampshire's Interest and Dividends Tax

New Hampshire does impose an Interest and Dividends Tax under RSA 77, but this tax is being phased out and does not apply to capital gains from real property sales. Some agents confuse this tax with a potential state withholding obligation, but it has no bearing on FIRPTA or on the sale of real estate by a foreign person. The [IRS FIRPTA guidance page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/firpta-withholding) remains the authoritative source for current federal withholding rates and procedures.

Foreign sellers who are also subject to federal capital gains tax (which is separate from the withholding) will file their U.S. tax return and claim credit for the amount withheld. Any over-withholding results in a refund from the IRS. This is why many foreign sellers pursue the 8288-B application—to avoid having 15% of the gross sales price locked up with the IRS for months while their return processes.
