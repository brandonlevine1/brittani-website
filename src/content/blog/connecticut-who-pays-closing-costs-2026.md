## Connecticut Who Pays Closing Costs — Buyer vs. Seller 2026: The Statutory Framework

The question of Connecticut who pays closing costs — buyer vs. seller 2026 trips up agents more often than it should, particularly because the state blends statute-mandated obligations with long-standing custom that many practitioners treat as law. Connecticut is an attorney-closing state, which means the allocation conversation happens earlier, involves more parties, and carries sharper liability exposure than in states where title companies quarterback the settlement process.

Connecticut General Statutes § 12-494 through § 12-504 govern the conveyance tax, the single largest closing cost that state law assigns to a specific party. Beyond that statutory carve-out, nearly every other fee is negotiable between buyer and seller, documented through the contract itself. The Connecticut Association of Realtors (CAR) Residential Purchase Agreement — Form RCA-2025, expected to remain in use through 2026 — provides blank fields for parties to designate who bears specific costs, but it does not impose defaults.

Agents who assume that "customary" equals "required" put their license and their client's deal at risk. The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection, which oversees real estate licensing under [Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-311 through § 20-329](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_392.htm), has disciplined agents for misrepresenting cost obligations during negotiations.

## The Conveyance Tax: Connecticut's Only Statutorily Assigned Cost

**The conveyance tax is the one closing cost that Connecticut law explicitly assigns to the seller.** Under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-494, the person conveying real property — the grantor — owes the tax, and the town clerk cannot record the deed without proof of payment. The rate structure as of 2026 applies as follows for residential property not classified as the seller's primary residence at the time of sale:

| Sale Price Bracket | Rate (Non-Primary Residence) | Rate (Primary Residence) |
|---|---|---|
| First $800,000 | 0.75% | 0.75% |
| $800,001 – $2,500,000 | 1.25% | 1.25% |
| Above $2,500,000 | 2.25% | 2.25% |

For sellers whose property qualifies as their primary residence and sells below $800,000, the effective tax is 0.75% of the sale price. Municipal surcharges of up to 0.25% apply in certain towns under enabling legislation, so agents must verify the local rate before estimating net proceeds.

The buyer has zero statutory obligation for conveyance tax. Any contract provision shifting this cost to the buyer is legally permissible but must be explicitly negotiated and documented — it will not happen by default.

## Customary Buyer Costs in Connecticut

Buyers in Connecticut typically shoulder the following costs, not because statute demands it, but because the CAR purchase agreement and decades of practice have established these allocations as the baseline negotiation position.

Title search fees run between $300 and $600 depending on the complexity of the chain of title. Title insurance — an owner's policy protecting the buyer — ranges from $1,500 to $4,000 on a median-priced Connecticut home. The buyer also pays for their own attorney, with fees commonly between $750 and $1,500 for a straightforward residential closing.

Lender-related costs fall entirely on the buyer's side: appraisal fees, credit report charges, origination points, and prepaid interest. Recording fees for the mortgage deed are the buyer's responsibility under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 7-34, which sets the recording fee schedule for town clerks statewide. Agents listing estimated buyer costs on a net sheet should pull current recording fees directly from the relevant town clerk rather than relying on outdated figures from prior transactions.

## Customary Seller Costs Beyond the Conveyance Tax

**Sellers typically pay their own attorney fees, the real estate commission, and any municipal charges tied to the transfer.** In many Connecticut towns, the seller is responsible for obtaining a municipal certificate of compliance (often called a "smoke/CO certificate") before closing, with fees ranging from $75 to $200.

Outstanding property taxes must be settled through the closing. Connecticut operates on a fiscal year running July 1 to June 30, and the seller owes prorated taxes through the date of closing. The settlement attorney calculates this credit, but listing agents who fail to account for it in their Seller's Estimated Net Proceeds sheet create problems that surface hours before the closing appointment.

| Cost Category | Typically Paid By | Approximate Range |
|---|---|---|
| Conveyance tax | Seller (statutory) | 0.75% – 2.25% of price |
| Real estate commission | Seller (per listing agreement) | Negotiated percentage |
| Seller attorney fees | Seller | $750 – $1,500 |
| Title search and insurance | Buyer | $1,800 – $4,600 |
| Buyer attorney fees | Buyer | $750 – $1,500 |
| Recording fees (deed) | Buyer or Seller (varies) | $120 – $250 |
| Recording fees (mortgage) | Buyer | $120 – $250 |
| Appraisal | Buyer | $400 – $600 |
| Municipal compliance cert | Seller | $75 – $200 |

Deed preparation costs historically fell to the seller, since the seller's attorney drafts the warranty deed. This remains the dominant practice, though some transactions shift this cost by agreement.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Closing Cost Allocation

**Mistake one: telling clients that closing cost splits are "standard" or "non-negotiable."** Connecticut law does not prescribe most allocations. Agents who present a cost breakdown as fixed rather than customary expose themselves to misrepresentation claims under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-328(6), which prohibits licensees from making false promises or misrepresentations likely to influence a party's decision.

Mistake two: failing to document seller concessions properly on the CAR purchase agreement. When a seller agrees to pay a portion of the buyer's closing costs, this must appear in the contract — not merely in an email thread between agents. Form RCA includes a specific line for seller concessions, and appraisers will scrutinize that figure. If the concession appears inflated relative to the sale price, the appraisal may come in low, and the agent who failed to warn the client about this risk owns the fallout.

Mistake three: neglecting the [impact of missed deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) on cost allocation. Connecticut contracts routinely include time-is-of-the-essence language. When a closing date passes because one side failed to produce required documents, the other party may incur per-diem interest charges, rate-lock extension fees, or storage costs. The responsible party's agent should have flagged the deadline risk weeks before it materialized.

Mistake four: confusing the conveyance tax with the controlling interest transfer tax under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-638. When property is held in an LLC or trust and more than a 50% interest transfers, the controlling interest tax applies at the same rates as the conveyance tax but is reported on a different form (OP-236CT). Agents involved in entity transfers who ignore this distinction create tax liability surprises for their clients.

Mistake five: omitting the buyer's Connecticut real property transfer fee. While this is technically folded into the conveyance tax payment process, certain municipalities assess a separate buyer surcharge. Agents operating in towns like Stamford, Bridgeport, and Hartford should verify whether any local buyer fee applies.

## What Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Brokers supervising agents in Connecticut have a statutory duty under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-312a to ensure that licensees under their supervision comply with all applicable laws and regulations. When it comes to closing costs, this translates into three audit points that should appear in every brokerage's compliance checklist.

First, brokers should confirm that every listing agent provides a written Seller's Estimated Net Proceeds sheet that reflects the correct conveyance tax rate for the specific municipality. A generic statewide estimate is insufficient when municipal surcharges exist. Second, brokers must verify that seller concessions documented in the purchase contract match what appears on the final Closing Disclosure. Discrepancies between contract terms and CD figures trigger lender red flags and can delay or kill closings.

Third, brokers should audit whether their agents are improperly guaranteeing closing cost figures early in the transaction. The CAR Code of Ethics and Connecticut licensing regulations require that estimates be clearly labeled as such. An agent who tells a buyer "your closing costs will be $8,200" without qualification is making a representation that, if inaccurate by a material amount, could result in a complaint to the Department of Consumer Protection.

Failure to supervise adequately can result in the broker's own license facing disciplinary action, including fines up to $25,000 per violation under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 20-328a. The Department of Consumer Protection's Real Estate Commission has [historically increased enforcement](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/License-Division/Real-Estate-Services) against brokerages that demonstrate systemic compliance failures rather than isolated agent errors.

## Negotiation Strategies Agents Should Understand

The post-NAR settlement environment has made closing cost conversations more complex. Buyer agents in Connecticut must now articulate their value proposition clearly, and one area where skilled agents differentiate themselves is in structuring closing cost credits that benefit their client without torpedoing the deal.

A seller credit toward the buyer's costs effectively reduces the buyer's cash-to-close requirement. Agents who understand how to structure these credits within lender guidelines — Fannie Mae caps interested-party contributions at 3% for high-LTV loans and 6% for loans at or below 75% LTV — protect their buyers from appraisal and underwriting complications. Agents who [understand where deals typically break down](/blog/where-deals-break-down) know that poorly structured concessions are a leading cause of late-stage contract failures.

For listing agents, offering a closing cost credit instead of a price reduction preserves the comparable sale price for neighborhood valuations while still making the deal work. This is a tactical distinction that experienced agents deploy regularly and newer agents often miss entirely.
