## Understanding Oregon Commission & Licensing Rules 2026

The Oregon Real Estate Agency (OREA) enforces a regulatory framework that directly controls how agents earn, split, and collect commissions—and the **Oregon commission & licensing rules 2026** carry enforcement teeth that can end careers. Every active licensee working under ORS Chapter 696 needs to understand not just the spirit of these laws but their specific mechanics: which forms to file, which deadlines to meet, and which mistakes trigger fines or license action. This guide breaks down the current statutory requirements, the forms you cannot skip, and the audit obligations that fall on principal brokers.

Oregon distinguishes itself from many states by requiring all compensation—regardless of negotiated amount—to flow through a principal broker's trust account or books before reaching an agent. This isn't a suggestion. It's codified in [ORS 696.290](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors696.html), and violation carries civil penalties, license suspension, or both.

## How Commission Structures Work Under ORS 696

**Oregon law does not set commission rates, but it tightly controls how commissions are documented and disbursed.** The state has never imposed a rate floor or ceiling. What OREA does regulate is the written agreement requirement, the flow of funds, and the disclosure obligations attached to any compensation arrangement.

Under ORS 696.290(1), no licensee may bring a legal action to collect compensation for real estate activity unless the agreement is in writing and signed by the person obligated to pay. This applies to listing agreements, buyer representation agreements, and any referral fee arrangement between brokerages. Oral agreements are void and unenforceable in Oregon courts—period.

The form most Oregon brokerages use for seller representation is the Oregon Association of Realtors (OAR) Exclusive Right to Sell Listing Agreement. For buyer representation, agents typically use the OAR Buyer Representation Agreement, which was revised in 2024 to align with post-NAR settlement requirements around upfront commission disclosure. Both forms include fields for specific compensation amounts or percentages and require signatures before services begin.

## Licensing Tiers and Renewal Requirements

Oregon operates a two-tier licensing structure: the real estate broker license (formerly called "salesperson" pre-2010) and the principal broker license. The distinction matters because only principal brokers can operate a brokerage, supervise other licensees, and disburse commission funds.

| Requirement | Broker License | Principal Broker License |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-license Education | 150 hours | 150 hours + additional 30 hours |
| Experience Required | None | 3 years active practice |
| CE Renewal Cycle | 30 hours / 2 years | 30 hours / 2 years |
| Supervision Authority | None (must be supervised) | Full supervisory authority |
| Commission Disbursement | Cannot disburse | Sole authority to disburse |

Renewal cycles run on a two-year schedule tied to the licensee's birth month. The 30-hour continuing education requirement must include a 3-hour Oregon Agency Law course, a 3-hour Oregon Real Estate Law course, and a 1-hour OREA Commissioner's Update course. Missing your renewal deadline doesn't grant a grace period for active practice—your license immediately becomes inactive, and any deals closed during an inactive period generate unenforceable commission claims.

OREA processes renewals through its eLicense online portal. The renewal fee for brokers is $300 biennially as of 2026. Principal brokers pay the same fee but carry additional reporting obligations regarding supervised licensees.

## Disclosure Obligations Tied to Compensation

**Oregon's Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet must be provided at first contact, before any substantive real estate discussion occurs.** This requirement under OAR 863-015-0215 is not optional and not waivable. The pamphlet discloses the types of agency relationships available and must be acknowledged in writing by the consumer.

Beyond initial disclosure, the 2024-2025 regulatory updates aligned with NAR settlement terms require Oregon agents to disclose buyer-side compensation in writing before touring properties. The OAR Buyer Representation Agreement now serves as the primary vehicle for this disclosure. Agents who show property without a signed buyer representation agreement risk violating both state law and MLS participation rules enforced by RMLS (Regional Multiple Listing Service) in the Portland metro area and other Oregon MLS systems.

Commission-sharing arrangements between cooperating brokerages must also be documented. Oregon does not require a specific OREA form for inter-brokerage compensation agreements, but the arrangement must be traceable in the principal broker's records. OREA auditors will look for written evidence of any compensation split during a brokerage audit.

## Common Mistakes That Trigger OREA Enforcement

Five errors account for the majority of commission-related enforcement actions in Oregon. Each one is avoidable with basic compliance hygiene.

**First: accepting compensation directly from a consumer or cooperating brokerage.** ORS 696.290(1) is absolute—funds must route through your principal broker. An agent who accepts a referral check mailed to their personal address has committed a licensable offense, even if the amount is modest and the intent innocent. OREA has issued suspensions for exactly this scenario.

Second: operating on an expired or inactive license. Agents who miss their renewal deadline and continue showing property, writing offers, or negotiating terms are engaging in unlicensed activity under ORS 696.020. The penalty structure includes civil fines up to $5,000 per transaction and potential criminal misdemeanor charges for repeated violations.

Third: failing to provide the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet before substantive contact. OREA's complaint records show this violation appears in roughly 30% of consumer-filed grievances. The remedy is simple—carry physical copies and send digital versions via your transaction management system—but agents routinely skip it during casual conversations that escalate into property discussions.

Fourth: using outdated forms that omit required disclosure language. Oregon form requirements evolve, and the 2024-2025 revisions to buyer representation forms are a prime example. Agents pulling old templates from personal files rather than downloading current versions from OAR's Form Library create liability exposure for themselves and their principal broker.

Fifth: failing to document commission modifications mid-transaction. When a seller agrees to reduce or increase the buyer-agent compensation after an offer is accepted, that modification must be captured in writing—typically via an addendum to the listing agreement or a separate compensation agreement. Verbal modifications are unenforceable and create E&O claim exposure. Agents who [miss critical documentation deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) in fast-moving transactions often discover this problem at closing.

## What Principal Brokers Must Audit and Enforce

Oregon's regulatory framework places primary compliance responsibility on the principal broker. Under ORS 696.301, principal brokers are accountable for the real estate activity of every licensee they supervise. This is not passive oversight—OREA expects documented systems.

| Audit Area | Frequency | Statutory Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Trust account reconciliation | Monthly | OAR 863-025-0025 |
| Licensee CE compliance tracking | Biennial / ongoing | ORS 696.174 |
| Signed agency disclosure confirmation | Per transaction | OAR 863-015-0215 |
| Buyer representation agreements on file | Per transaction | ORS 696.290 |
| Commission disbursement documentation | Per transaction | ORS 696.290(1) |
| Advertising compliance review | Quarterly minimum | OAR 863-015-0270 |

Principal brokers who delegate transaction file review to office managers or transaction coordinators still bear personal liability for deficiencies. OREA does not accept "I didn't know" as a defense during an audit. The agency's enforcement division conducts both complaint-triggered investigations and random audits of active brokerages.

**One area that catches brokerages off guard: advertising compliance.** OAR 863-015-0270 requires that all advertising by a licensee include the licensed business name of the brokerage. Social media posts, website pages, and even yard signs that display only the agent's name or team name without the brokerage name violate this rule. OREA has escalated enforcement of digital advertising violations since 2024.

For brokerages running high transaction volumes, maintaining compliant files across dozens of agents is a significant operational burden. This is exactly [where deal breakdowns occur](/blog/where-deals-break-down) when compliance systems rely on memory rather than automated tracking.

## Penalties and Liability When Compliance Fails

OREA's enforcement options range from letters of reprimand to license revocation. The agency also has statutory authority to impose civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation under ORS 696.990. Multiple violations within a single transaction can stack, creating five-figure penalty exposure from a single deal.

Beyond OREA action, commission disputes that reach civil court face an additional hurdle: Oregon courts will not enforce any commission agreement that doesn't strictly comply with ORS 696.290's writing requirement. An agent who performed months of work for a buyer but failed to secure a signed buyer representation agreement has no legal remedy to collect payment. Oregon case law—most notably *Holtan v. Cook*, 249 Or. 580 (1968)—has consistently upheld the writing requirement without exception.

> "No action shall be brought...to collect compensation for professional real estate activity...unless the agreement for such compensation is in writing and signed by the person obligated to pay." — ORS 696.290(1)

E&O insurance carriers in Oregon have also tightened underwriting for agents with OREA compliance history. A single formal complaint—even one that results in only a reprimand—can increase premiums or trigger policy non-renewal at the next cycle.

Transaction cancellation is another real-world consequence. Buyers and sellers who discover mid-transaction that their agent's license was inactive or that required disclosures were missing may exercise cancellation rights under the purchase agreement. The ripple effects—lost earnest money, delayed closings, damaged client relationships—extend far beyond the regulatory penalty itself.
