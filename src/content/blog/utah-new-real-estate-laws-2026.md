## The Biggest Regulatory Shift Utah Agents Have Seen in a Decade

The phrase **Utah new real estate laws 2026** is not just a search query — it represents the most consequential batch of statutory updates since the state overhauled its licensing structure in 2011. Three bills signed by Governor Cox between March and May of this year rewrite the rules on buyer representation, seller disclosures, and wire fraud liability. Every licensed agent and principal broker in the state needs to understand what changed, what forms are affected, and what the penalties look like for noncompliance.

The Utah Division of Real Estate, which sits under the Department of Commerce, published its final administrative rules in June. Those rules took effect July 1, with a grace period extending to September 1, 2026 for certain buyer-agreement provisions. That grace period is not an invitation to wait. It is a countdown.

## Buyer-Broker Agreements Are Now Mandatory Statewide

**House Bill 287 codifies the requirement for a written buyer-broker agreement before an agent provides substantive real estate services to any buyer.** The bill amends Utah Code 61-2f-401.5 and draws a hard line: no touring, no CMAs delivered to a prospective buyer, no offer drafting — none of it — without a signed agreement in place.

The Utah Association of Realtors has released a revised Exclusive Buyer-Broker Agreement (Form 200B, revised May 2026) and a Non-Exclusive Buyer-Broker Agreement (Form 200NB) to satisfy this mandate. Both forms require agents to disclose their compensation structure in plain dollar or percentage terms, consistent with the broader post-NAR settlement environment addressed [in our coverage of similar buyer agency shifts](/blog/wisconsin-buyer-agency-agreement-requirements-2026).

The agreement must contain four elements to be valid under the statute: a defined term of representation not exceeding 120 days, an explicit statement of the agent's duties, a compensation disclosure section, and a cancellation clause outlining the buyer's right to terminate. Missing any one of those elements does not just create a voidable contract — it triggers a presumption against the agent in any subsequent commission dispute.

## Seller Disclosure Overhaul: Form 25.1 Replaces 25.0

Senate Bill 98 expanded the scope of the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure to include seven new categories. The old Form 25.0 is officially retired as of September 1, 2026. Its replacement, Form 25.1, adds mandatory fields for known flooding history (regardless of FEMA zone), past insurance claims, unpermitted improvements, and solar lease or PACE lien encumbrances.

**Agents who continue using Form 25.0 after September 1 are not just outdated — they are out of compliance.** The Division has confirmed in its July bulletin that use of a superseded disclosure form will not satisfy the statutory requirement, meaning the transaction effectively proceeds without valid disclosure. That exposes both the seller and the listing agent to buyer rescission rights and civil penalties.

| Feature | Old Form 25.0 | New Form 25.1 (Sept 2026) |
|---------|---------------|---------------------------|
| Flooding history | Optional note | Mandatory specific field |
| Insurance claims (5 yr) | Not addressed | Required disclosure |
| Unpermitted improvements | General "known defects" | Standalone checkbox section |
| Solar lease / PACE lien | Not addressed | Required with payoff estimate |
| Radon testing results | Referenced by attachment | Integrated into form body |
| Delivery deadline | 10 calendar days | 7 calendar days after listing |

The compressed delivery deadline is worth emphasizing. Under the old statute, sellers had 10 calendar days from the listing agreement date to complete the form. That window shrinks to 7 calendar days under the amended Utah Code 57-1-37. Listing agents who fail to obtain timely completion bear shared liability for nondisclosure claims.

## Wire Fraud Liability: Senate Bill 142

Utah is one of a growing number of states placing statutory duties on agents — not just title companies — regarding wire fraud prevention. Senate Bill 142 amends Utah Code 61-2f-401 to require that every purchase agreement include a wire fraud advisory, and that agents verify any wire instructions transmitted through their offices against a known-good source before forwarding them to clients.

The bill does not create a private right of action. Instead, it gives the Utah Real Estate Commission explicit authority to discipline agents who fail to follow the verification protocol, including fines under the amended penalty schedule in Utah Code 61-2f-504. Agents who relay fraudulent wire instructions without independent verification can face civil penalties of up to $5,000 per incident, plus license suspension.

This mirrors [the wire verification process structures](/blog/wire-instructions-verification-process) we have outlined previously. The practical takeaway: never forward wire instructions received via email without calling the title or escrow company at a phone number you already have on file.

## What Happens If You Fail to Comply

The penalty framework under Utah's 2026 amendments is tiered. Understanding the tiers matters because agents often assume that a missed disclosure simply means an upset client. It can mean far more.

| Violation | First Offense | Repeat Offense | Additional Risk |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|
| No buyer-broker agreement | $1,000 fine + education order | $3,000 fine + 30-day suspension | Commission forfeiture |
| Use of retired Form 25.0 | Written warning (before Dec 2026) | $2,500 fine | Buyer rescission right |
| Missed disclosure delivery deadline | $1,500 fine | $5,000 fine + suspension | Seller civil liability |
| Wire instruction forwarding without verification | $5,000 fine + suspension | License revocation hearing | Joint liability for buyer losses |
| Failure to include wire fraud advisory in contract | $1,000 fine | $2,500 fine | Deal voidability |

Beyond administrative penalties, agents face civil exposure. Utah courts have historically applied the economic loss rule to limit agent liability, but the 2026 amendments create a statutory duty of care that plaintiffs' attorneys can point to as a per se standard. A missed disclosure deadline is no longer just sloppy — it is a statutory violation that triggers a presumption of negligence.

## Common Mistakes Agents Will Make Under the New Laws

**Every new regulatory cycle produces a predictable wave of errors.** Based on the Division's own enforcement data from previous rule changes, here are the mistakes most likely to generate disciplinary actions in late 2026 and 2027.

First, agents will use unsigned or incomplete buyer-broker agreements. The 120-day term limit catches people off guard. An agreement without a specific end date is invalid under the statute, and the agent cannot later enforce a commission claim based on it.

Second, listing agents will forget to update their listing packet templates. If your office still has Form 25.0 loaded as the default seller disclosure in your document management system, you are one autopilot transaction away from a violation. The form number matters. "Close enough" language on a custom form will not satisfy the Division's requirement that the UAR-approved Form 25.1 be used.

Third, agents will treat the wire fraud advisory as optional boilerplate. Senate Bill 142 is explicit: the advisory must appear in the purchase agreement, not as a separate handout. Agents who staple a one-page flyer to the contract instead of integrating it into the agreement itself are not in compliance.

Fourth, buyer agents will fail to deliver a copy of the signed buyer-broker agreement to the cooperating listing agent upon making an offer. This new requirement under the amended Rule R162-2f-401a catches agents who view the buyer agreement as a private document. It is not. It must accompany the first offer to establish the buyer agent's legal standing in the transaction.

Fifth, agents will miss the compressed 7-day seller disclosure window because they assumed the old 10-day timeline still applied. Calendar confusion during the transition period is guaranteed.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Principal brokers carry supervisory liability under Utah Code 61-2f-401(10). The 2026 amendments do not change that baseline, but they expand the surface area of what a broker must verify. Here is what every managing broker should be auditing starting now.

**Transaction files must contain a fully executed buyer-broker agreement dated on or before the first substantive service.** The Division has signaled that "substantive service" includes property tours, CMA delivery, and offer preparation. Brokers should timestamp first-contact events and cross-reference them against agreement dates during file review.

Brokers must also verify that every listing file contains Form 25.1 — not 25.0 — completed and delivered within the 7-day statutory window. A simple checklist item confirming the form version number prevents an entire category of violations. This is exactly the kind of deadline-driven audit that [structured systems prevent agents from missing](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines).

Wire fraud compliance requires brokers to implement a written office policy describing the verification procedure. The Division's administrative rule R162-2f-401b(3) states that brokers must maintain a "written protocol accessible to all affiliated licensees" outlining how wire instructions are confirmed. Brokers who lack this written policy expose their entire office to collective liability.

Finally, brokers should conduct a quarterly review of all active buyer agreements to ensure none have exceeded the 120-day cap without renewal. An expired agreement that an agent continues to operate under creates a representation vacuum — the buyer has no agent, the agent has no authority, and the commission has no contractual basis.

## How Utah's Changes Compare to the National Trend

Utah is not acting in isolation. The buyer-broker agreement mandate follows the [NAR settlement framework](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) that took effect nationally in August 2024, but Utah's version is stricter in two ways: it sets a hard 120-day cap (the NAR practice changes do not specify a maximum term), and it requires delivery to the listing side upon offer submission.

The seller disclosure expansion reflects a nationwide pattern of states adding climate and encumbrance categories to their forms. The wire fraud provisions align with similar 2025-2026 legislation in Colorado, Arizona, and Nevada. Utah's version is notable for placing liability squarely on the agent rather than treating it as exclusively a title company issue.
