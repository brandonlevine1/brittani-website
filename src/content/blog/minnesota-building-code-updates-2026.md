## Minnesota Building Code Updates 2026: The Changes That Hit Your Transaction Files

The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (DLI) formally adopted revisions to the Minnesota State Building Code effective January 1, 2026, and the ripple effects are already landing on listing agreements and purchase contracts across the state. If you handle residential transactions, these Minnesota building code updates 2026 changes affect how you advise clients on inspections, disclosures, and repair negotiations. Ignoring them is not a neutral position — it is a liability position.

The 2026 cycle incorporates portions of the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) and International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), along with Minnesota-specific amendments under Minnesota Rules Chapter 1300 through 1365. These are not cosmetic tweaks. The energy efficiency mandates, updated fire separation requirements, and revised egress standards will surface in inspection reports and appraisal footnotes throughout the year.

**Real estate agents do not enforce building codes, but they absolutely bear responsibility when code-related defects become disclosure issues.** Minnesota Statute 82.81 establishes the duties of licensees, and Statute 513.55 governs seller disclosure. Both statutes create pathways for buyers to pursue agents who knew or should have known about material defects tied to code compliance.

## What Changed in the 2026 Minnesota State Building Code

The DLI published its final rule amendments in November 2025 after a public comment period and review by the Construction Codes Advisory Council (CCAC). The major residential changes fall into three categories: energy performance, fire and life safety, and structural provisions.

Energy performance requirements now demand higher insulation R-values in Climate Zones 6 and 7 (which cover most of Minnesota). New homes must meet a blower door test result of no more than 3.0 ACH50, down from 4.0 in the prior cycle. Existing homes are not retroactively required to comply, but any renovation exceeding 50 percent of the home's assessed value triggers compliance with the current energy code under Minnesota Rules 1322.

Fire separation between attached garages and living spaces now requires 5/8-inch Type X gypsum on both sides of the common wall — a change from the prior one-side requirement. This matters because inspection reports on homes built between 2012 and 2025 will frequently flag single-sided installations that were code-compliant at the time of construction but may concern buyers unfamiliar with grandfathering provisions.

Egress window well dimensions in below-grade bedrooms were updated to align with the 2021 IRC minimum of 9 square feet of clear opening area. Older homes with 5.7-square-foot window wells (legal under prior codes) will not violate current code unless a new bedroom is added, but buyers may still use this as a negotiation lever during the inspection period.

| Code Area | Prior Standard (Pre-2026) | 2026 Requirement | Triggered By |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air tightness (new construction) | 4.0 ACH50 | 3.0 ACH50 | New build or gut rehab |
| Garage fire separation | 1/2\" Type X one side | 5/8\" Type X both sides | New build or garage renovation |
| Egress window well area | 5.7 sq ft minimum | 9.0 sq ft minimum | New bedroom addition |
| Insulation R-value (attic, Zone 7) | R-49 | R-60 | New construction or re-roof with structural access |
| Radon mitigation system | Passive rough-in | Active system required | New construction (select counties) |

## How Code Updates Affect Seller Disclosures

Minnesota's Seller Disclosure Statement — formally referenced as the form required under Minnesota Statute 513.55 — asks sellers to identify known defects related to the structural, mechanical, and environmental condition of the property. The statute does not specifically mention "building code compliance" as a line item, but it broadly requires disclosure of conditions that materially affect the value or desirability of the property.

When a seller has obtained permits for past work and that work was inspected under the old code, there is no obligation to retrofit. However, if a seller performed unpermitted work that does not meet the code that was in effect at the time of construction, that is a different story entirely. Unpermitted work is a known defect if the seller is aware of it, and failure to disclose creates exposure under both Statute 513.55 and common law fraud.

**Agents who list properties with visible additions, finished basements, or converted garages should ask pointed questions about permits during the listing intake process.** The answers — or lack thereof — inform what goes on the disclosure form and what language you include in the MLS remarks.

## Common Mistakes Agents Make With Building Code Issues

Mistake number one: conflating "grandfathered" with "no disclosure needed." A home with 5.7-square-foot egress wells in a below-grade bedroom is grandfathered, yes, but if the listing agent knows the bedroom does not meet current code and a buyer's agent represents it as a conforming bedroom, that is a misrepresentation. MLS data entry is where this most frequently goes wrong — listing a below-grade room as a "bedroom" when it lacks conforming egress creates downstream liability regardless of grandfathering.

Mistake number two: failing to distinguish between municipal amendments and the state baseline code. Cities like Minneapolis, St. Paul, Rochester, and Duluth adopt local amendments that exceed the state code. An agent working a deal in Minneapolis must know that the city's rental licensing code imposes requirements beyond what DLI mandates. Telling a buyer "it meets state code" when municipal code is more restrictive is a factual error that can unwind a deal post-closing.

Mistake number three: ignoring the interaction between the [inspection contingency timeline](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) and code-related repair estimates. Under the standard Minnesota purchase agreement (commonly referenced as MN:PA-1 or the Minnesota REALTORS form), the inspection contingency period defaults to a set number of days. When code-triggered repairs involve permit applications and municipal review, the timeline often proves too short. Agents who do not proactively extend the contingency window — or at minimum advise their client about the risk — set themselves up for blown deadlines and lost earnest money.

Mistake number four: not advising buyers that code compliance costs money on older homes. A buyer who purchases a 1985 rambler and wants to finish the basement will need to meet 2026 code for the new work. That means current egress standards, current insulation values, and current radon mitigation requirements. The purchase price should reflect this, but too often agents let buyers discover these costs after closing rather than [during the diligence period](/blog/where-deals-break-down).

Mistake number five: relying on the home inspector to "catch everything" without independently flagging known code issues the agent personally observed. Minnesota Statute 82.81(3) imposes a duty on agents to disclose material facts they are aware of. Waiting for an inspector to find an issue the agent already noticed is not a defense — it is evidence of concealment.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers carry supervisory liability under Minnesota Statute 82.81 and Minnesota Rules 2805. When an agent in your office misstates a bedroom count, omits a known code deficiency from disclosures, or fails to advise a client about permit history, the broker is exposed alongside the agent.

**Start with a file audit of disclosure forms completed in the past six months.** Look specifically at below-grade bedroom counts, remarks about additions or renovations, and whether permit verification was discussed with the seller. If your agents are listing homes with post-2010 additions and no permit history on file, that is a compliance gap waiting to become a complaint at the Minnesota Department of Commerce.

Brokers should also verify that agents are using current versions of purchase agreement forms. The Minnesota Association of REALTORS periodically updates form language to reflect statutory and code changes. Running transactions on outdated forms — even by one revision cycle — introduces ambiguity into contingency language and repair obligations that can cost your brokerage money in arbitration.

| Broker Audit Item | What to Check | Risk if Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Bedroom count accuracy | Egress compliance for below-grade rooms | Misrepresentation claim |
| Permit history documentation | Seller interview notes in file | Failure to disclose |
| Form version currency | MN:PA-1 and disclosure form revision dates | Ambiguous contract language |
| Municipal code awareness | City-specific amendments noted in transaction notes | Material omission |
| Inspection contingency duration | Days vs. complexity of property | Missed deadline, lost earnest money |

Training sessions should address the distinction between "code-compliant at time of construction" and "meets current code." Your agents need to articulate this difference clearly to buyers without practicing law or engineering. A simple sentence in showing feedback or buyer consultation notes — "This home was built under 2006 code; current code differs in the following areas" — demonstrates competence and creates a paper trail.

> Minnesota Statute 82.81, Subd. 3: "A licensee shall disclose to any party to a transaction any material facts of which the licensee is aware that could adversely and significantly affect the party's use and enjoyment of the property."

## Practical Steps for Agents Listing or Selling in 2026

For listing agents, the pre-listing consultation should now include a direct question about all work performed on the property since purchase, whether permits were pulled, and whether final inspections were completed. Document the answers in writing. If the seller cannot confirm permit status, recommend they contact the local building department for a permit search — most municipalities in Minnesota offer this online or for a nominal fee.

For buyer's agents, every property showing that includes a finished basement, converted porch, or detached ADU should trigger a mental flag. Before your buyer falls in love with the space, verify that the work was permitted and inspected. This is especially relevant under the 2026 updates because [relying on memory rather than a documented checklist](/blog/why-checklists-beat-memory) is how agents miss these issues repeatedly.

When negotiating repairs after inspection, reference the specific code provision at issue rather than generic language like "not up to code." A repair request citing "Minnesota Rules 1309.0310 egress window compliance" carries more weight with sellers and their counsel than vague references. It also demonstrates that you understand the distinction between a preference and a code deficiency.
