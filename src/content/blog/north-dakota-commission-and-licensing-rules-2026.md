## Why North Dakota Commission & Licensing Rules 2026 Demand Your Attention Now

The North Dakota Real Estate Commission (NDREC) has not slowed down on enforcement. If you hold an active license in the state, understanding North Dakota commission & licensing rules 2026 is not optional — it is the baseline for keeping your business alive. Regulatory changes adopted in the 2025 legislative session and refined through NDREC administrative rulemaking now carry real teeth heading into the new licensing cycle.

North Dakota's regulatory framework sits inside Chapter 43-23.3 of the North Dakota Century Code (NDCC) and its companion administrative rules under Title 70 of the North Dakota Administrative Code. These two bodies of law govern everything from how you earn a commission to what disclosures you owe your clients before the first showing. Agents who treat this knowledge as background noise tend to be the ones writing checks to the NDREC's disciplinary fund.

This post breaks down the statutes, form requirements, penalties, common mistakes, and broker-level audit responsibilities that matter most for 2026. Every section references specific code provisions so you can verify the details yourself.

## Licensing Structure and Renewal Requirements

North Dakota issues two tiers of real estate licenses: salesperson and broker. A salesperson must work under a licensed broker, while a broker can operate independently or sponsor other licensees. The NDREC administers both license types, and the application process runs through its online portal along with supporting documentation submitted on NDREC Form 1 (Salesperson Application) or NDREC Form 2 (Broker Application).

**Renewal happens on a two-year cycle, and the NDREC does not send grace-period reminders.** Each licensee must complete 15 hours of approved continuing education (CE) during the renewal period, which includes a mandatory 3-hour core course prescribed by the Commission. The remaining 12 hours are elective but must come from NDREC-approved providers listed on the [NDREC's official website](https://www.ndrec.nd.gov/).

Failing to renew on time does not just pause your license — it voids your ability to collect compensation on any transaction that closes while you are in lapsed status. NDCC 43-23.3-02 makes clear that performing any act of real estate brokerage without an active license is a Class B misdemeanor. That is not a slap on the wrist; it is a criminal charge.

## Commission Rules Under NDCC 43-23.3

Commission structures in North Dakota remain negotiable between parties, but the law imposes strict guardrails on who can receive compensation and how that compensation must be documented. NDCC 43-23.3-18 prohibits a licensee from paying any portion of a commission to an unlicensed individual. The only exception is a licensed agent in another jurisdiction who holds a valid cooperative agreement or who is licensed under a reciprocal compact.

All commissions must flow through the licensee's supervising broker. A salesperson cannot receive payment directly from a client, a title company, or another brokerage — the funds must pass through the broker's trust account or operating account first. This rule catches more agents off guard than any other, especially those who work on teams where informal payment splits are common.

**NDREC Form 6 (Disclosure of Agency and Compensation) must be provided to all parties before substantive real estate services begin.** This form outlines who the agent represents, how the agent will be compensated, and whether compensation is shared with a cooperating broker. Skipping this form, or filling it out after the fact, is one of the most frequently cited violations in NDREC disciplinary orders.

## What Happens When Agents Fail to Comply

The NDREC has broad disciplinary authority under NDCC 43-23.3-20. Penalties range from a formal reprimand to license revocation, and the Commission can impose fines of up to $2,000 per violation. In cases involving fraud or misrepresentation, the Commission refers matters to the North Dakota Attorney General for criminal prosecution.

Deal cancellation is a real risk. If a buyer or seller discovers that their agent was unlicensed at the time of the transaction, they may have grounds to void the contract entirely under NDCC 43-23.3-22. Courts have also allowed recovery of commissions already paid if the agent lacked a valid license when the services were rendered. The financial exposure goes beyond fines — it extends to disgorgement of earned fees and potential civil liability for damages.

Brokers face their own jeopardy. NDCC 43-23.3-12 holds the supervising broker responsible for the acts of their salespersons performed within the scope of the brokerage relationship. A single agent's compliance failure can trigger an audit of the entire brokerage, and the NDREC has exercised that authority more aggressively in recent years. ## Common Mistakes Agents Make in North Dakota

**First, agents routinely fail to deliver NDREC Form 6 before providing substantive services.** The statute says "before" — not "at the time of offer" and not "when we get around to it." Substantive services include showing property, preparing a market analysis, or advising a seller on pricing. If your client has already toured three homes before you hand them a disclosure form, you are already out of compliance.

Second, agents split commissions informally with unlicensed assistants or transaction coordinators. North Dakota law is explicit: only licensed individuals may receive compensation tied to the performance of real estate brokerage activities. Paying a flat fee to an unlicensed TC for administrative tasks is generally permissible, but tying that fee to a percentage of commission or to the closing of a deal crosses the line. The distinction matters, and the NDREC has disciplined agents who got it wrong.

Third, agents let their continuing education lapse and then continue to practice. The two-year CE cycle catches people who procrastinate. The NDREC tracks completion through its approved provider network, and if your hours are not logged by the renewal deadline, your license goes inactive automatically. There is no 30-day buffer. Agents who close a transaction during that gap face both disciplinary action and potential loss of their commission — a scenario that [explains why agents miss deadlines](/blog/why-agents-miss-deadlines) more often than they admit.

Fourth, agents fail to update their license record when they change brokerages. NDCC 43-23.3-09 requires that a transfer of association be filed with the NDREC promptly. Practicing under a brokerage that is not reflected on your license record creates a gap in supervision that the Commission treats seriously.

Fifth, agents operating near the Minnesota or Montana borders occasionally perform brokerage acts in North Dakota without holding a North Dakota license. NDCC 43-23.3-03 allows limited reciprocity, but it requires a formal cooperative brokerage agreement and compliance with North Dakota disclosure requirements. Simply holding a license in a neighboring state does not authorize you to practice in North Dakota without additional steps.

## What Brokers Need to Audit and Enforce

Brokers are the compliance backbone of every North Dakota brokerage, and the NDREC expects them to act like it. NDCC 43-23.3-12 places affirmative duties on brokers to supervise their agents, maintain proper records, and ensure that trust accounts are managed according to NDREC rules under NDAC 70-02-03.

**At minimum, brokers should audit four areas quarterly.** First, verify that every salesperson's license is active and properly associated with the brokerage in NDREC's system. Second, confirm that NDREC Form 6 is present in every transaction file before any substantive service was performed. Third, review trust account reconciliations to ensure that earnest money deposits are handled within the timelines specified under NDAC 70-02-03-01 — generally within three business days of receipt. Fourth, confirm that no compensation has been paid to unlicensed persons.

| CE tracking | Agent CE hours current before renewal deadline | Biannually | NDCC 43-23.3-06 |

Transaction files get misfiled, CE deadlines slip through, and Form 6 compliance becomes inconsistent across agents. ## North Dakota Commission & Licensing Rules 2026: Staying Ahead of Enforcement

The NDREC published updated guidance in early 2026 that signals a continued focus on disclosure violations and trust account irregularities. Board meeting minutes from March 2026 reference an increase in consumer complaints related to compensation transparency, which aligns with national trends following the NAR settlement framework. North Dakota agents should expect more scrutiny, not less.

One area to watch is the evolving treatment of buyer-broker compensation agreements. While North Dakota has not adopted a state-level mandate identical to other states, the NDREC's interpretive guidance increasingly emphasizes that all compensation arrangements — including those between cooperating brokers — must be documented in writing and disclosed to the consumer before services begin. Agents operating under verbal agreements or informal office policies are exposed.

Record retention matters too. NDAC 70-02-01-08 requires brokers to maintain transaction records for a minimum of six years. That includes signed copies of NDREC Form 6, purchase agreements, addenda, and any written communication related to compensation. The six-year window means that a transaction you close today could be reviewed by the NDREC as late as 2032.
